Decision Assessment Document
 Ingram v. Nicholson, May 23, 2007, 21 Vet.App. 232
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court, in addressing the effect of the Federal Circuit’s *Deshotel* decision on the pending claims doctrine, held that a reasonably raised claim remains pending until there is either a recognition of the substance of the claim in an RO decision from which a claimant could sufficiently deduce that the claim was adjudicated or an explicit adjudication of a subsequent “claim” for the same disability. Note: [Deshotel v. Nicholson, 457 F.3d 1258 (Fed. Cir. 2006)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014899/) reh’g and reh’g en banc denied (Dec. 5, 2006).

If there is no explicit denial of the original claim prior to the granting of the subsequent claim, then, as part of an appeal of an effective-date decision, an appellant can raise the fact that he or she filed the original claim for the same disability at an earlier date than the claim which was subsequently granted.  Failure to adjudicate a reasonably raised claim can only be the basis of a CUE motion as to a final decision where the issue was relevant to a decision actually made.  *See* [*Moody v. Principi*, 360 F.3d 1306 (Fed. Cir. 2004)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000015386/).  (Note: In *Moody*, a decision concerning effective-date had become final and the Federal Circuit held that the appellant properly alleged CUE in the effective-date decision on the theory that it failed to correctly identify which document was the appellant’s claim for purposes of assigning an effective date under [38 U.S.C. § 5110](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5110).)

The Court held that *Deshotel* did not overrule the pending claim doctrine articulated in *Norris v. West*, 12 Vet.App. 413 (1999) [see attached], nor created a general doctrine of *sub silentio* denials that supplants the pending claim doctrine as the Federal Circuit must have intended a more limited application of *Deshotel*.  The Court concluded that *Deshotel* and Andrews stand for the proposition that where an RO decision discusses a claim in terms sufficient to put the claimant on notice that it was being considered and rejected, then it constitutes a denial of that claim even if the formal adjudicative language does not “specifically” deny that claim.  See [*Andrews v. Nicholson*, 421 F.3d 1278 (Fed. Cir. 2005)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000015186/).

Regarding the duty to sympathetically read an appellant’s pleadings, the Court held that it cannot be based on a standard that requires legal sophistication beyond that which can be expected of a lay claimant and must consider whether the appellant’s submissions, considered in toto, have articulated a claim.  Although there is no statutory or regulatory definition of “sympathetic reading,” the Court stated that the doctrine clearly includes a duty to apply some level of expertise in reading documents to recognize the existence of possible claims that an unsophisticated pro se claimant would not be expected to be able to articulate clearly.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting Manual revision or regulatory change.  However, this holding provides further guidance in adjudicating pending issue claims.  The Court provided detailed analyses of numerous cases that have affected evolution of the pending claim doctrine.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who rendered honorable service from 1964 to 1968, underwent a right bronchoscopy and pneumonectomy at the Salt Lake City VAMC in April 1985.  He filed a formal application for benefits in May 1986.  In the section of the VA Form 21-526 entitled “Nature of sickness, disease or injuries for which this claim is made and date each began,” the veteran wrote:  “Feb. – 1985 – Right lung was removed, Salt Lake City [VAMC].”  He also completed sections of the application form entitled “if you claim to be totally disabled” and for “non-service-connected pension.”  In August 1986, the veteran submitted the following statement:  “The removal of a vital organ (right lung) has greatly decreased my capacity for air-lung capacity – by fifty percent.  Also, I am not capable under this condition to continue my normal life style.”  He also stated that he could not continue his carpenter occupation due to the disability.  That same month the RO denied the veteran’s claim for pension benefits.

A formal application for benefits was again filed on April 15, 1992.  He asserted in this application form and in later correspondence that he suffers with various disabilities due to the 1985 surgery and VA negligence.  After initially denied by the RO, the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) granted compensation benefits under [38 U.S.C. § 1151](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1151) for residuals of a pneumonectomy.  An effective date of April 15, 1992, was eventually assigned.

The Board, in its December 2003 decision, determined that neither the May 1986 application for benefits nor the August 1986 statement could have been construed a claim for compensation benefits under section 1151 as there was no allegation of negligence or similar fault in these documents.  On appeal to the Court the veteran argued that the 1986 filings constituted informal claims for compensation benefits under section 1151, which would have entitled him to an earlier effective date under *Norris*.

The Court had previously issued a decision on this case on July 12, 2006; however, the Court withdrew that decision and granted the Secretary’s motion for reconsideration in light of *Deshotel*.

The Secretary urged the Court to apply *Deshotel* and dismiss the appeal because the veteran had not challenged as CUE the 1986 RO decision “based on any failure to adjudicate” an asserted section 1151 claim.  The section 1151 claim is to be considered as having been deemed denied and the only remedy would have been to have timely filed a Notice of Disagreement (NOD) or the filing of a CUE motion.  The *Andrews* and *Deshotel* holdings were espoused as not limited by the type of claim involved, but supplanting the “pending claim” doctrine.  Thus, under the Secretary’s theory, if multiple claims are filed and the RO adjudicates one of the claims identified in the application, the RO has adjudicated the entire application, including all remaining claims made in the application even if those claims were not addressed, generally or specifically, in the RO decision.

The appellant and amicus curiae submitted on his behalf argued that *Deshotel* was not applicable in this case, because [38 C.F.R. § 3.103(e)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=57b86f88d7346a45c2f5f73ef4c1c3e0&node=se38.1.3_1103&rgn=div8) required an express determination, including a denial, of each and every claim.  The appellant contended that the RO, in 1986, gave no reason for the decision to deny a section 1151 claim; thus, it is remained pending.  The amicus briefs advanced that the pending claim doctrine has a long history within VA, *sub silentio* denials are contrary to VA practice regarding notice and to the nature of an *ex parte*, nonadversarial system, and *Deshotel* is an extension of *Andrews* that did not overrule any of its precedents and should be limited to its specific facts.

The Court, at their outset of its analysis, indicated that it must determine whether the 1986 RO decision decided a final section 1151 claim, which would have to be subject to a collateral attack (CUE), or whether the alleged section 1151 claim was still pending at the time of the April 1992 correspondence and is part of the present claim stream.  The Court stated that it needed to resolve the following question:

           “Assuming a claim has been reasonably raised by a claimant, is

           that claim denied *sub silentio* by an RO decision that should

           have adjudicated it, or does that claim remain pending under

           § 3.160(c) and *Myers v. Principi,* 16 Vet.App. 228 (2002) until

           it is explicitly adjudicated?”

The Court provided an extensive discussion of several precedent cases that affecting the doctrine concerning pending claims and CUE motions in analyzing the parties’ conflicting theories.

In *Deshotel*, the veteran, in 1984, filed to reopen a previously denied claim for service connection for residuals of a head injury.  The RO conducted examinations, to include a psychiatric examination.  Service connection for post traumatic headaches was granted in 1985.  Although the RO decision did not specifically address any claim for a psychiatric disability, the narrative stated that a VA examination had shown no psychiatric symptoms.  The veteran sought service connection for a psychiatric condition in 1999, which was treated as a claim to reopen based on new and material evidence.  Service connection as awarded with a 1999 effective date.  The veteran sought an effective date back to the date of original claim in 1984.  The RO, Board, and Court denied the earlier effective date claim.

The Federal Circuit, in affirming the previous denials and giving its imprimatur to *sub silentio* denials, held:  “Where the veteran files more than one claim with the RO at the same time, and the RO’s decision acts (favorable or unfavorably) on one of the claims but fails to specifically address the other claim, the second claim is deemed denied and the appeal period begins to run.”

The Court, in rejecting the argument that *Deshotel* supplanted the pending claim doctrine, contrasted *Deshotel* with the facts of *Ingram* and noted multiple problems with accepting a broad application of the Federal Circuit’s holding.

In *Deshotel*, since the 1985 RO adjudication stated that there was no current psychiatric symptomatology, the Court concluded that it would be reasonable to expect a claimant to deduce that the psychiatric disability claim had been denied.  The veteran had been reasonably put on notice that the psychiatric disability claim had been denied.  However, in *Ingram*, the veteran asserted he filed a [section 1151](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1151) claim, which is clearly unrelated to the NSC pension claim which the RO adjudicated.  The Court indicated that if the RO’s original decision had found no lung disability, the veteran might have been given sufficient notice that he was not entitled to section 1151 benefits.  But, when the veteran was informed of the denial of pension benefits because the condition was not established as permanent, he had no reason to comprehend how a section 1151 claim might have been decided by the RO.  The Court indicated that this stands in contrast to *Deshotel* where, although not superficially addressed, the RO provided enough information in its decision for the veteran to reasonably know that he would not be awarded compensation for the psychiatric disability.

The Court stated that adoption of a broad, general doctrine of *sub silentio* denials would run afoul of due process concerns of notice and opportunity to be heard in VA’s nonadversarial claims system.  It would relieve VA of such obligations as the duties to notify, assist, and provide reasons for decisions under [sections 5103](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103) and [5104](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5104).  The Court surmised that it would cause confusion at the ROs by creating uncertainty as to whether a claim had been denied, resulting in premature appeals, and would shift the burden onto a claimant to prove the high standard of CUE that not only did the RO err in not providing reasons for the “deemed denial” but that the claimant was entitled to a grant of the benefits.

A broad reading of *Deshotel* was found to conflict with the legislative history and congressional intent that a claimant not only be informed of a denial of his claim but also be provided reasons for any such denial.  Furthermore, a broad reading would diverge with such statutory provisions as sections 5108 and 5109.  How would a claimant know what evidence is needed to reopen a denial in terms of new and material evidence without VA having provided reasons for the denial? Similarly, how could a claimant allege CUE if not provided with some basis for denial in a prior decision?

The Court concluded that a broadly interpreted doctrine of *sub silentio* denials has grave implications for due process and protecting the appellate rights of veterans.

The Court indicated that aside from the support found in prior caselaw, statutes, and regulation, the pending claim theory is clearly a preferable approach to administering the veterans benefits system.  Treating the failure to sympathetically read and adjudicate a reasonably raised claim as a pending claim benefits veterans because it protects their appellate rights and works no hardship on VA in that it requires only that each claim be specifically addressed.

In discussing VA’s duty to sympathetically consider pleadings, the Court deemed that the Board’s articulation of a “four corners” standard for reviewing pleadings to be overly formal in a system where the Secretary must take a sympathetic view of pleadings to determine whether a claim has been made.  Although the Court declined to craft specific standard, it noted that the duty to sympathetically read a pleading must be based on reasonable expectations of a *pro se* claimant and the Secretary, i.e., that the *pro se* claimant knows what symptoms he is experiencing that are causing disability and the Secretary knows the law and can evaluate whether there is potential under the law to compensate an averred disability based on a sympathetic reading of the material in a *pro se* submission.

The Board decision was vacated and remanded with instructions to consider whether the May 1986 application and appellant’s submissions in support of the application raise a claim for benefits under section 1151 based on a sympathetic reading of those documents that does not require conformance with legal pleading requirements or intent to seek benefits under section 1151 specifically.  If the Board concludes that a claim was raised, it must decide whether that claim was ever adjudicated or whether it was still pending at the time of the April 1992 correspondence from the veteran.  The Board must address whether the veteran is entitled to an effective date earlier than April 15, 1992, for the grant of section 1151 benefits.
