Decision Assessment Document
 Clemons v. Shinseki, Feb 17, 2009, 23 Vet.App. 1
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that a service connection claim that only identifies post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) cannot only be limited to that diagnosis, but must be considered a claim for any mental disability that may be reasonably raised by several factors, to include the veteran’s description of the claim and/or symptoms and any information and evidence submitted by the veteran or obtained by VA.

The Court explained that because a veteran was reasonably requesting benefits for symptoms of a mental condition that he was not competent to medically identify, the adjudication of the claim does not end in the face of currently diagnosed mental conditions that are different from his lay hypothesis on the claim form. The Court noted that it is generally within the province of medical professionals to diagnose or label a mental condition, not the claimant.

**Impact on VBA:**

A Manual change may be required to clarify the “Making a Decision” table at [M21-1MR III.iv.4.H.32.j](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014201/), which currently states “If… ‘a VA medical examination fails to establish a diagnosis of PTSD’… ‘Then…deny the claim on that basis’.” This instruction will need to be revised to ensure that any co-existing mental condition identified by the evidence be considered and a determination of service connection be made. The Office of General Counsel has noted that it is filing many Joint Motions for Remand, based on the holding in this case, for approval by the Court.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served in the Navy from 1951 to 1958 to include service aboard the USS ROCHESTER (CA-124) during which it completed numerous gun strikes upon enemy positions from off the Korean coast during 1952-53. His service medical records (SMR) document treatment for anxiety.

The veteran filed a claim for compensation benefits in May 2002 for “P.T.S.D. Korea War.” In response to a request for additional information, he described handling dead bodies and reported that he had flashbacks and nightmares twice a week about handling dead bodies. VA medical records showed anxiety and psychological testing determined a schizoid personality disorder, rather than PTSD.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in 2006, denied service connection for PTSD on the basis of no confirmed PTSD diagnosis and diagnostic testing showing a schizoid personality disorder. The Board concluded that a VA examination was unnecessary because the record already contained competent and probative evidence regarding a potential diagnosis, or lack thereof, concerning PTSD. The Board did not discuss the possibility of service connection for any other acquired psychiatric disorders.

The veteran appealed to the Court. Both the Secretary and the veteran’s attorney submitted a joint motion for remand (JMR) to the Court in which the parties agreed that the veteran had reasonably raised a claim for entitlement to service connection for an acquired psychiatric disorder in addition to the claim of service connection for PTSD, and that the Board failed to fully adjudicate the inextricably intertwined claims. The parties requested that the Court vacate the Board’s decision and remand the case.

The Court denied the motion and ordered oral argument to discuss whether it had jurisdiction to grant a JMR that appears to pertain in part to an anxiety disorder claim not decided by the Board and not properly before the Court. The Court deemed that it had jurisdiction and granted the JMR with clarification in an order.

The Court concluded that the veteran’s claim for benefits based on PTSD encompassed benefits based on an anxiety disorder and/or a schizoid disorder because the evidence developed during the processing of the claim indicated that the symptoms for which the veteran was seeking VA benefits may have been caused by an anxiety and/or schizoid disorder. The Court found that because the veteran submitted only one claim over which it had jurisdiction, it rejected the parties’ view that the veteran had multiple, separate claims that were inextricably intertwined.

The Court observed that multiple medical diagnoses that differ from the claimed condition do not necessarily represent wholly separate claims. Although a veteran who has no special medical expertise may testify as to the symptoms he can observe, he generally is not competent to provide a diagnosis that requires that application of medical expertise to the facts presented. As a self-represented lay person when the claim was filed, the veteran had neither the legal nor medical knowledge to narrow the universe of his claim or his current condition to PTSD.

The Court stated that the Board should have considered alternative conditions within the scope of the claim filed in view of the medical evidence showing other mental disorders and should not have limited its consideration of the claim based upon the veteran’s belief that he suffered from PTSD. The Court held that the Board did not err by not failing to adjudicate a separate claim for an anxiety disorder and/or schizoid disorder; rather it erred when it failed to weigh and assess the nature of the current condition the veteran suffered when determining the breadth of the claim before it.

The Court also noted that it matters little that the veteran believes his symptoms should be diagnosed as PTSD if the medical evidence establishes that his symptoms are actually something different. The fact that the veteran may be wrong about the nature of his condition does not relieve the VA of its duty to properly adjudicate the claim.

The Court distinguished the present case from [*Boggs v. Peake*, 520 F.3d 1330, 1335 (Fed. Cir. 2008)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014850/) in which the Federal Circuit held that “a properly diagnosed disease or injury cannot be considered on the same factual basis as a distinctly diagnosed disease or injury.” *Boggs* was viewed as inapplicable as it did not inform as how to determine the scope of a claim at the time the claim is filed. The Court characterized *Boggs* as relying upon a diagnosis to define the scope of a claim only retrospectively – *after* there has been a finding of fact based upon competent medical evidence.
