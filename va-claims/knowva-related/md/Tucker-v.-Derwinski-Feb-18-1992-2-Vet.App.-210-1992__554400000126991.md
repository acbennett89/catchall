Decision Assessment Document
Tucker v. Derwinski, Feb 18, 1992, 2  Vet.App. 210 (1992)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that a medical opinion by a Regional Office rating board is not independent medical evidence and cannot be relied on by a Board of Veterans' Appeals (Board) panel to support its opinion.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court's holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in the Manual M21-1 at III.iv.5.A.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran was rated 100 percent service-connected for bipolar disorder in December 1982.  This evaluation was first reduced to 70 percent in December 1986, then to 50 percent in April 1987, and finally to 30 percent in June 1989.  The record showed multiple hospitalizations for detoxification 1987-1989 which had resulted in confirmed ratings.  VA examination in April 1989 continued a diagnosis of bipolar disorder, which examiner felt involved moderate vocational impairment.  Incident to ensuing reduction, the Regional Office (RO) rating board indicated that the claims folder had not been reviewed by the examiner, and that the veteran's history had been unreliably reported by the veteran.  The rating board further opined that he had been treated primarily for alcohol dependence since 1986, "with no objective evidence of psychiatric impairment."

The Court indicated that the rating decision over which we have jurisdiction (50 percent rating was reduced to 30 percent), the rating board was faced with a psychiatric exam where "apparently the claims folder was not reviewed by the examiner."  The Court concluded that the rating board chose to substitute its own opinion for that of the doctor instead of insisting on correction of the deficiency or an adequate examination in accordance with 38 C.F.R. 4.1 and 4.2

The Court noted that it had previously held that Board panels "must consider only independent medical evidence to support their findings rather than provide their own medical judgment in the guise of a Board opinion."  Colvin v. Derwinski, 1 Vet.App. 171, 175 (1990).  "This procedure ensures that all medical evidence contrary to the veteran's claim will be made known to him and be a part of the record before this Court."  Id.  If the Board finds that the medical evidence in the record is not adequate, it must remand for further development.  38 C.F.R. 19.182 (1991).  Because a medical opinion by a Regional Office rating board or hearing officer is not "independent medical evidence" as described in Colvin and, therefore, cannot be relied on by a Board panel to support its opinion, much time and effort would be saved if the rating boards insisted on adequate examination results initially rather than leaving the Board panels to do so.

The Court held that the Board’s factual finding that the veteran's service-connected bipolar disorder produced no more than definite impairment is not plausible based on the medical evidence of record and, therefore, is "clearly erroneous."

Accordingly, the Court reversed the May 21, 1990, Board decision as to the rating reduction from 50 percent  to 30 percent and remanded the case with directions to restore the veteran’s 50 percent rating for service-connected bipolar disorder as of October 1, 1989.
