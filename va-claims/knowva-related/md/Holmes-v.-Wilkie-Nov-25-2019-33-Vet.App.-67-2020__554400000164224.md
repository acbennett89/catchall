Decision Assessment Document
Holmes v. Wilkie, Nov 25 ,2019, 33 Vet.App. 67 (2020)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that 38 C.F.R. 4.124a, Diagnostic Code (DC) 8100, because it rates migraine headaches, contemplates more than headache symptoms.  DC 8100 requires VA to consider all symptoms experienced as a result of migraine attacks, and then rate those symptoms based on the frequency, severity, and economic impact of the attacks.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated as guidance in the Manual M21-1 at III.iv.4.N.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The regional office (RO), in June 2010, awarded service connection for migraine headaches with a 50 percent evaluation.  Symptoms shown during an August 2015 VA examination included headache pain, nausea, and sensitivity to light.  That same month, the RO issued a decision continuing the 50 percent evaluation.  The veteran appealed the decision and submitted evidence showing symptoms associated with migraine attacks to include light-sensitivity, blurred vision, light-headedness, mood swings, dizziness, depression, and anxiety.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in March 2019, maintained the 50 percent evaluation for migraine headaches.  The Board explained that any symptoms related to the veteran’s headaches that are productive of economic inadaptability and/or that cause prostrating attacks are taken into consideration.  The Board referenced Dorland’s Illustrated Medical Dictionary’s definition of migraines in concluding that the rating criteria for migraines contemplate the various manifestations of such disability by focusing on the overall functional impairment, rather than a demonstration of particular symptoms.  Dorland’s defined migraine headaches as “familial symptom complex of periodic attacks preceded by prodromal sensory symptoms and commonly associated with irritability, nausea, vomiting, constipation, or diarrhea, and photophobia.”  The Board, finding that the veteran’s symptoms were contemplated by the relevant diagnostic criteria, concluded that the veteran’s 50 percent rating was adequate because it was assigned based on the severity, frequency, and duration of the symptoms reported by the veteran and the resulting impairment of earning capacity.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board clearly erred in finding that all his symptoms were contemplated by the rating criteria, as DC 8100 does not contemplate non-headache symptoms such as nausea, vertigo, mood swings, sleep impairment, isolation, or depression.  He also alleged that the Board abdicated its duty to maximize benefits by not considering separate ratings for those symptoms.

At the outset, the Court rejected the veteran’s argument that the Board erred by relying on a dictionary to conclude that the migraine rating contemplates more than headaches because the Board made its own impermissible medical determination.  The Court explained that neither the Board, not the Court, turns to a dictionary to determine whether something is a symptom of migraines, but to determine whether migraines include more than headaches.  The Court stated its goal was not to replace a medical professional, but to determine what VA meant when it said that migraines “with very frequent completely prostrating and prolonged attacks productive of severe economic inadaptability” should be rated as 50 percent disabling.

The Court observed that because the rating focus on “attacks” and the frequency, duration, severity, and economic impact of the attacks differentiate the levels of disability, and since migraines include more than just headaches, the rating criteria must not be merely concerned with one symptom, such as headaches.  VA must look at everything the veteran experiences as a result of migraine attacks and then consider the frequency, duration, severity, and economic impact of those symptoms.

The Court held that the bottom line is that the rating criteria for migraines contemplate all migraine symptoms; thus, DC 8100 contemplates more than just headache symptoms and requires VA to consider all symptoms the veteran experiences as a result of migraine attacks, and then rate those symptoms based on the frequency, duration, severity, and economic impact of the attacks.

The Court clarified that its reading of the regulation does not foreclose the possibility that migraines could cause or aggravate a separate disability that would then need to be compensated through other means, such as secondary service connection under 38 C.F.R. § 3.310(a).  For example, if a manifestation was noted to be constant, as opposed to just during migraine attacks, this may raise the possibility of a secondary disability.  Also, if the migraines went beyond economic inadaptability and led to inability to secure substantially gainful employment, a total rating under 38 C.F.R. § 4.16(b) is an option, or extra-schedular consideration under 38 C.F.R. § 3.321(b)(1) may be raised if the veteran presents an exceptional case with symptoms more severe, frequent, or long-lasting than that contemplated in the rating criteria.

In applying its holding to the facts of the case, the Court observed that the Board did not make any unfavorable determinations that the symptoms did not result from the veteran’s migraines; however, the Board also did not render a finding that the veteran has separate disabilities that are caused or aggravated by his migraines.

The Court indicated that the Board’s conclusion – that the rating criteria contemplate more than just headaches – was correct and largely controls the results of the case.  The veteran’s argument was focused on fighting the definition used by the Board, but he did not explain how he would prevail if the Board’s definition was correct.  Since the regulation considers more than just headaches – it considers all symptoms of a migraine – the veteran would have to provide that his symptoms are either more severe, frequent, or disabling; are longer lasting; or cause greater economic inadaptability than what the 50 percent rating in DC 8100 contemplates.  The Court found that the veteran had not done so.

The Court noted that the Board did not use the dictionary definition to establish an exclusive list of symptoms.  Instead, it used the definition to conclude that DC 8100 contemplates various manifestations of such disability by focusing on the overall functional impairment, rather than a demonstration of particular symptoms.  Thus, the Court concluded that the veteran’s failure to show error in the Board’s decision leads it to affirm the Board’s decision.

The Court also addressed and rejected the veteran’s argument that the Board failed to exhaust all schedular alternatives before proceeding to an extra-schedular analysis.  They concluded that if the Board was correct that if all of the veteran’s symptoms were adequately contemplated by the rating criteria, there was nothing to compensate on an extra-schedular basis or through alternative means.  Thus, if the veteran fails to show error in the Board’s conclusion that the schedular rating was adequate, then he cannot prove prejudicial error in the Board’s extra-schedular analysis.  The Court concluded that if the Board correctly found that DC 8100 compensates the symptoms alleged to stem from migraines, there is no more compensating for the Board to do.

The Court affirmed the Board’s decision.
