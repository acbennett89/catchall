Decision Assessment Document
Ortiz-Valles v. McDonald, May 20, 2016, 28 Vet.App. 65 (2016)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the plain meaning of 38 C.F.R. 4.16(a) does not limit consideration of marginal employment to only currently employed veterans when determining entitlement to a total disability rating based on individual unemployability (TDIU).  VA must explain why the evidence does not demonstrate that a veteran is incapable of more than marginal employment if the facts of the case reasonably raise the issue of whether the veteran’s ability to work might be limited to marginal employment.

The Court held that if the evidence or facts reflect that a veteran is capable only of marginal employment, then the veteran is incapable of securing or following a substantially gainful occupation and is therefore entitled to TDIU if the veteran’s service-connected disabilities are the cause of that incapability.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated into the guidance contained in Manual M21-1, IV.ii.2.F.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The Social Security Administration (SSA), in December 1998, awarded the veteran disability benefits because of severe depression, finding that he could no longer perform past work as a bank teller and had not engaged in substantially gainful activity since June 1997.

The veteran, in April 2009, filed for a TDIU evaluation.  At the time of filing, service connection was in effect for nephrolithiasis, lumbar myositis, vertigo, and left-side radiculopathy.  His combined disability was 70 percent.  The regional office (RO) denied the claim.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in May 2012, remanded the appeal to include obtaining examinations to determine whether the veteran’s service-connected disabilities, either individually or cumulatively, rendered him unable to obtain and maintain substantially gainful employment.  A VA spine examiner found increased back pain with prolonged sitting, standing, or walking.  The examiner opined that the veteran would be able to perform a sedentary type job with duty restrictions.  A different VA examiner indicated that the veteran’s vertigo would limit him for positions that are labor related with heavy machines or driving, but would not limit his ability to work in a semi-sedentary job or light duties like a receptionist or clerk.

The Board, in June 2014, denied the veteran’s appeal.  The Board found that the evidence did not show that the veteran was unable to secure or follow a substantially gainful occupation consistent with his work experience solely due to his service-connected disabilities.

The veteran argued before the Court that because entitlement to a TDIU evaluation is premised on whether a veteran is capable of securing or following a substantially gainful occupation, VA must consider whether he is capable of more than marginal employment.  He contended that a veteran being unemployed is an extraneous factor that should not preclude a finding that the veteran is unable to engage in more than marginal employment.

 The Secretary argued that since the definition of marginal employment references “earned annual income,” it means that a veteran must be employed, otherwise there would be no earned income.

 The Court rejected the Secretary’s argument, holding that the Secretary’s interpretation of § 4.16(a) can only be supported under the plain meaning rule if read in isolation.  However, the law precludes such a narrow focus, as regulations must be considered as a whole.  The Court found that 4.16(a) does not differentiate between employed and unemployed veterans in terms of the eligibility requirements for a TDIU evaluation.

 The Court was unpersuaded that the regulation’s reference to a veteran’s earned annual income as a guide for determining whether a veteran is engaged in marginal employment restricts VA’s duty to consider the issue when a veteran is unemployed.  The Court concluded that VA simply cannot add restrictions to a regulation where they do not exist and that it was clear that the language of 4.16(a) focuses on a veteran’s capabilities and not his current employment status.

 In applying its holding to the facts of the case, the Court found that the Board did not expressly consider whether any employment the veteran could secure would be “substantially gainful.”  The Court held that the Board’s failure to consider whether the veteran is capable of more than marginal employment rendered its reasons and bases inadequate in light of the 2012 VA examinations showing restriction to working in a “semi-sedentary or light [duty position] like receptionist clerk” or he could only participate in a “sedentary type job, where he could perform different activities from a sitting position, at least in a part-time job” due to his service-connected disabilities.

 The Court directed that the case be remanded for the Board to reconsider the evidence and expressly state whether the veteran would be able to obtain or maintain a substantially gainful occupation to include being capable of more than marginal employment.

 The veteran also requested that the Court define the term “substantially gainful occupation.”  However, the Court stated it would decline to define the term without first allowing VA to take a position on the matter.  It noted that it was VA’s responsibility to define the terms contained within its regulations, and encouraged it to undertake such action.
