Decision Assessment Document
 Allen v. Principi, Feb 2, 2001, 237 F.3d 1368
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

Compensation for disabilities due to alcohol and drug abuse.

**Impact on VBA:**

The impact is significant, since this decision overrules the holding in VAOPGCPREC 7-99, which stated that compensation could not be paid for a substance abuse disability which is service connected on either a direct or secondary basis.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran is service-connected for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), currently evaluated as 50% disabling.  In connection with a claim for increase, a VA examination was conducted in 1996 to determine the severity of the veteran’s PTSD and to ascertain whether there was a relationship between the veteran’s PTSD and his alcohol abuse.  The examiner wrote that the reason for the veteran’s “alcohol admissions had to do with his rapidly accelerating symptoms of [PTSD]”.  The doctor diagnosed the veteran with severe PTSD along with chronic alcohol abuse and dependence, in remission, as secondary to PTSD.   The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (BVA) declined to increase the veteran’s evaluation finding that “alcohol abuse may not be service connected, on either a direct or secondary basis, and impairment for non service connected substance abuse may not be considered when evaluating the severity of the service connected PTSD”.  The BVA found that, in order to receive a 70 percent rating, the veteran would be required to demonstrate both occupational and social impairment.  The BVA found insufficient evidence of occupational and social impairment to increase the veteran’s evaluation.

The Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (CAVC) found that the BVA erred in its application of Diagnostic Code 9411, by requiring the veteran to exhibit both occupational and social impairment.  The CAVC also determined that the holding in Barela v. West, 11 Vet. App. 280 (1998), applied to this case.  Barela held that 38 USC § 1110 precludes a veteran from receiving compensation – including an increase in disability compensation -- for disabilities due to alcohol or drug abuse, but does not bar an award of service-connection for alcohol or drug abuse.  The veteran appealed to the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (Federal Circuit).

The veteran sought disability compensation under two alternative theories.  First, he claimed that his alcohol abuse disability is secondary to his PTSD.  The second theory is that, even if his alcohol-abuse disability is not service connected, he should be able to obtain an increase in his rating evaluation based upon evidence of his alcohol abuse and its effect upon his PTSD.

The Federal Circuit found that section 1110, when read in light of its legislative history, does not preclude a veteran from receiving compensation for an alcohol or drug abuse disability acquired as secondary to, or as a symptom of, a veteran’s service connected disability, and overruled the CAVC’s interpretation of section 1110.  According to the Federal Circuit, section 1110 precludes compensation only in two situations:  1) for primary alcohol abuse disabilities (an alcohol abuse disability arising during service from voluntary and willful drinking to excess); and 2) for secondary disabilities (such as cirrhosis of the liver) that result from primary alcohol abuse.

The Court noted that for purposes of determining whether a veteran is entitled to compensation, § 1110, in its first line, refers to “disability resulting from personal injury suffered or disease contracted in line of duty”.  The last line of § 1110 contains an express exclusion from recovery: “no compensation shall be paid if the disability is a result of the veteran’s own willful misconduct or abuse of alcohol or drugs”.  Thus, compensation is authorized if the disability is caused by an “injury suffered or disease contracted in line of duty”.  Compensation is precluded if the disability is caused by the veteran’s own abuse of alcohol or drugs.

In this case, according to the opinion of one VA doctor, the veteran’s alcohol abuse disability resulted from PTSD.  If so, according to the Court, his disability cannot result at the same time from an “abuse of alcohol or drugs”.  Therefore, if, on remand, VA finds that the veteran’s alcohol abuse is in fact a disability resulting from a disease contracted in the line of duty (PTSD), then it cannot be found to be a disability resulting from alcohol or drug abuse.

The Court pointed out that prior to the 1990 amendment, which added to section 1110 the words “or abuse of alcohol or drugs”, VA, in 38 CFR § 3.301 authorized the payment of compensation for the secondary effects of alcohol abuse (such as cirrhosis) but not for a primary disability.  According to the Court, the legislative history of the 1990 amendments reveals that in adding these words, Congress intended only to narrowly change the statute for the specific purposes of precluding compensation as previously allowed by section 3.301.  There was no indication in the legislative history that Congress intended to preclude compensation for an alcohol abuse disability resulting from a non-willful service-connected disorder, or for that matter, to erase the distinction between willful and involuntary acts.

The Court stated that the 1990 amendments were also not designed to change the agency’s application of 38 CFR § 3.310(a).  Prior to the 1990 amendments, alcohol or drug abuse secondary to a service connected disorder, such as PTSD, was evaluated under section 3.310(a) as “part of” the veteran’s PTSD disorder.  The Court summarized that there is nothing in the legislative history evidencing congressional intent to override VA’s application of section 3.310(a), or to bar compensation for veterans suffering from alcohol or drug abuse resulting secondarily from a service connected disorder, such as PTSD.

The Court concluded that 38 USC § 1110 does not preclude compensation for an alcohol or drug abuse disability as evidence of the increased severity of a service connected disability.  The Court stressed that the holding of this case is quite limited.  Veterans can only recover if they can adequately establish that their alcohol or drug abuse disability is secondary to or is caused by their primary service connected disorder.  Such compensation should only result where there is clear medical evidence establishing that the alcohol or drug abuse disability is indeed caused by a veteran’s primary service connected disability, and where the alcohol or drug abuse disability is not due to willful wrongdoing.
