Decision Assessment Document
 McKinney v. McDonald, Mar 11, 2016, 28 Vet.App. 15 (2016)
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that a determination as to whether a “defect,” under 38 U.S.C. 1111, is or is not noted on an examination for the purpose of determining the presumption of soundness will be based on whether disability compensation benefits are available for the condition for which the veteran seeks.  (The Court indicated that although the focus of the case was on "defect," because the meaning of "defects," "infirmities," and "disorders" are broad and overlapping, the same rationale for interpreting "defect" narrowly under section 1111 applies equally to "infirmities" and "disorders.")

The Court held that when the level of hearing loss noted on an entrance examination does not meet VA’s definition of hearing disability under 38 C.F.R. 3.385, that hearing loss is not a defect.  Therefore, the Court held that, in this case, since the veteran’s hearing loss shown on the entrance examination did not meet the requirements of 38 C.F.R. 3.385, he was entitled to the presumption of soundness under section 1111.

**Impact on VBA:**

Manual M21-1, IV.ii.2.B.1.g was revised to comply with the Court’s holding.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran’s service examination in January 1969 included audiometric testing showing a bilateral loss of 35 decibels in the 4000-hertz frequencies.  All other frequencies showed normal thresholds or losses of no more than 15 decibels.  The Navy determined that the veteran’s hearing impairment was minor and caused no significant limitation in his ability to perform most Navy jobs.  Audiometric testing was not conducted at separation.

The veteran, in April 2009, filed a claim for service connection for bilateral hearing loss asserting exposure to artillery fire in training missions and noise from helicopters.  A VA examiner, in May 2011, reviewed the audiology results of the in-service entrance examination and opined that the veteran had a pre-existing hearing loss at 4000 hertz, bilaterally.   Audiometric testing conducted in conjunction with the examination showed bilateral hearing loss averaging 44 decibels in the right ear and 35 decibels in the left ear.  The examiner stated that since no audiometric test was conducted at separation, she could not offer an opinion regarding the effect of in-service noise exposure on the current hearing loss without resorting to speculation.   The regional office denied the claim for service connection for hearing loss and the veteran appealed.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in April 2013, denied the appeal.  The Board found that the veteran was not entitled to the presumption of soundness because his service entrance audiometric test showed some degree of preexisting hearing loss.  Relying upon Hensley v. Brown, 5 Vet.App. 155 (1993),  the Board concluded that he had some degree of hearing loss upon entering service, as the entrance audiometric test revealed that the pure tone threshold at 4000 hertz was outside the normal range of hearing.  (The Court, in Hensley, stated that the threshold for normal hearing is from 0 to 20 decibels, and higher threshold levels indicate some degree of hearing loss.)

The Board found the veteran’s statements of exposure to loud noise in service to be competent, credible, and consistent with the circumstances of service; however, the Board, characterizing the VA examiner’s opinion as providing neither positive nor negative support for service connection, concluded that the preexisting hearing loss was not incurred in or aggravated by service, as there was no other competent medical opinion evidence addressing the etiology of hearing loss.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred in concluding that he had a preexisting hearing loss because the degree of hearing loss noted on his entrance examination did not constitute a hearing disability under § 3.385.

The Court held that the veteran was entitled to the presumption of soundness under section 1111 because the degree of hearing loss noted on the entrance examination did not meet VA’s definition of a “disability” for hearing loss under § 3.385.

The Court concluded that a preexisting hearing loss was noted on the entrance examination; however, it stated that whether the preexisting hearing loss means the veteran was not entitled to the presumption of soundness may not be resolved without reviewing VA’s regulation defining hearing loss as a disability.  The Court assessed that if the veteran’s hearing loss does not meet the standards under § 3.385, disability compensation benefits are denied for hearing loss.

The Court next reviewed the holdings in Winn v. Brown, 8 Vet.App. 510 (1996)  and Terry v. Principi, 340 F.3d 1378 (Fed. Cir. 2003),  and found that those decisions did not apply the ordinary meaning of defect in section 1111 because the courts concluded that the ordinary meaning of the term was not consistent with the statutory and regulatory scheme governing disability compensation for veterans.  The Court concluded that these decisions stand for the proposition that when VA, by regulation, provides that disability compensation is not permitted under 38 U.S.C. § 1110 for a particular condition, the excluded condition can never amount to a “defect” within the meaning of section 1111.  The Court held that it applied this rationale to hold that when the level of hearing loss noted on an entrance examination does not meet VA’s definition of a hearing “disability,” that level of hearing loss is not a “defect.” (In Winn, the Court, to determine the meaning of "defect," linked the term in section 1111 with the phrase in 38 U.S.C. 1110, "disability arising from personal injury ... or disease."  The Court, stating the close connection of these provisions require that the language of section 1111 must be read in connection with section 1110 on which it is conditioned, held that the term "defect" in section 1111 necessarily means a defect that amounts to or arises from a disease or injury resulting in a disability authorized by section 1110.  In Terry, the Federal Circuit following the holding in Winn that "defect" should be read narrowly to include only those defects that VA consider to amount or to arise from a disease or injury.")

The Court, holding that the hearing loss noted on the veteran’s entrance examination was not a “defect, infirmity, or disorder,” concluded that the Board erred in determining that the veteran was not entitled to the presumption of soundness and reversed the Board’s judgment.

The Court also held that the VA examiner, in May 2011, did not provide a rationale for her conclusion that in order to render a nexus opinion, an audiology test conducted contemporaneously with the separation examination was needed.  The Court stated that it was unclear that the phrase “without resorting to speculation” reflected the limits of knowledge within the medical community at large or limits of the VA examiner’s knowledge.  The Court also found that the VA examiner erred in failing to consider the veteran’s testimony of having first noticed change in his hearing during the 1970s, which is relevant because the veteran served between 1969 and 1971.  The Court noted that if the veteran first noticed hearing loss during service or soon after discharge, such fact may have assisted the VA examiner in determining the likelihood that in-service exposure to acoustic trauma affected his hearing.

The Court held that the Board erred in relying on the examiner’s opinion and vacated and remanded the matter for the Board to obtain a medical opinion consistent with its opinion and readjudicate the hearing loss claim.

The veteran also appealed the Board’s denial of service connection for a respiratory disorder to include as due to asbestos exposure.  He claimed he incurred a respiratory condition due to asbestos exposure on Navy ships.  In 2007, he submitted private evidence from 1996 showing diagnoses of asbestosis and including an opinion that his asbestos-related pleural disease were caused by asbestos exposure in service.

The veteran also submitted a 2003 referral from the Methodist Charlton Medical Center to Dr. Attiah, a pulmonologist, which included pulmonary function tests (PFTs) related to asbestos exposure.  The RO requested records from the medical center, which eventually informed the RO that Dr. Attiah’s office was not located at the facility and suggested that he be contacted at the phone number provided by the veteran.  The record contains no evidence that the RO made any further attempt to contact Dr. Attiah.

The veteran underwent a VA examination in February 2008.  The examiner referred to 2007 PFTs showing a “restrictive pattern with mild impairment” and opined that the veteran’s shortness of breath is less likely than not secondary to his asbestosis.

The Board, in denying the appeal, acknowledged that two pulmonologists had diagnosed the veteran with asbestosis in 1996; however, the Board found no proof of a current diagnosis of asbestosis.  The Board also denied service connection for restrictive lung disease on the basis that it was not etiologically related to service.

The Court held that the Board gave an inadequate statement of reasons or bases for summarily rejecting the 1996 medical reports because they were not sufficiently proximate to the date of the veteran’s claim.  The Court found that these records were relevant to as whether any current respiratory disability is related to purported in-service asbestos exposure, and the Board was required to address the evidence in determining entitlement to service connection on that basis.

The Court found other errors in the Board’s reasons or bases, to include relying on the 2008 VA examination report, which it found to be inadequate due to the examiner’s failure to discuss the prior asbestosis diagnoses.   The Court further found although the Board recognized the scope of the veteran’s claim was broad and included entitlement to service connection for any respiratory illness, including one caused by asbestos exposure, the Board failed to adequately consider whether any current respiratory disability was generally related to service.

The Court also found that the Board erred in determining that VA fulfilled its duty to assist in view of the RO’s failure to further attempt to contact the veteran after receiving notice from the medical center that it did not have Dr. Attiah’s medical records and suggested that the RO contact the physician at the telephone provided for Dr. Attiah on the June 2007 release-of-information forms.

Accordingly, the Court remanded this issue for further development.
