Decision Assessment Document
 VAOPGCPREC 07-02, Dec 3, 2002, Termination of Benefits of Fugitive Felons Under Section 505 of Public Law No. 107-103
 Office of General Counsel Precedent Opinion

**What the case is about**

When the benefits of a veteran's surviving spouse are terminated pursuant to 38 U.S.C. § 5313B because the surviving spouse is a fugitive felon, may benefits be paid to the surviving spouse's dependent children?

When the benefits of a veteran's child are terminated pursuant to 38 U.S.C. § 5313B because the child is a fugitive felon, and there are other children of the veteran in receipt of benefits, how are the other children's benefits affected?

**Impact on VBA**

The General Counsel (GC) held that if a surviving spouse of a veteran becomes a fugitive felon and consequently loses eligibility for either dependency and indemnity compensation (DIC) or improved death pension benefits, then, by operation of 38 USC § 5313B, additional benefits payable to the surviving spouse for children of the veteran would cease.  Instead, the children become eligible in their own right, and should make application for those benefits.  GC also held that if a veteran’s child receiving either DIC or improved death pension benefits becomes a fugitive felon, thereby losing individual eligibility for benefits under 38 USC § 5313B, then the benefits payable to other eligible children of the veteran do not change.

**Summary on facts and findings**

On December 27, 2001, Congress enacted Pub. L. No. 107-103, 115 Stat. 976.  Section 505(a) of that law added 38 USC § 5313B, which provides in pertinent part that:

A veteran who is otherwise eligible for a benefit … may not be paid or otherwise provided such benefit for any period during which such veteran is a fugitive felon.  A dependent of a veteran who is otherwise eligible for a benefit … may not be paid or otherwise provided such benefit for any period during which such veteran or such dependent is a fugitive felon.

The term “dependent” in the statute is defined to include “a spouse, surviving spouse, child, or dependent parent of a veteran.”  GC noted that if a surviving spouse of a veteran becomes a fugitive felon, then 38 USC § 5313B bars payment of the full amount of the award, including payment of DIC or improved death pension being apportioned to the veteran’s children not in the custody of the “fugitive felon” spouse.  Instead, the children may receive benefits in their own right because the statutes governing DIC (38 USC § 1313(a)) and improved death pension (38 USC § 1542) benefits provide independent eligibility for a veteran’s children when there is no surviving spouse eligible for benefits.

Referencing the statutes governing entitlement to improved death pension and DIC benefits, GC stated that the benefit amount payable to each entitled child does not change when another child loses eligibility for benefits by becoming a “fugitive felon” under 38 USC §  5313B.  GC noted that there was no statutory provision for increasing other children’s shares when another child loses eligibility, so long as the child who loses eligibility still meets the definition of a “child” for title 38 purposes.

Title 38 CFR, Part 3, Adjudication, and M21-1, Part IV, Authorization Procedures, Chapters 25 and 26, should be amended to reflect the provisions of this opinion and 38 USC § 5313B covering the new prohibition on providing certain benefits with respect to persons who are fugitive felons.
