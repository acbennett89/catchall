Decision Assessment Document
 Fagan v. Shinseki, Jul 22, 2009, 573 F.3d 1282
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit, in rejecting the veteran’s argument that the Veterans Court misinterpreted the benefit of the doubt doctrine codified at [38 U.S.C. § 5107(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5107), held that the Veterans Court did not err as a matter of law when it treated as “non-evidence” or not pertinent a VA medical examiner’s statement that she was unable to render an opinion concerning the relationship of the veteran’s hearing loss to military service.  The Federal Circuit characterized the statement as providing neither positive nor negative support for service connection.  Therefore, this statement was not pertinent evidence, one way or the other, regarding service connection.

Note: 38 U.S.C. § 5107(b) provides:  The Secretary shall consider all information and lay and medical evidence of record in a case before the Secretary with respect to benefits under laws administered by the Secretary.  When there is an approximate balance of positive and negative evidence regarding any issue material to the determination of a matter, the Secretary shall resolve all reasonable doubt in favor of the claimant.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.  Evidence of record, such as an examiner’s statement of being unable to render an opinion, should not be characterized as “non-evidence.” In referring to such evidence in the rating decisions, it should more appropriately be described in such terms as having neither positive nor negative value.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served from 1968 to 1970, was exposed to loud noise from recreational hunting prior to service.  Audiometric testing upon entering service revealed moderately severe hearing loss in the left ear.  During service in Vietnam he was further exposed to loud noise from explosions, airplanes, helicopters, and wind.  The service records document no complaint of or treatment for hearing loss.  Audiometric testing was not undertaken at separation; however, a whisper-voice test was administered, which found no hearing loss in the left ear.

After service, the veteran participated in activities involving noise exposure, such as hunting, and working with weed eaters, leaf blowers, and chainsaws.  He did not utilize hearing protection until approximately 2002.  The veteran indicated having failed a hearing test when attempting to enlist in the Connecticut Army National Guard in 1971 or 1972; however, VA’s attempts to obtain those records were unsuccessful.  Aside from lay statements from friends and family members in 2004 as to past recollections of the veteran’s hearing loss, there is no evidence of hearing loss in the record until the veteran underwent a medical examination in 2002.

The veteran filed a claim for service connection for bilateral hearing loss in 2002.  He underwent a VA examination in October 2003 in which the examiner noted the in-service and post-service noise exposure, as well as lay statements concerning hearing loss.  The examiner dismissed the reliability of the whisper-voice test, stating that it did not rule out or confirm hearing loss.  The examiner rendered the following opinion:

          “… due to the veteran’s history of post-military noise exposure,

           and without audiometric information obtained at the time of his

           release from service or shortly thereafter, it is not possible to

           determine if the further decrease in high frequencies for the left

           ear and the high frequency loss in the right ear is related to military

           service.”

The regional office denied the claim.  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) sustained the denial in January 2006.  The Board considered the veteran’s testimony as to failing a National Guard hearing test, in-service noise exposure, and lay statements; however, it also observed that there was no medical evidence during service or shortly thereafter showing hearing loss in service, the first documentation of hearing loss was 30 years after service, and the veteran had not sought treatment for hearing loss until 2002.  The Board concluded that the preponderance of the evidence was against the veteran’s claim.

The Veterans Court, affirming the Board’s decision, characterized the VA examiner’s statement about being unable to opine as to whether the veteran’s hearing loss was related to service as “non-evidence.”  The Veterans Court ruled that the evidence regarding nexus was not in equipoise; therefore, the benefit-of-the-doubt rule under section 5107(b) was inapplicable.

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that the Veterans Court erred as a matter of law in interpreting “evidence” in [section 5107(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5107) too narrowly and, as a result, improperly excluded the evidence in the VA examiner’s report.  He contended that the Veterans Court’s misinterpreted “evidence” to only include definitive medical opinions and, thus, dismissed the VA medical examiner’s report from relevant evidence because the examiner was unable to come to a definitive medical opinion.

The Federal Circuit disagreed with the veteran, explaining that the Veterans Court did not adopt a rule permitting the exclusion of all submitted evidence discussed in an examiner’s report, unless the report provides a definite, conclusive opinion.  The Veterans Court reference to the examiner’s remarks as “non-evidence” was characterized by the Federal Circuit as merely referring to the examiner’s statement that she was unable to render an opinion.

The Federal Circuit found that the Veterans Court properly decided that the veteran could not benefit from section 5107(b) because the preponderance of the evidence weighed against a nexus.  The Veterans Court had cited the same evidence upon which the Board relied, which included the examiner’s inability to render a decision regarding service connection.  Thus, although the Veterans Court characterized the VA medical examiner’s remarks as “non-evidence,” it did not fail to consider any relevant evidence on the question of record.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court decision.  However, in a footnote, the Federal Circuit reiterated its statements from [*Hogan v. Peake*, 544 F.3d 1295 (Fed. Cir. 2008)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014876/) that it found troubling the reference to statements as “non-evidence.”  Regardless of whether an ultimate opinion is offered in an examiner’s report, that report, as well as the facts discussed therein regarding the etiology of a claimed disability must be considered as “evidence” of whether the disability was incurred in service.
