Decision Assessment Document
 Dalton v. Nicholson, Feb 16, 2007, 21 Vet.App. 23
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that a veteran awarded a total disability evaluation on the basis of individual unemployability (TDIU) resulting from a disability or disabilities already established as service connected is entitled to consideration of [38 U.S.C. § 5110(b)(2)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5110) in determining the effective date of the TDIU grant.

Although an increase to the next disability level during the one year prior to receipt of a claim is generally required, the “increase in disability” requirement for section 5110(b)(2) application may be satisfied by either meeting the schedular requirements or demonstrating inability to maintain or secure substantially gainful employment in accordance with [38 C.F.R. § 4.16(a)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=0fb0ccbcf2b19aa5d887f34c0a3f27d8&node=se38.1.4_116&rgn=div8), provided that both criteria to support a TDIU award are present during the applicable period.

The Court referenced its previous precedents that TDIU entitlement requires an increase in disability that satisfies the requirements of section 5110(b)(2); however, it cautioned that it did not intend to answer the question of whether every TDIU award is an award of increased compensation.

The holding, regarding application of section 5110(b)(2) to a TDIU claim, was limited to the factual scenario presented:  A TDIU grant based on inability to secure or follow a substantially gainful occupation due to an already service-connected disability and the TDIU award based in part on the worsening of the service-connected disability.

The Court, invoking its own and Federal Circuit case law, rejected an invitation to interpret [38 U.S.C. § 1154(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1154) as eliminating the need for medical nexus evidence in establishing service connection for a disability.

However, in finding errors in the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) reasoning, the Court held that the Board was not permitted to weigh the veteran’s lay evidence against contrary evidence in determining whether the lay evidence was satisfactory to trigger section 1154(b) presumption, nor may the Board rely on the absence of an official record to find lay evidence incredible as it would be inconsistent with the purpose of the statute.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran’s honorable service from 1966 to 1968 included service in Vietnam.  Service medical records (SMRs) from April 1967 show that he was involved in automobile accident and complained of left-side pain.  X-rays were negative.  Left flank contusion was diagnosed.  Separation showed no complaint or findings of back problems.

Service connection for post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) was granted by the regional office (RO) in June 1995 with a 50 percent evaluation.  The Board denied an appeal for increased evaluation in April 1999.  This decision was not appealed and became final.

The RO received a statement in support of claim from the veteran on June 20, 2000, that identified an original claim for service connection for a back injury.  The veteran stated that he hurt his back in Vietnam in 1968.  He referenced a SMR entry and indicated that he was filing for service connection under the “combat advantage rule.”  The veteran also stated that “this is an informal claim for I.U. [TDIU] benefits.  I am unable to work because of my PTSD and back condition.”

Chronic PTSD and severe, recurrent major depression was diagnosed by a VA psychiatric examination conducted the following month.  The examiner commented that the veteran did not seem capable of maintaining gainful employment.  A [VA Form 21-8940, *Veterans Application for Increased* *Compensation Based on Unemployability*](http://www.vba.va.gov/pubs/forms/VBA-21-8940-ARE.pdf), was received by the RO on November 17, 2000.

The RO increased the schedular evaluation for PTSD to 70 percent and awarded a TDIU evaluation.  Effective date of June 20, 2000, was assigned for both grants.  The claim for a back disability was denied.  A Notice of Disagreement was filed in response to the September 2001 RO decision concerning the denial of service connection for a back disability and the effective date of the 70 percent and TDIU ratings for PTSD.

A VA examination addressing the veteran’s back was undertaken in April 2003.  The veteran conveyed to the examiner that he hurt his back in Vietnam when an ammunition dump exploded.  The examiner concluded that, after reviewing the claims file and SMRs, that there was no evidence of a back injury in service and opined that it was not likely that the current back problems are related to military service.

The Board, denying the veteran’s claims in May 2004, concluded that the veteran’s back condition was unrelated to service as the lack of subsequent treatment following the left flank contusion in 1967 and absence of notation of back problems at separation showed that any injury was transient in nature and not productive of a chronic disability.  The Board also determined that entitlement to an increased rating for PTSD was first demonstrated subsequent to June 20, 2000, and a formal claim for PTSD was first filed on June 20, 2000.

The veteran argued before the Court that the VA did not provide him adequate notice under [38 U.S.C. § 5103(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103) by failing to inform him of the missing evidence that would entitle him to an earlier effective date, the Board erred by not applying [section 5110(b)(2)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5110) and [38 C.F.R. § 3.400(o)(2)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=ff67be32a984b7a96e0e246dae157572&node=se38.1.3_1400&rgn=div8) in determining an effective date for the PTSD evaluation, and that application of [section 1154(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1154), or together with [38 U.S.C. § 105(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/105), creates a presumption of service connection eliminating need to submit medical nexus evidence linking any current back disability to service.

VCAA challenge:

The Court found that the Board erred in concluding that the duty to notify had been satisfied, as there was no pre-adjudicatory letter addressing what was necessary to warrant an earlier effective date for the schedular 70 percent and TDIU grants.

However, the Court determined that the notice error was not prejudicial as the record demonstrated that the veteran, through his counsel, was aware of the information and evidence necessary to establish entitlement to an earlier effective date and was afforded a meaningful opportunity to effectively participate in the adjudication of these claims.

The Court, in making this determination, noted that the veteran had been represented by counsel since 1998 and that the veteran essentially argued that the VA was required to provide him with a “predecisional adjudication” concerning the sufficiency of the evidence he had submitted. Note: The Court has recently held in [*Locklear v. Nicholson*, 20 Vet.App. 410 (2006)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014878/) that VA is not required to provide a claimant with a “predecisional adjudication.”

Earlier Effective Date for TDIU:

The Court indicated that this appeal posed the question of whether a TDIU grant is an award of increased compensation governed by section 5110(b)(2) for purposes of assignment of an effective date.  The Secretary conceded that there may be instances when section 5110(b)(2) is triggered in a TDIU claim, such as arising in conjunction with the worsening of a service-connected disability; however, the Secretary also suggested that, because a TDIU award is based on a finding of individual unemployability, not all TDIU awards result from an increase in disability and would not fall under section 5110(b)(2).

The Court referenced its previous case law that held that a TDIU award is an “increase” in compensation and that an already service-connected veteran who becomes unable to secure or follow a substantially gainful occupation as a result of that service-connected disability has logically undergone an increase in disability.  The Court also noted that the VA Form 21-8940 is an application for “increased compensation” based on individual unemployability.

The Secretary’s suggestion that section 5110(b)(2) application is limited to TDIU awards where there is a concurrent increase in the schedular rating was rejected.  The Court held that – provided that both [§ 4.16](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=c1c0b3b7a58554f0a48640de632c76f8&node=se38.1.4_116&rgn=div8) criteria are met – either the schedular requirements or an inability to secure or follow a substantially gainful occupation under § 4.16(a) manifesting within the year prior to receipt of the TDIU claim satisfies the “increase in disability” requirement of section 5110(b)(2).  It is irrelevant which of the two criteria first became factually ascertainable.

The Court, finding that the Board erred in referencing 38 U.S.C. § 5110(a), rather than section 5110(b)(2), and by failing to determine whether both criteria for TDIU under § 4.16(a) were present during the year prior to June 20, 2000, with either one first becoming ascertainable at any time during that period, remanded this issue for the Board’s application of section 5110(b)(2) and § 3.400(o)(2).

Back Disability and section 1154(b) Application:

The Court, referencing case law interpreting Congressional intent that section 1154(b) should reduce the evidentiary burden for combat veterans with respect to evidence of in-service incurrence or aggravation of an injury or disease, rejected the argument that the statute provides a statutory presumption that a claimed condition is service connected.

In finding the Board’s analysis faulty, the Court concluded that the Board failed to make the necessary credibility determination under section 1154(b) and prematurely concluded that the preponderance of the evidence does not demonstrate incurrence of in-service back injury.  The Court also found error in the Board’s conclusion that the back injury was apparently transient in nature as such a finding is a medical conclusion that the Board is not competent to make and the Board’s reliance upon the opinion of a VA medical examiner that impermissibly ignored the veteran’s lay assertions that he had sustained a back injury in service. *See*[*McLendon v. Nicholson*, 20 Vet.App. 79 (2006)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014871/); [*Buchanan v. Nicholson*, 451 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2006)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014893/); [*Coburn v. Nicholson*, 19 Vet.App. 427 (2006)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014858/).

The Court remanded this issue for determination by the Board as to whether a back injury was incurred in service consistent with its holding.  If the Board makes such affirmative determination, VA’s duty to assist would require a new examination as such determination would render the 2003 VA examination to be inadequate.
