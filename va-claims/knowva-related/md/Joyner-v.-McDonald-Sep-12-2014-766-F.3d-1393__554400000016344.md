Decision Assessment Document
 Joyner v. McDonald, Sep 12, 2014, 766 F.3d 1393
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court erred in concluding that pain cannot constitute a qualifying chronic disability under [38 U.S.C. § 1117](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1117).  The plain language of the statute makes clear that pain, such as muscle pain or joint pain, may establish an undiagnosed illness that causes a qualifying chronic disability.

The Federal Circuit held that section 1117 does not require a veteran to demonstrate that a medical professional has eliminated all possible diagnoses before the veteran can be compensated for a disability due to an undiagnosed illness.

Although [38 C.F.R. § 3.317(a)(1)(ii)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=a1b1784a47f71fa667b817e6456ef898&node=se38.1.3_1317&rgn=div8) provides that a “qualifying chronic disability” is one that “by history, physical examination, and laboratory tests cannot be attributed to any known clinical diagnosis,” the Federal Circuit held it does not require that a veteran must be subjected to all possible medical testing available and then “diagnosed” with an “undiagnosed illness” after all possible medical conditions have been ruled out.  The statute and regulation require only that the veteran has been evaluated and no diagnosis could be made concerning the cause of the qualifying chronic disability.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran’s service included a tour of duty in the Persian Gulf.  During service he was twice treated for neck pain; however, the separation showed his neck to be normal.  He later filed a claim for disability compensation for a neck condition, which the regional office and the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) denied under [38 U.S.C. § 1110](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1110), finding no evidence of a diagnosed neck condition.

In his appeal to the Veterans Court, the veteran argued that the Board erred by failing to consider whether he was entitled to service connection under section [1117](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1117).  The Veterans Court held that the veteran was not entitled to compensation under section 1117 because pain does not constitute a disability.

The Federal Circuit, holding that pain may establish a qualifying chronic disability under section 1117, noted that the implementing regulation also states that “muscle pain” or “joint pain” “may be manifestations of undiagnosed illness.”  See [38 C.F.R. § 3.317(b)(4), (5)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=a1b1784a47f71fa667b817e6456ef898&node=se38.1.3_1317&rgn=div8).

The Federal Circuit rejected the government’s argument that section 1117 imposes a burden on a veteran to demonstrate that a medical professional has eliminated all possible diagnoses before being compensated for a disability stemming from an undiagnosed illness pursuant to section 1117(a)(2)(A).

The Federal Circuit noted that VA, in promulgating the final rule, § 3.317, explained that the regulation only required the following:  “Physicians should simply record all noted signs and reported symptoms, document all clinical findings, and provide a diagnoses where possible. If the signs and symptoms are not characteristic of a known clinical diagnosis, the physician should so indicate.”  See 60 Fed. Reg. 6661, 6662 (Feb. 3, 1995).  The statute and regulation require only that the veteran has been evaluated and no diagnosis could be made concerning the cause of the qualifying chronic disability.

The Federal Circuit vacated the Veterans Court decision and remanded for the Veterans Court to analyze whether the veteran has an undiagnosed illness and whether he satisfied the remaining elements under section 1117 to establish entitlement to compensation.
