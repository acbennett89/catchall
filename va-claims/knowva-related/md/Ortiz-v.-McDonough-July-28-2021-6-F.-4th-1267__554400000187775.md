Decision **Decision Assessment Document**

**Ortiz v. McDonough, July 28, 2021, 6 F. 4th 1267**

**United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (Federal Circuit)**

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit found that the addition of 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(3) in 2010 was a “liberalizing” regulatory change under § 3.114(a), and that VA had conceded that the Veteran was awarded service connection for PTSD under 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(3). It therefore held that under 38 C.F.R. § 3.114(a)(3) an effective date for a period of one year prior to the date of receipt of the claim was appropriate. As a result of this decision, for PTSD claims filed on or after July 12, 2010 and granted under the authority of 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(3), adjudicators must consider whether an effective date under 38 C.F.R. § 3.114(a)(3) is warranted. This decision applies to claims pending on or filed after July 28, 2021 and is not a basis to re-adjudicate claims decisions that were finalized prior to that date.

**Impact on VBA:**

The M21-1 Adjudication Procedures Manual will be updated at V.ii.4.A.6.c., VIII.iv.1.A.3.f. and VIII.iv.1.D.2.d to reference this decision.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

VA acknowledged service-connection for the Veteran’s posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) pursuant to 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(3) The dispute before the Federal Circuit concerned the effective date of the award.

The Veteran first claimed service-connection for PTSD in 1997 but was denied because he did not provide corroborating evidence of his alleged in-service stressor. That decision became final.

In 2010 VA amended § 3.304(f) by adding what is now subsection (f)(3) to state an exception to the corroborating evidence requirement relevant to PTSD claims in certain circumstances. Specifically, in relevant part, § 3.304(f)(3) provides: “If a stressor claimed by a veteran is related to the veteran's fear of hostile military or terrorist activity and a VA psychiatrist or psychologist, or a psychiatrist or psychologist with whom VA has contracted, confirms that the claimed stressor is adequate to support a diagnosis of posttraumatic stress disorder and that the veteran's symptoms are related to the claimed stressor, in the absence of clear and convincing evidence to the contrary, and provided the claimed stressor is consistent with the places, types, and circumstances of the veteran's service, the veteran's lay testimony alone may establish the occurrence of the claimed in-service stressor.”

More than a year after the aforementioned regulatory change took effect, the Veteran filed to reopen his claim and invoked the lightened proof requirement of § 3.304(f)(3). VA reopened and granted the Veteran’s claim, assigning a 100 percent evaluation for his PTSD effective May 22, 2012, the date VA received his request to reopen. The Veteran contended that the effective date should have been one year earlier (May 22, 2011) based upon 38 C.F.R. § 3.114(a). He argued that § 3.304(f)(3) was a “liberalizing” law or VA issue, entitling him to the extra year of benefits under § 3.114(a).  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals and then the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims rejected his request for an earlier effective date because they concluded that § 3.304(f)(3) was not a “liberalizing” measure.

The Federal Circuit disagreed and reversed, concluding that the addition of § 3.304(f)(3) in 2010 was “liberalizing” under § 3.114(a). The Federal Circuit found it sufficient to come within § 3.114(a)’s “liberalizing” category that § 3.304(f)(3) reduced the affirmative burden of production to establish an element of entitlement to compensation by eliminating the need for corroborating evidence of an alleged stressor in certain specified circumstances.  Applying § 3.114(a)’s provision for establishing an effective date up to one year prior to the receipt of a claim when a grant is based upon a liberalizing law or a liberalizing VA issue, the Federal Circuit therefore determined the correct effective date for benefits in this case is May 22, 2011, rather than May 22, 2012.

In this case neither VA nor the Veteran disputed that the PTSD claim was granted pursuant to the 2010 addition of what is now subsection (f)(3) to 38 C.F.R. § 3.304.  The only question before the Federal Circuit concerned the proper effective date under 38 C.F.R. § 3.114(a), and the only issue in dispute about the applicability of § 3.114(a) was whether the 2010 adoption of what is now § 3.304(f)(3) was a “liberalizing” change under § 3.114(a).

The Federal Circuit engaged in statutory and regulatory analysis and construction and considered the plain meaning of the word ‘liberalizing.’ The Federal Circuit also provided very detailed reasoning for its conclusion that its prior decisions in Spencer v. Brown, 17 F.3d 368 (Fed. Cir. 1994), and Routen v. West, 142 F.3d 1434 (Fed. Cir. 1998) were not dispositive or controlling concerning the outcome of the issue presented in this case.

Of note, VA did not dispute before the Federal Circuit that addition of §3.304(f)(3) in 2010 made the evidentiary burden for establishing service connection for PTSD based upon fear of hostile terrorist or military activity less strict.  Moreover, in reaching its decision the Federal Circuit noted that statements VA made in adopting the 2010 Final Rule underscore a conclusion that § 3.304(f)(3) is liberalizing. In the Final Rule, VA recognized that the 2010 amendment effectively “eliminate[d]” an evidentiary “requirement” when the specified preconditions were met and characterized the effect of § 3.304(f)(3) as “liberalizing.”

Although not explicitly stated, in reaching its decision the Federal Circuit appears to have overruled the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claim’s ruling in Foreman v. Shulkin, 29 Vet.App. 146 (2018), which held that 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(3) was not “liberalizing” under 38 C.F.R. § 3.114(a).
