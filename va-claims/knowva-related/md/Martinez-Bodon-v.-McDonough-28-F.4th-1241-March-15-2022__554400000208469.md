**Decision Assessment Document**

***Martinez-Bodon v. McDonough,* 28 F.4th 1241 (March 15, 2022)**

**United States Court of Appeals, Federal Circuit**

**What the case is about:**

The Veteran appealed a decision of the United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (CAVC) affirming a Board of Veterans Appeals (Board) decision that he is not entitled to service connection for a mental condition. Upon appeal the Federal Circuit Court of Appeals (Federal Circuit) affirmed that 38 C.F.R. §§ 4.125(a) and 4.130 require a diagnosis in conformity with Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), American Psychiatric Association (2013) (DSM–5) as a precondition to service connect a mental condition.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Manual M21-1 will be amended at V.ii.3.C.2.d. to reference this Federal Circuit decision.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The Veteran served on active duty in the Army from August 1967 to March 1969. In April 2016, he filed a claim for benefits for diabetes and for anxiety secondary to his diabetes. At his VA psychiatric examination in September 2016, he reported: “I can’t sleep well, my right eye trembles too much and that bothers me and I get very anxious about it.”  He denied having other symptoms. The VA examiner found that these symptoms did not meet the “criteria for a mental condition as per DSM–5” and thus concluded that she could not establish a relationship between the Veteran’s diabetes and anxiety. Therefore service connection for diabetes was acknowledged but service connection for a mental condition was denied.

The Veteran appealed that denial to the Board which reached the same conclusion. He then appealed to the CAVC, arguing that even without a formal diagnosis, his symptoms constitute a disability for service-connection purposes under the federal Circuit’s definition of “disability” in *Saunders v. Wilkie*, 886 F.3d 1356, 1363 (Fed. Cir. 2018). The CAVC rejected that argument and affirmed the Board’s decision, interpreting two regulations, 38 C.F.R. §§ 4.125(a) and 4.130, “to require a DSM–5 diagnosis as a precondition to compensate mental conditions.” *Martinez-Bodon v. Wilkie,* 32 Vet. App. 393, 401 (Vet. App. 2020).

The Veteran pursued his appeal to the Federal Circuit. In considering his arguments the Federal Circuit first addressed the role of that *Saunders* played in this case. The Federal Circuit noted that *Saunders* held that pain that reaches the level of a “functional impairment of earning capacity” could constitute a “disability” under 38 U.S.C. § 1110. Before the CAVC the Veteran argued that requiring a formal diagnosis of a mental condition to establish service connection is inconsistent with that holding. Specifically, he asserted “that the holding in *Saunders* extends to more than just pain and ‘encompasses any undiagnosed disability that results in functional loss.” However, the Federal Circuit determined that *Saunders* does not apply in the circumstances of this case. As explained in *Larson v. McDonough*, 10 F.4th 1325, 1329 (Fed. Cir. 2021), “*Saunders* articulated a definition of ‘disability’ for § 1110 purposes that is distinct from and not coextensive with disabilities listed on the rating schedule.” The question raised in *Saunders* was whether conditions not on the rating schedule may still be considered for service connection purposes under § 1110. The Federal Circuit specified that is not the question raised in this case. Instead, in this case there is a specific ratings table for mental conditions (at 38 C.F.R.§ 4.130), and the Veteran is seeking a rating under that table. The Federal Circuit found that because §§ 4.125 and 4.130 speak precisely to the issue raised, they must govern and noted that to the extent the Veteran asked the Federal Circuit to review the actual contents of that ratings table, it lacked jurisdiction to do so.

While the Veteran argued that the CAVC wrongly interpreted 38 C.F.R. §§ 4.125(a) and 4.130, the Federal Circuit ultimately held that § 4.125(a) and the rating schedule in § 4.130 require a mental condition to be formally diagnosed in order for service connection to be established.

The Federal Circuit specified that 38 C.F.R. §  4.125(a) must be read in conjunction with § 4.130 because “regulations that precede the rating schedule for [a condition] explain how to arrive at proper evaluations under the [diagnostic codes] appearing in the disability rating schedule.” Thus, § 4.125(a) instructs that a diagnosis consistent with the DSM–5 must be present before a veteran may obtain a rating under § 4.130. Both regulations emphasize the importance of the DSM–5 to diagnosis and rating, incorporate it by reference, and indicate that a DSM–5 conforming diagnosis is required for service connection.

A zero percent rating explicitly requires that “[a] mental condition has been formally diagnosed” under § 4.130. The Veteran argued that the absence of that language from the other rating levels in § 4.130 means that no diagnosis is required above the zero percent level. The Federal Circuit noted that the rating regime must be read as a whole, and that the structure of the entire ratings table indicates that each higher level includes the same baseline requirement: a diagnosis. Each rating level is based on severity, with symptoms and occupational and social impairments increasing in severity with each step up in rating. The Federal Circuit quoted the CAVC’s reasoning that “[t]his upwardly cascading nature of the rating criteria, when read in the context of the introductory paragraph’s emphasis on the DSM–5, plainly supports that § 4.130 requires a DSM–5 diagnosis in order to rate mental conditions at every rating level.” The argument advanced by the Veteran would mean that “a veteran would have to meet a more stringent requirement—a DSM–5 diagnosis—for a noncompensable rating than he or she would for a 30, 50, or 100% rating” and the Federal Circuit determined such an absurd result should be avoided. The Federal Circuit emphasized that it cannot be the case that while the DSM–5 is critical to rating and diagnosis at the noncompensable level it could be discarded at levels that could provide significant compensation.

The Veteran also argued that the “if” at the beginning of § 4.125(a) indicates that the regulation is “conditional and applies only when a diagnosis is at issue in a case,” that is, in “cases where a mental health disorder has been diagnosed.” Under that argument, if there has not been a diagnosis, then § 4.125 would not apply, and VA would proceed to rate a veteran’s symptoms anyway. But the Federal Circuit specified such an interpretation is at odds with the regulatory framework discussed above, and that to give full effect to the regulation § 4.125(a) is best read not as conditioned on diagnoses, but as a requirement that a diagnosis be made for any rating to apply under § 4.130.

Finally, the Federal Circuit noted that other regulations for service connection also recognize § 4.125 as requiring a diagnosis in order for a rating to be assigned. For example, the regulation governing service connection for posttraumatic stress disorder states that “[s]ervice connection for posttraumatic stress disorder requires medical evidence diagnosing the condition in accordance with § 4.125(a) of this chapter.” 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f) (emphasis added). Although the Veteran argued this shows VA knows how to explicitly require a diagnosis when intended and failed to do so at the compensable levels in § 4.130, the Federal Circuit noted that the PTSD regulation makes specific reference to § 4.125(a) only because it is in a separate part of chapter 38. Because § 4.130 directly follows § 4.125, there is no need to restate the requirement. The Federal Circuit also found that the regulatory history of the regulations in question show that VA understood them to unambiguously require a diagnosis and that the DSM–5, incorporated by reference and subject to notice-and-comment rulemaking, provides a standardized guide for diagnoses upon which raters can rely.

The Federal Circuit concluded that the plain language of the regulations, their structure, and their consistent emphasis of the DSM–5 lead to conclusion that a DSM–5 diagnosis is required for service connection to be awarded under § 4.130. It therefore affirmed the CAVC decision denying the Veteran’s claim for service connection for an anxiety disorder.
