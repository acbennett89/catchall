Decision Assessment Document
 Robinson v. Shinseki, Mar 31, 2009, 22 Vet.App. 440
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the statutory scheme set forth in [38 U.S.C. § 501](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/501) established that Congress certainly contemplated that the VA Secretary would institute substantive rules under the authority of this section. Accordingly, the Court held that section 501(a) authorizes VA to promulgate [38 C.F.R. § 3.11](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=7195f8a162ef31156f52e59a958b54c7&node=se38.1.3_111&rgn=div8) and that the regulation is an entirely reasonable gap-filling measure within the rulemaking authority granted by Congress.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran was in continuous receipt of 100 percent disability compensation from his discharge in 1978 until his death by homicide in 1985. The death certificate indicated that he had died from a gunshot wound to the head and chest, having been shot by another person.

 The surviving spouse’s claim for dependency and indemnity compensation (DIC) benefits was denied in September 1985 on the grounds that she “intentionally and wrongfully caused the death” of her husband. She was convicted in the State of Florida of second-degree murder. The conviction was overturned; however, she remained in custody and entered a plea of nolo contendere with protestation of innocence in a negotiated plea agreement. A Florida court found her guilty of the lesser offense of manslaughter and sentenced her to time served.

The surviving spouse received a “Certificate of Restoration of Civil Rights” in August 1990 and the Florida Governor, in 2002, granted her a pardon without the right to own, possess, or use firearms.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in May 2006, denied DIC benefits solely on the basis of the manslaughter conviction. The Board found that the manslaughter conviction was essentially a guilty verdict establishing that she had wrongfully and intentionally caused the veteran’s death. The Board also indicated that a nolo contendere plea cannot be used as an admission of the facts charged in a subsequent civil suit and that it was the judgment of the court – not the plea – that constituted a determination of guilt. The Board also noted that the pardon was less than fully unconditional and did not expunge the manslaughter conviction.

The appellant argued before the Court that § 3.11, which cited [section 501(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/501) as its authority, was unlawful because the VA Secretary exceeded the lawful rulemaking authority granted to him by Congress. She reasoned that because the statutory list of section 501(a) contained only procedural matters, the statute only authorized the Secretary to adopt procedural rules and did not authorize substantive rulemaking. The appellant noted that other sections of title 38 explicitly granted substantive rulemaking power to the Secretary and that if Congress had intended to convey general substantive rulemaking powers in section 501(a), it would have done so.

Note: Section 501(a) provides that “the Secretary has authority to prescribe all rules and regulations which are necessary or appropriate to carry out the laws administered by the Department and are consistent with those laws, including – (1) regulations with respect to the nature and extent of proof and evidence and the method of taking and furnishing them in order to establish the right to benefits under such laws; (2) the forms of application by claimants under such laws; (3) the methods of making investigations and medical examinations; and (4) the manner and form of adjudications and awards.”

The Court, in rejecting the appellant’s arguments, held that section 501(a) authorized the Secretary to promulgate [§ 3.11](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=7195f8a162ef31156f52e59a958b54c7&node=se38.1.3_111&rgn=div8). The Court described [section 501(c)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/501) as granting the Secretary authority to regulate where necessary to carry out VA’s mission, whereas other statutory provisions under title 38 give authority with a specific mandate to promulgate regulations. The Court also stated the examples given under section 501(a) do not restrict the general language stating that the Secretary may prescribe “all regulations which are necessary and appropriate.”

To support its conclusion that Congress certainly contemplated that the Secretary would institute substantive rules under section 501, the Court noted that § 3.11 went through the notice and comment procedure, as set forth in [5 U.S.C. § 553](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/553), and indicated that section 553 provides that the notice and comment procedure does not apply to “interpretative rules, general statements of policy, or rules of agency organization, procedure, or practice.”

The Court also held that the manslaughter conviction resulting from a duly supported plea of nolo contendere was sufficient to support the Board’s finding that the appellant intentionally and wrongfully caused the veteran’s death. The Court stated that the Board correctly noted that it is the judgment of the court – not the plea of the defendant – that constitutes a determination of guilt. The Court also observed that Florida law requires that a court must be satisfied that a plea is voluntary and that a factual basis for the plea exists before it accepts a nolo contendere plea.

The Court affirmed the Board’s May 2006 decision.
