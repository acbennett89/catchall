Decision Assessment Document
Francway v. Wilkie, Oct 15, 2019, No. 2018-2136
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that a veteran is required to raise a challenge to the presumption of competency of a VA medical examiner in the first instance.  Once a challenge to the competency of a medical examiner is raised, the presumption of competency has no further effect, and VA bears the burden of establishing the examiner’s qualifications.

The Federal Circuit further held that since the veteran is obligated to raise the issue in the first instance, the claimant must have the ability to secure from the VA the information necessary to raise the competency challenge.  Once a request for information is made as to the competency of the examiner, the veteran has the right, absent unusual circumstances, to the curriculum vitae and other information about the qualifications of a medical examiner.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Federal Circuit’s holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in the M21-1 Manual under III.iv.3.D.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served in the Navy from August 1968 to May 1970, contended that he injured his back in service and that his current low back disability is related to the in-service injury.  In April 2003, he filed a claim for service connection for his low back disability.  He was examined multiple times between 2003 and 2011 by an orthopedist and had his medical records separately reviewed by the orthopedist and an internist.  They concluded, along with a physician’s assistant, that the veteran’s low back disability was not likely connected to his injury in service.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in 2013, remanded the case based on a buddy statement attesting to the veteran’s history of a low back disability after his in-service injury in 1969.

In 2014, the veteran was examined by the same orthopedist who had examined him previously.  The orthopedist concluded that the veteran’s current back symptoms were unlikely to be related to his in-service injury.  However, the orthopedist did not review the buddy statement.  Subsequently, the internist who also had previously examined the veteran, reviewed the veteran’s file to include the buddy statement, and concluded that it would be speculative to say that his current back symptoms were related to his earlier injury.  The case was returned to the Board after denial of the claim by the regional office.  The Board concluded that there was insufficient evidence of a nexus between the veteran’s injury in service and his current low back disability.

The veteran argued, for the first time, before the Veterans Court that the internist who had reviewed the buddy statement was not an appropriate medical specialist within the meaning of the remand order.  However, the Veterans Court held that the veteran had not preserved that claim because he did not challenge the examiner’s qualifications before the Board.

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that the presumption of competency is inconsistent with the VA’s duty to assist under 38 U.S.C. 5103A and the benefit-of-the-doubt rule under 38 U.S.C. 5107(b).

At the outset, the Federal Circuit noted that it had previously held that the Board and Veterans Court must properly apply a presumption of competency in reviewing the opinion of VA medical examiners.  See Rizzo v. Shinseki, 580 F.3d 1288 (Fed. Cir. 2009).  The Federal Circuit also declined to order en banc review to consider overruling Rizzo, as it characterized the “presumption of competency” to be far narrower than the veteran asserts and to not be inconsistent with the statutory scheme.

The Federal Circuit also rejected the veteran’s argument that the Rizzo held that the veteran bears the burden of persuasion, or at least the production, of showing that the examiner was incompetent.  The Federal Circuit explained that the presumption of competency requires nothing more than is required for veteran claimants in other contexts – simply a requirement that the veteran raise the issue.

The Federal Circuit held that once the issue is raised, the presumption of competency has no further effect, and VA bears the burden of establishing the examiner’s qualifications.  VA also must provide the veteran with information requested about the examiner’s competency, if such request is made, as the veteran has the right to such information in the absence of unusual circumstances.

The veteran also separately argued that his case is distinguishable because the issue of the examiner’s’ competency arose in the context of a remand order from the Board requiring an “appropriate medical specialist.”  The veteran contended that the Board cannot presume the competency of the selected examiner in a specialty because the presumption is one of general medical competence not one regarding an examiner’s expertise in various specialties.

The Federal Circuit saw no reason to distinguish between how the presumption applies to “general” medical examiners as compared to “specialists.”  The presumption is that VA has properly chosen an examiner who is qualified to provided competence medical evidence in a particular case absent a challenge by the veteran.

The Federal Circuit, finding that the veteran did not properly challenge the medical examiner’s qualifications before the Board, which is required by presumption of competency, concluded that there was no legal error with the Veterans Court decision.  The Federal Circuit affirmed.
