Decision Assessment Document
 Jandreau v. Nicholson, Jul 3, 2007, 492 F.3d 1372
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that lay evidence can be competent and sufficient to establish a diagnosis of a condition when: (1) A layperson is competent to identify the medical condition, (2) the layperson is reporting a contemporaneous medical diagnosis, or (3) lay testimony describing symptoms at the time supports a later diagnosis by a medical professional.

**Impact on VBA:**

We will be reviewing the current Manual to determine if any changes will be necessary to comply with the Federal Circuit’s holding.  No regulatory revisions will be required.

The Federal Circuit’s holding does not alter well established case law concerning application of lay evidence or VA’s definition of competent lay evidence under [38 C.F.R. § 3.159(a)(1)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=050b37a9df283a27b7b87cb659a59ec0&node=se38.1.3_1159&rgn=div8) and (2).  Lay evidence may be competent and sufficient to establish a diagnosis of a condition under certain circumstances.  However, lay evidence still cannot establish the existence of a disability if identifying the disability’s existence is beyond the competence of lay observation and requires demonstration by competent medical evidence.

The Federal Circuit, in a footnote, provided an informative example that sometimes the lay person will be able to identify a simple condition such as a broken leg, but not, for example, a form of cancer.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served honorably from 1957 to 1959.  He filed for VA disability compensation in 1997, asserting that he had dislocated his right shoulder in service.  The veteran’s service medical records were destroyed in the 1973 National Personnel Records Center (NPRC) fire.  In support of his claim he submitted a statement from a fellow serviceman attesting that he remembered the veteran “being in great pain after dislocating his shoulder while in training.”  Medical reports from 2000 were submitted that showed a current right shoulder disability.  A report from a private physician assessed “right shoulder pain, most likely sequelae of his dislocation of the shoulder.”

The regional office denied the claim.  The Board upheld the denial, reasoning that the private medical report merely recorded the veteran’s assertion of a dislocated shoulder in service without diagnosing a dislocation in service.  The Board rejected the private medical report as “simply information recorded by a medical examiner, unenhanced by medical opinion.”  The Board also rejected the lay statements as not competent to establish the etiology of a current disability.

The Veterans Court concluded that the Board did not err in rejecting the lay evidence and the private medical report, which was premised on a fact that the veteran was not competent to establish that he had a right shoulder dislocation in service because it required a medical diagnosis.  The Veterans Court held that where the determinative issue involves either medical etiology or a medical diagnosis, competent medical evidence is required; however, lay assertions of symptomatology or injury may be sufficient where the determinative issue is not medical in nature.

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that destruction of records while in the government’s custody should result in a relaxed evidentiary standard and that the Veterans Court erred in determining that lay evidence was insufficient if the determinative issue involves a medical diagnosis or etiology.

The Federal Circuit, in rejecting the veteran’s contention that destruction of medical records should result in a relaxed evidentiary standard, indicated that it had considered and rejected the same argument in [*Cromer v.* *Nicholson*, 455 F 3d 1346 (Fed. Cir. 2006)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014894/) that service connection should be presumed when a veteran’s medical records are destroyed while in the government’s custody.

The Federal Circuit agreed with the veteran that the Veterans Court erred in holding that lay evidence was insufficient where the determinative issues involves either medical etiology or a medical diagnosis and that whether the veteran experienced a dislocation of his right shoulder required a medical diagnosis.  The Federal Circuit found the Veterans Court holding to be inconsistent with its decision in [Buchanan v. Nicholson, June 14, 2006, No. 05-7174](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014893/).  The Federal Circuit articulated a framework in determining when lay evidence can be competent and sufficient to identify a medical condition and further held that such determination is a factual issue to be addressed by the Board, rather than a legal issue to be resolved by the Veterans Court.

The Federal Circuit reversed and remanded to the Veterans Court for remand to the Board for consideration under the correct legal standard.
