Decision Assessment Document
 Fountain v. McDonald, Feb 9, 2015, 27 Vet.App. 258
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that 38 C.F.R. § 3.309(a), includes tinnitus, as an organic disease of the nervous system when there is evidence of acoustic trauma.

**Impact on VBA:**

VA implemented the Court’s holding in Manual M21-1 at IV.ii.2.B.2.b. that organic diseases of the nervous system include tinnitus, when there is evidence of acoustic trauma, for the purpose of considering entitlement to presumptive service connection under § 3.309(a).

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served honorably served in the Army from February 1977 to July 1980.  He served as a motor transport operator, driving large engine diesel trucks.  Bilateral hearing loss was diagnosed at separation.  He filed a claim for problems with his hearing at separation and the regional office (RO), in December 1980, granted service connection for bilateral hearing loss with a noncompensable rating.

In January 2009, the veteran filed a claim for service connection for tinnitus, stating that a practice round exploded in front of his face during service, blowing off his helmet, and causing “temporary deafness and considerable tinnitus.”   The veteran underwent a VA examination in May 2009.  Recurrent bilateral tinnitus was diagnosed; however, the audiologist opined that the tinnitus was “less likely as not (less than 50/50 probability) caused by or a result of in-service acoustic trauma.”  The audiologist’s opinion was based upon review of the VA records and service records that were silent for complaint or diagnosis of tinnitus.

The RO denied the claim in May 2009.  The RO conceded exposure to acoustic trauma in service but denied on the basis of finding no nexus between the veteran’s current tinnitus and service.  The veteran, stating that his tinnitus began in service, filed to reopen in November 2010.  The RO denied the claim and the veteran appealed in April 2011, arguing that his November 2010 statement should be considered new and material evidence.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in January 2013, found that the November 2010 and April 2011 statements constituted new and material evidence; however, the Board denied the case on the merits as it determined that the weight of the competent evidence was against a nexus between his current tinnitus and acoustic trauma in service.  The Board concluded that although the veteran was competent to report symptoms as they come to him through his senses, tinnitus is not the type of disorder for which a lay person can provide competent evidence on questions of etiology.  The Board found that the veteran’s statements that his tinnitus began in service were not credible in light of the absence of the complaints of tinnitus symptoms during service and for many years after service, as well as the failure to file a claim for VA benefits for tinnitus before 2009.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board failed to provide adequate reasons or bases for rejecting his testimony concerning the continuity of symptoms since service and erred by categorically excluding his lay statements as incompetent on the question of the etiology of tinnitus.  The veteran also argued that a “common sense reading” of § 3.309(a) requires that tinnitus be included under “other organic diseases of the nervous system.”  The veteran further maintained that VA Training Letter (TL) 10-02 supports this reading as tinnitus is similar to sensorineural hearing loss, which TL 10-02 recognized as an organic disease of the nervous system but labels tinnitus as a mere symptom, in that tinnitus is regarded as condition of the central nervous system.

The Court found that the Secretary’s position excluding tinnitus from an “organic disease of the nervous system,” as expressed in TL 10-02, to be unpersuasive and chose not to adopt it because (1) identifying tinnitus as a symptom is consistent with the Rating Schedule, which identifies tinnitus as a disability subject to compensation payments; (2) TL 10-02 is internally inconsistent as some language superficially appears adverse to the veteran’s position, while other language supports the veteran’s proposed interpretation; (3) TL 10-02 overlooks material points within the medical evidence that ostensibly supports tinnitus as a disease under § 3.309(a); (4) descriptions in VA rulemaking and a VA General Counsel Precedent Opinion 2-03 (May 22, 2003) support defining tinnitus as an organic disease; and (5) post-Walker[2]Board decisions accepting that tinnitus as an organic disease of the nervous system undermines the Secretary’s argument. Note: VA, in clarifying in the 2003 final rule that separate 10 percent evaluations cannot be assigned for bilateral tinnitus under DC 6260, identifies tinnitus as a disability arising in the brain and as a “single disease entity.”  See 68 Fed. Reg. 25, 822 (2003) (Final Rule).  Additionally, the Federal Circuit, in *Walker v. Shinseki*, 708 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2013), held that the continuity of symptomatology provisions of 38 C.F.R. § 3.303(b) are limited to the chronic diseases identified under § 3.309(a).

In applying its holding that tinnitus may be considered an organic disease of the nervous system under § 3.309(a) to the facts of the case, the Court found numerous errors in the Board’s reasons and bases to support its conclusion that the lack of medical evidence of a diagnosis of or treatment for tinnitus until many years after service weighed against the veteran’s claim and his credibility.

The Court found that the Board failed to explain why the veteran would have reasonably have been expected to report his symptoms to medical providers before 2009 or to have filed a VA disability compensation for tinnitus before 2009, as the Board may not rely solely on the fact the veteran did not file, at a particular point, a claim for VA benefits for tinnitus at the same that he filed a claim for another condition.  The Court also held that the Board failed to provide an adequate statement of reasons or bases in light of governing case law as to why tinnitus is not the type of disorder that a lay person can provide competent evidence on regarding questions of etiology. Note: 38 U.S.C. § 1154(b) requires VA to give “due consideration” to “all pertinent medical and lay evidence” when evaluating a claim for disability benefits and the Federal Circuit held in *Davidson v. Shinseki*, 581 F.3d 1313 (Fed. Cir. 2009) rejected VA’s position that “competent medical evidence” is required whenever the issue involves “either medical etiology or a medical diagnosis.”

The Court vacated and remanded the Board’s decision with instructions that the Board (1) cite and correctly apply the relevant caselaw concerning lay statements; (2) if on remand the Board again determines that tinnitus is not of the type of disability for which lay evidence concerning etiology is not competent, the Board must fully consider the nature of tinnitus with its observable subjective symptoms and fully discuss its determination; and (3) consider a theory of secondary service connection for tinnitus based on the veteran’s service-connected bilateral hearing loss consistent with the language specified in TL 10-02 or VA Fast Letter 08-10.
