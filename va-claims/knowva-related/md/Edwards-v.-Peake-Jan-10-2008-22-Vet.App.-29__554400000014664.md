Decision Assessment Document
 Edwards v. Peake, Jan 10, 2008, 22 Vet.App. 29
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that a March 1988 regional office (RO) decision was constitutionally sufficient because the notice was reasonably calculated to provide notice of the denial of the claim and opportunity to present objections.

The March 1988 decision informed the veteran that it could not grant his claim for disability benefits because he failed to report for a VA examination and no further action would be taken unless notification is received of willingness to report for examination.  The notice further informed that additional evidence may be submitted to strengthen his claim, it was in his interest to send new evidence as promptly as possible, and that the RO would carefully consider and inform him if it changes the decision.  The veteran was also informed that he may appeal the decision at any time within one year from the date of the notice.

The Court also held that the information provided in the notice was entirely consistent within the statutory scheme for allowing a claimant the opportunity to reopen a claim at a later date based upon the submission of new and material evidence.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasoning:**

The RO denied the veteran’s claim for service connection for a psychiatric condition in 1981.  In July 1987, he filed to reopen his claim for a nervous condition.  The RO denied the claim in March 1988 because the veteran failed to report for a VA examination.  He filed another claim to reopen on January 14, 1997, which ultimately resulted in a grant of service connection for a psychiatric condition effective the date of the claim to reopen.  A Notice of Disagreement was filed concerning the January 1997 effective date.  Following a Court remand, the Board denied the appeal in June 2004.

The veteran argued violations of his right to constitutional due process before the Court, which included the argument that he was not provided constitutionally sufficient notice of the *res judicata* effect of the failure to appeal the 1988 RO decision or submit new evidence within the one-year period following the decision. Note: Res judicata - rule that a final judgment rendered by a court of competent jurisdiction on the merits is conclusive and constitutes an absolute bar to a subsequent action involving the same claim.*Black’s Law Dictionary, 6th ed., pg. 1305*

The Court, in finding that the notice of the March 1988 decision was sufficient, held that the notice was “reasonably calculated” to provide notice of the denial of the claim and opportunity to present objections.  The Court declined to address the constitutional question of whether the veteran was deprived of a protected property interest.

The Court rejected the veteran’s argument that the RO notice failed to set a time deadline for the submission of new evidence as it clearly advised him of the period in which an appeal must be initiated and promised to provide further information.

The veteran’s argument that the notice lulled him into thinking that the March 1988 decision would remain indefinitely subject to change by the submission of any new evidence was unavailing as the Court found nothing in the notice that was inaccurate or affirmatively misleading with regard to the deadline for appealing or submitting new evidence.

The Court distinguished the veteran’s case from a district court case cited in support of the veteran’s appeal in which it was held that a notice from the Social Security Administration (which informed the claimant that she could file another claim for benefits “at any time” – when, in fact she only had four years to do so) was sufficiently misleading that it violated the claimant’s constitutional rights.  See [*Butland v. Bowen*, 673 F.Supp. 638 (D.Mass.1987)](http://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/673/638/1557365/). In contrast, the RO notice stated that the evidence should be submitted “as promptly as possible,” indicating time was of the essence and also informed the veteran that he had one year to appeal.  Nothing supported the veteran’s assertion that the notice lulled him into failing to act, as there was no demonstration of prejudice.

The Court also rejected the veteran’s argument that due process considerations required more tailored notice because of his mental disability. The Court found that the evidence did not support such argument.  The veteran’s equitable tolling argument was rejected as well, as the Court informed the veteran that the equitable relief sought is solely available at the discretion of the VA Secretary under [38 U.S.C. § 503(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/503).

The Court affirmed the Board’s June 2004 decision.
