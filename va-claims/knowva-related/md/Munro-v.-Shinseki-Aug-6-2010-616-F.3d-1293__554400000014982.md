Decision Assessment Document
 Munro v. Shinseki, Aug 6, 2010, 616 F.3d 1293
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that the implicit denial rule may be applied to terminate the pending status of both formal and informal claims.

Note: The implicit denial rule provides that, in certain circumstances, a claim for benefits will be deemed to have been denied, and thus finally adjudicated, even if VA did not expressly address that claim in its decision. The Federal Circuit held in  Adams v. Shinseki, 568 F.3d 956 (Fed. Cir. 2009) that factors bearing on whether an adjudication that specifically addresses one claim implicitly denied another include whether VA provided sufficient information for a reasonable claimant to know benefits for the asserted disability were denied; the relatedness of the claims, such as whether the disabilities involved are closely related; and the timing of the claims, such as whether the claims were closely associated both in time and in the manner in which they were presented to VA.

The Federal Circuit characterized [38 C.F.R. § 3.157(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=baf1c6cc1b3b15f93f07b583f302ad47&node=se38.1.3_1157&rgn=div8) as only establishing that evidence such as VA medical reports be accepted as informal claims for increased compensation, not that informal claims require separate consideration from formal claims.

The Federal Circuit also held that failure to mention every pending informal claim in a VA decision does not preserve those claims. Acknowledging that VA hospitals regularly treat veterans with service-connected disabilities creates multiple treatment records that may lead to multiple informal claims under § 3.157, the Federal Circuit stated that it was not necessary for the VA to address every one of these informal claims separately, nor is it reasonable to expect them to do so, particularly where the claims relate to the same service-connected disability.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, in September 1992, filed a claim for service connection for “respiratory difficulties.” The regional office (RO), in March 1993, granted service connection for a granuloma of the left lung, but assigned a noncompensable evaluation, as it attributed the veteran’s respiratory difficulties to non-service-connected chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) resulting from 40 years of smoking.

In May 1995, the veteran underwent pulmonary testing at a VA medical center, where a physician diagnosed severe obstructive airways disease. In April 1997, the same VA physician diagnosed severe COPD and stated that the veteran was totally 100 percent permanently disabled.

In September 1997, the veteran filed a claim for increased evaluation for the service-connected lung condition. He requested that all VA medical records from 1993 to 1997 be obtained to support his claim; however, the VAMC only supplied records dating back to the start of 1996. The RO, in November 1997, denied the claim and noted in the decision that it had considered the outpatient records from the Fargo VAMC dated from March 1996 to July 1997. The veteran did not appeal the decision.

On March 31, 2003, the veteran filed another claim for increased evaluation for the lung condition. The RO eventually granted a 60 percent schedular evaluation and total evaluation due to individual unemployability effective March 31, 2003. The veteran contested the effective date, arguing that he was entitled to an earlier effective date for the higher evaluation based on a pending informal claim raised by the April 1997 VA treatment record. The RO denied an earlier effective date because it found that the April 1997 treatment report had been considered as part of the November 1997 RO decision and that the report could not be considered an informal claim because it was related to the COPD. The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) upheld the RO denial.

The Veterans Court, citing [*Williams v. Peake*, 521 F.3d 1348 (Fed. Cir. 2008)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014837/),[[2]](#f2) affirmed the Board’s decision. The Veterans Court held that under the implicit denial rule, even if the May 1995 and April 1997 medical documents were informal claims, the November 1997 RO decision adjudicated them.  Note: The Federal Circuit held in Williams that “a subsequent final adjudication of a claim which is identical to a pending claim that had not been finally adjudicated terminates the pending status of the earlier claim. The later disposition, denying the claim on its merits, also decides that the earlier identical claim must fail.”

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that implicit denial rule does not apply to pending informal claims. He asserted that [§ 3.157(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=baf1c6cc1b3b15f93f07b583f302ad47&node=se38.1.3_1157&rgn=div8) mandated the separate adjudication of informal claims. The veteran also argued that the implicit denial rule was inapplicable because he had not been put on notice that the earlier pending claims had been rejected by an explicit discussion of those earlier claims in a later VA decision.

The Federal Circuit held that there was no basis to distinguish between formal and informal claims in applying the implicit denial rule and that the rule may terminate the pending status of both formal and informal claims.

In rejecting the argument that informal claims were different because the veteran may not have been aware that the records existed, the Federal Circuit observed that VA regulations provide an absolute right for veterans to secure their VA medical records. See [38 C.F.R. § 1.513(b)(1)(ix)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=5b8bccf0ca21ea79e1b2897b2009113c&node=se38.1.1_1513&rgn=div8). In this case, the veteran was aware of the medical records alleged to constitute the informal claims, as he had actually urged the VA to obtain and consider those records in connection with the September 1997 formal claim.

In applying its holding to the facts of the case, the Federal Circuit indicated that here, as in *Williams*, the informal claims raised by the veteran in the May 1995 and April 1997 medical reports and the formal claim filed in September 1997 were identical. Thus, VA’s denial of the later claim was sufficient to notify him that his earlier informal claims were also denied.

The Federal Circuit observed that the RO’s November 1997 decision referenced VA outpatient records dated March 1996 to July 1997 as having been considered, which would have included the medical report upon which the veteran’s April 1997 informal claim was founded. The RO’s consideration of this report was a clear indication that any informal claim raised by the April 1997 report was also denied. Also, the veteran had based his claim on VA outpatient records from 1993 to 1997, which would include the May 1995 treatment record.

The Federal Circuit held that a reasonable claimant would have read the November 1997 RO decision as denying the entire claim for increased benefits based on the records that the veteran alleged were pertinent. Thus, the veteran had multiple reasons to know that any pending informal claims he may have had were denied by the November 1997 RO decision, even though the RO, in its November 1997 decision, did not expressly discuss the May 1995 and April 1997 informal claims.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court judgment.
