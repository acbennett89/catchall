Decision Assessment Document
 Reeves v. Shinseki, Jun 14, 2012, 682 F.3d 988
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that since a combat veteran alleged that he not only had suffered acoustic trauma in service, but that the acoustic trauma led to permanent hearing loss during service, VA was required to apply the [38 U.S.C. § 1154(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1154) presumption to the question of whether he suffered acoustic trauma *and* permanent hearing loss in service.

Note: The statute provides:  “In the case of any veteran who engaged in combat with the enemy in active service with a military, naval, or air organization of the United States during a period of war, campaign, or expedition, the Secretary shall accept as sufficient proof of service-connection of any disease or injury alleged to have been incurred in or aggravated by such service satisfactory lay or other evidence of service incurrence or aggravation of such injury or disease, if consistent with the circumstances, conditions, or hardships of such service, notwithstanding the fact that there is no official record of such incurrence or aggravation in such service, and, to that end, shall resolve every reasonable doubt in favor of the veteran.”

The Federal Circuit held that a surviving spouse’s motion filed in the Federal Circuit to be substituted in a deceased veteran’s appeal pending in that court qualifies as an informal claim for accrued benefits for purposes of establishing standing to be substituted.  The Federal Circuit made clear that VA has no authority to dictate procedures to it that must be followed when allowing a claimant to substitute on a pending appeal.

**Impact on VBA:**

Regulations implementing the substitution statute have been drafted and will soon be published.  Regarding the section 1154(b) holding, the Federal Circuit made clear that the combat presumption must be applied in cases in which a combat veteran alleges exposure to acoustic trauma and permanent hearing loss in service.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served as a heavy mortar crewman during World II, filed a claim for service connection for hearing loss in 1981.  He stated:  “During my service I experienced a hearing loss due to firing [an] 81 mm mortar and [from] treatment of malaria with quinine … My hearing, especially the right ear, has been deteriorating ever since my active duty.”  He submitted a medical opinion from his physician, stating that the veteran was diagnosed with bilateral, nerve-type hearing loss in 1962, which was due to noise exposure or treatment of malaria with quinine.  A statement from an officer he served with during World War II attested that the veteran’s hearing could have been damaged due to long-term exposure to a mortar’s muzzle blast.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in denying the veteran’s claim in 1983, acknowledged exposure to mortar fire and malaria treatment during service.  However, the Board concluded that the initial finding of hearing loss in 1962 was too remote from service to either be related to either mortar fire or malaria treatment in service.

In 2004, the Board reopened the veteran’s claim, citing new evidence from a physician who stated that he found the veteran to have hearing difficulties when treating him from 1946 to 1954.  Another private physician and a VA medical examiner associated the veteran’s hearing loss to in-service noise exposure.  Service connection for hearing loss was granted effective June 13, 2002, the date he filed his application to reopen his claim.

In December 2006, the veteran alleged that the 1983 Board decision contained clear and unmistakable error (CUE) by failing to afford him the combat presumption of[section 1154(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1154).  The Board denied the motion, finding that the application of the combat presumption was unnecessary because VA had accepted that the veteran suffered acoustic trauma in service.  The Board noted that in reaching a conclusion on nexus, the 1983 Board might have relied upon the expertise of a medical doctor, who was one of its three signatory members.  While “highly improper” under current law, such standards were inapplicable to a claim alleging CUE in a 1983 Board decision.

The Veterans Court, in December 2010, affirmed the Board decision, holding that under the law extant in 1983, the Board was not precluded from relying upon its own medical judgment in concluding that the veteran’s hearing loss was not incurred in service.

The veteran filed a notice of appeal with the Federal Circuit in February 2011.  He died three days later.  The surviving spouse filed a motion to be substituted for her husband on appeal.  The motion, which included a copy of the veteran’s death certificate, stated that the surviving spouse was entitled to receive any benefits that were due and payable to her husband as of the date of his death.

The Federal Circuit, in June 2011, granted the request for substitution.  The government argued that the surviving spouse had no standing to be substituted for her deceased husband on appeal because she had not filed a claim for accrued benefits with the VA.

The Federal Circuit rejected the argument.  Noting that the issue of substitution is separate from that of standing, the Federal Circuit stated that, even assuming *arguendo* that standing can be established only if a surviving spouse files an accrued-benefits claim, the claimant’s motion to be substituted for her husband qualifies as an informal claim for accrued benefits.

The Federal Circuit concluded that the claimant’s motion for substitution clearly qualified as an informal claim for accrued benefits, as it evinced intent to obtain the benefits due and payable to her husband as of the date of his death. The Federal Circuit also observed that VA’s current practice is to treat motions for substitution and claims for accrued benefits as “one and the same.”  See VA Fast Letter 10-30 (August 10, 2010).

Although rejecting the government’s argument that a survivor should be required to file an accrued-benefits claim with the VA before being allowed to substitute before the court, the Federal Circuit indicated that it may be appropriate to remand in situations in which the appropriate accrued-benefits beneficiary is in dispute.  However, this was not in issue in the present case as there was no dispute that the surviving spouse was entitled to any benefits due and unpaid to the veteran at the time of his death.

The Federal Circuit observed that the enactment of [section 5121A](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5121A) mandated a shift in its approach to substitution in veterans’ cases, as Congress deemed it “unfair and inefficient” to require an accrued-benefits claimant to restart the claims process after a veteran’s death.  The statute clearly provides that a veteran’s claim is not extinguished by his death, but survives so that it can be processed to completion.  As the premise of previous court decisions was that Congress did not intend for a veteran’s claim to survive his death, because section 5121A rejects that premise, there is no continuing justification for disallowing an accrued-benefits claimant to be substituted for a veteran who dies while his appeal is pending before the Federal Circuit. *See Landicho v. Brown*, 7 Vet.App. 42 (1994) ; [*Zevalkink v. Brown*, 102 F.3d 1236 (Fed. Cir. 1996)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/102/102.F3d.1236.94-7101.95-7012.html).

Regarding the CUE issue, the surviving spouse argued that if the 1983 Board had properly applied section [1154(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1154), it would have found that the veteran injured his hearing during combat and that the Board erred by failing to resolve every reasonable doubt in the veteran’s favor when denying his claim for service-connection benefits.  The government argued that there was no CUE in the 1983 Board decision because the failure to apply section 1154(b) was harmless error.

The Federal Circuit, in rejecting the argument that the failure to apply section 1154(b) was harmless error, found that the government erred by conflating the question of whether the veteran was exposed to acoustic trauma with the issue of whether he suffered permanent hearing loss during service.

The Federal Circuit observed that the veteran had not only asserted he suffered acoustic trauma in service, but that the acoustic trauma led to hearing loss while he was in service.  Although the 1983 Board had accepted that the veteran suffered acoustic trauma, it was also required to apply the section 1154(b) presumption to the separate question of whether he also suffered permanent hearing loss in service.  Thus, the Board erred in concluding that since the veteran’s noise exposure was of record, the combat presumption was unnecessary.

The Federal Circuit elaborated that if the veteran had been able to use the section 1154(b) presumption to show that he incurred a permanent hearing disability in service, it presumably would have been far easier for him to establish that there was a nexus between his military service and his post-service hearing loss.  Instead of attempting to establish that the in-service acoustic trauma led to hearing loss following service, he would have only had to show that the hearing loss incurred in service was a chronic condition that persisted in the years following his active duty.  Furthermore, there was no evidence that the 1983 Board, in denying the appeal, afforded the veteran the benefit of every reasonable doubt in the question of whether his hearing loss was incurred in service, as required by section 1154(b).

The Federal Circuit reversed the Veterans Court decision and remanded with instructions to reevaluate the case using the correct application of section 1154(b) to determine whether the 1983 Board decision contained CUE.
