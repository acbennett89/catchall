**Decision Assessment Document**

**Huerta v. McDonough, April 27, 2021, 34 Vet. App. 76**

**United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)**

**What the case is about:**

The Court considered whether rating criteria pertaining to osteomyelitis under 38 C.F.R. 4.71 and Diagnostic Code 5000 require evidence of an active infection for a 100% rating where there is a diagnosis of chronic osteomyelitis of the pelvis. The Court found that Diagnostic Code 5000 is not ambiguous, and its plain language warrants a 100% disability rating where there is a diagnosis of chronic osteomyelitis of the pelvis.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s ruling concerning DC 5000 will be incorporated in M21-1 V.iii.1.D.1 and 2.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The Veteran served in the U.S. Army from November 1983 to November 1986 and from June 1994 to July 1995. After a car accident in service in 1985 he underwent emergency surgery that included a spine stabilization with wiring and a spinal fusion with a bone graft from his left pelvis.

A year later, he discovered that he had unresolved osteomyelitis at the site of the pelvic bone graft. A course of antibiotics resolved his symptoms for about four years, when the site of the pelvic bone graft broke open. The wound was closed using a muscle flap surgery which revealed that the Veteran had a chronic sinus tract from his iliac bone graft.

About two years later the site of the pelvic bone graft again became infected and the Veteran was placed on antibiotics. His symptoms persisted for 3 months, the infected area was incised and drained and he was given more antibiotics. An x-ray revealed osteomyelitis of his left sacroiliac joint.

After another 2 years, the bone graft site became discolored, painful, and swollen. By the time the Veteran sought treatment, his infection site was discharging. After another incision and drainage and another course of antibiotics within three months the site was no longer draining or exhibiting other symptoms, and the veteran had no fever, chills, sweats or weight loss. Nonetheless, private physicians who were treating the Veteran expressed concern that his pelvic condition required further treatment, and at least one suspected he had continuing osteomyelitis. Though further therapy was recommended, the record for 20 years after the private physicians' recommendation is silent for any treatment for or complaints of symptoms relating to the left pelvis.

The Veteran sought disability compensation, and in August 2016 a VA examiner opined that the Veteran did not have an osteomyelitis infection after his automobile accident and stated that physical examination revealed no signs attributable to osteomyelitis.

In response the Veteran submitted an "Osteomyelitis Disability Benefits Questionnaire" from his treating orthopedic surgeon, who stated that a recent MRI showed foreign bodies in the superficial soft tissues at or near the site of the 1985 iliac bone graft and indicating those foreign bodies caused chronic osteomyelitis. The Veteran’s treating physician opined that the Veteran’s osteomyelitis was chronic, recurrent, and refractory to medical and surgical treatment.

VA then obtained an addendum medical opinion wherein the examiner, upon review of all records concluded that the Veteran did not have current active osteomyelitis and his private physician’s diagnosis of chronic osteomyelitis was incorrect. The VA examiner stated that because the veteran's condition was last symptomatic in 1992, his chronic osteomyelitis was "inactive."

In a supplemental opinion, the Veteran’s treating orthopedic surgeon reiterated that the Veteran had chronic osteomyelitis with a "long history of intractability and debility" that was likely related to the multiple infections experienced. The Veteran’s treating orthopedic surgeon stated that prior episodes of infection were not treated correctly, but that, even with proper treatment, osteomyelitis is severe, persistent, and often incapacitating, and chronic osteomyelitis in adults is more refractory to therapy i.e., difficult to manage.

The Regional Office granted service connection for osteomyelitis and assigned a 10% disability rating.

In a March 2019 decision, the Board of Veterans Appeals (Board) assigned initial staged ratings dating back to 1986, including an initial 100% rating due to active pelvic osteomyelitis, a 20% evaluation based on active infection within the previous 5 years, and a 10% rating because, per the VA examiner, the Veteran had not had an active infection for over 5 years. The Board denied entitlement to a 100% rating for the entire period, finding it “unreasonable to assume that an automatic 100% disability rating is warranted for osteomyelitis, which initially manifests in the 'pelvis, vertebrae' or 'extends into major joints,' but which is later resolved without residual symptoms." The Board noted that VA examiners said the Veteran’s osteomyelitis resolved in 1992 without residuals.

The Court applied rules of regulatory interpretation and ultimately found that the language for assigning a 100% disability rating under DC 5000 is clear and unambiguous and establishes that a diagnosis of chronic osteomyelitis of the pelvis is sufficient basis to warrant a 100% rating. The Court based its determination on the structure and punctuation of the language utilized in DC 5000 and adopted an interpretation of that language specifying that there are five discrete manifestations of osteomyelitis capable of satisfying the 100% rating.

Specifically, the Court determined that a 100 percent disability evaluation is warranted under DC 5000 for osteomyelitis: (1) Of the pelvis; (2) of the vertebrae; (3) extending into the major joints; (4) with multiple localization; or (5) with long history of intractability and debility, anemia, amyloid liver changes, or other continuous constitutional symptoms.

Based on that determination, the Court concluded that the Board committed legal error in determining that the Veteran's chronic pelvic osteomyelitis did not entitle him to a 100% disability rating. In light of that error the Court did not address the adequacy of any contested VA and private medical opinions. The Court therefore REVERSED the Board's determination that a 100% disability rating under DC 5000 was not warranted for the entire period on appeal.
