Decision Assessment Document
 Jones v. Shinseki, Mar 25, 2010, 23 Vet.App. 382
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that VA may only rely upon a medical examiner’s conclusion that an etiology opinion would be speculative if the examiner explained the basis for such an opinion or the basis is otherwise apparent in VA’s review of the evidence.

The Court held that VA, to fulfill its duty to assist, is not required to perform repetitive medical examinations until it obtains a conclusive opinion or formally declares that further examinations would be futile; however, it must be clear from the examiner’s opinion and/or VA’s analysis of the record that the examiner has not used the phrase “without resort to mere speculation” as a substitute for the full consideration of all pertinent and available medical facts to which a claimant is entitled.  If review of the record leaves this issue in doubt, it is the duty of the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) to remand for further development.

The Court further held that the examiner may also have an obligation to conduct research in the medical literature depending on the evidence in the record at the time of examination.  The examiner should also precisely identify what facts cannot be determined if unable to give an etiology opinion without resorting to speculation.

**Impact on VBA:**

VBA will revise Manual, [M21-MR III.iv.3.A.9.e](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000015809/) and [III.iv.4.A.1.b](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014194/) to comply with the Court’s decision.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served from 1964 to 1967 to include service in Vietnam, filed a claim for service connection for multiple disabilities to include hearing loss and “busted ear drums” in October 2002, asserting hearing problems from exposure to small arms, mortar, and artillery fire during service.  Service connection for diabetes mellitus and right ear disabilities, to include hearing loss, were eventually granted.  Service connection for left ear hearing loss was denied.  The regional office (RO), in August 2006, denied the veteran’s claim for service connection for erectile dysfunction due to medication for diabetes mellitus.  The veteran appealed the denials of service connection for hearing loss and erectile dysfunction and underwent VA examinations for both conditions.

In a May 2006 VA audiology examination, the examiner, in addressing the etiology of the veteran’s left ear hearing loss stated there was “insufficient information to resolve the etiology and onset of the left ear hearing loss without resorting to speculation.”  The examiner considered the veteran’s report of tinnitus, noting a report of symptoms beginning in 1965; however, he stated during a 2001 VA examination that he had tinnitus “as long as he could remember.”  The examiner stated that with two different accounts of onset, it would be impossible to resolve the etiology of the tinnitus without resort to speculation; however, the left ear hearing loss and tinnitus were more likely than not related to one or more common factors.

Erectile dysfunction was diagnosed during a January 2007 VA genitourinary examination.  In addressing the question of etiology, the examiner stated that it could not be resolved “without resort to mere speculation.”

The Board, in its September 2007 decision, denied service connection for hearing loss and erectile dysfunction.  In considering both issues, the Board noted the inability of the VA medical examiners to opine on the etiology issue without resort to speculation.

The veteran argued before the Court that VA had not fulfilled its duty to assist in providing a medical examination when the examination report fails to proffer an opinion on the etiology of a disability.  The veteran also argued that [38 U.S.C. § 5103A(d)(2)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) requires VA to seek additional opinions until either VA obtains an opinion that takes a definitive position on the etiology question or VA formally concludes that no further reexaminations should be undertaken because “no reasonable possibility exists that such assistance would aid in substantiating the claim.”

The Court held that the Board may only rely upon a medical opinion articulating that an opinion cannot be given without resorting to speculation if the examiner explained the basis for rendering such opinion or the basis is otherwise apparent in the Board’s review of the evidence.  The Court stated that the phrase “without resort to mere speculation” cannot become a mantra that inhibits the careful consideration to which each claimant’s case is entitled.  The Court stated that the phrase should reflect the limitation of knowledge in the medical community at large and not of a particular examiner.

The Court stated that it must be clear that the inability to opine on questions of diagnosis and etiology is not the first impression of an uninformed examiner, but an assessment arrived at after all due diligence in seeking relevant medical information that may have bearing on the requested opinion.  The Court held that it would be inappropriate for VA to demand a conclusive opinion from a physician whose evaluation of the “procurable and assembled” information prevents the rendering of such an opinion.  When an examiner has done all that reasonably should be done to become informed about a case and the inability to render a requested opinion is adequately explained by the examiner or otherwise apparent in the Board’s review of the evidence, there is nothing further to be obtained from that particular examiner.

In applying its holding to the case at hand, the Court found the examiner’s opinion regarding etiology of the left ear hearing loss to be ambiguous.  The reasoning set up a perceived credibility issue relating to both tinnitus and hearing loss, which required a response by the Board.  The Court remanded the matter for the Board to consider entitlement to service connection for tinnitus and to return the case to the examiner for a medical etiology opinion on tinnitus based on the Board’s fact finding as to which of the purportedly contradictory accounts the examiner should use as the basis of his opinion.  Because the examiner stated that the tinnitus and the left ear hearing disability were as likely as not related to the same factor(s), the left ear hearing disability claim was also remanded.

The Court, in addressing the erectile dysfunction claim, found the explanation for the January 2007 opinion – “cannot resolve issue without resolve to mere speculation” – to have internal consistencies and failed to articulate a reasoned explanation as to why no opinion was possible without resort to speculation.  The Court also found that the Board’s reasons merely reiterated the examination findings and rationale without further elaboration; thus, the Board erred in accepting the examiner’s inconclusive opinion and should have remanded for clarification and perhaps for further medical opinions.

The Court then set aside the September 2007 Board decision and remanded the service connection claim for erectile dysfunction to the Board for clarification and possible further development.
