Decision Assessment Document
 Menegassi v. Shinseki, Apr 21, 2011, 638 F.3d 1379
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that under [38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(5)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=d18fb6c420cf6e46654a61cf0280ba8f&node=se38.1.3_1304&rgn=div8), medical opinion evidence may be submitted for use in determining whether the occurrence of a stressor is corroborated.

The Federal Circuit concluded that § 3.304(f)(5) allows a veteran claiming posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) from an in-service personal assault to submit evidence other than in-service medical records to corroborate the occurrence of a stressor, to include medical opinion evidence.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served from 1982 to 1989, filed a VA compensation claim in January 2001, alleging that she suffers from PTSD due to an alleged in-service sexual assault.  A VA medical examiner diagnosed the veteran with PTSD and opined that it was more likely than not that sexual trauma during service caused her PTSD based on the accounts of the alleged incident relayed by her to the VA examiner.  The regional office denied the claim.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in February 2008, determined that there was no evidence of a reported sexual assault or behavioral changes from the in-service medical or personnel records, or any other records contemporaneous to the veteran’s service.  The Board, pursuant to [§ 3.304(f)(5)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=d18fb6c420cf6e46654a61cf0280ba8f&node=se38.1.3_1304&rgn=div8), also considered post-service evidence to include a lay statement, notes from a treatment program, and the VA medical examiner’s opinion.  The Board determined that a preponderance of the evidence was against a finding of verification of the occurrence of the alleged in-service personal and denied the claim for service connection for PTSD.

The Veterans Court, in February 2010, affirmed the Board’s decision.  The Veterans Court stated that an opinion by a mental health professional based on a post-service examination of the veteran cannot be used to establish the occurrence of a stressor.

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that the Veterans Court erred as a matter of law by stating that a post-service examination report by a mental health professional cannot be used to establish the occurrence of a stressor related to a PTSD claim stemming from an in-service personal assault.  The Government conceded error by the Veterans Court but contended that the error was harmless.

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court erred in stating that a medical opinion based on a post-service examination cannot be used to establish the occurrence of a stressor.  The Federal Circuit observed that § 3.304(f)(5) specifically designates, and VA’s interpretation contemplates, that medical opinion evidence be submitted in corroborating a stressor.  Note: [38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(5)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=d18fb6c420cf6e46654a61cf0280ba8f&node=se38.1.3_1304&rgn=div8) provides that examples of evidence other than service records to corroborate a veteran’s account of an in-service personal assault “include but, are not limited to:  records from … mental health counseling centers, hospitals, or physicians.”

The Federal Circuit concluded that it had jurisdiction to determine whether or not the error of the Veterans Court was harmless, finding that whether the Board – in its analysis – considered the evidence as defined by the scope of [§ 3.304(f)(5)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=d18fb6c420cf6e46654a61cf0280ba8f&node=se38.1.3_1304&rgn=div8) to be a purely legal issue that required no determination of fact or application of facts to law.  Note: The Federal Circuit held in [*D’Amico v. West*, 209 F.3d 1322, 1327 (Fed. Cir. 2000)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/209/209.F3d.1322.99-7110.html), that a harmless error analysis of the Veterans Court’s judgment cannot be conducted when the analysis would require “fact-finding and/or application of law to fact.”

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court’s error was harmless, as the Veterans Court’s opinion made it clear that the Board correctly interpreted the requirement of § 3.304(f)(5) and considered all available evidence relevant to the veteran’s attempt to corroborate the occurrence of the alleged stressor.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court decision, holding that the Veterans Court’s error was harmless because the Board considered the record evidence under a correct interpretation of the legal standard imposed by § 3.304(f)(5).
