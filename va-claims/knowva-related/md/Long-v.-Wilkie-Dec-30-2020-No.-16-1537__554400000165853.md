Decision Assessment Document
Long v. Wilkie, Dec 30, 2020, No. 16-1537
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that exceptionality remains the touchstone in determining whether extra-schedular consideration is warranted under 38 C.F.R. 3.321(b)(1) and the first step of Thun v. Peake, 22 Vet.App. 111 (2008) is only satisfied when a veteran presents symptoms that are truly unusual or exceptional.

The Court held that because the determination of whether exceptional symptomatology is fact-bound and highly contextual, Thun’s first step should be approached as a totality of the factors inquiry rather than as a mechanical formula.  Thun’s first step centers on whether the veteran’s disability picture as a whole – the symptomatology in total – presents an impairment that is so exceptional that the rating schedule is not capable of assessing it in the first instance.  The Court explained that the inquiry is not reducible to a mere comparison between symptoms and the diagnostic criteria but requires a reasoned assessment of the veteran’s full disability picture and the capacity of the rating schedule to evaluate such.

(The first step of Thun requires adjudicators to compare the level of severity and symptomatology of the veteran’s service-connected disability with the established criteria found in the rating schedule for that disability.  If the criteria reasonably describe the veteran’s disability level and symptomatology, then the veteran’s disability picture is contemplated by the rating schedule and the schedular evaluation is adequate and no referral is required).

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in the Manual M21-1 under III.iv.6.B.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

In 2009, the veteran was awarded service connection for hearing loss.  A noncompensable evaluation was assigned.  The veteran appealed the evaluation but did not contest the schedular noncompensable evaluation.  Instead, the veteran argued for an extra-schedular evaluation, alleging that he experienced certain functional effects of hearing loss not contemplated by the schedular rating criteria.

The veteran asserted that the mechanical nature of the hearing loss criteria rendered it inadequate because it failed to account for such functional effects of hearing loss to include symptoms of anxiety and depression, decreased self-esteem and personal satisfaction, problems with speech discrimination that were not improved by hearing aids, interference with ability to work with students, increased difficulty writing lesson plans and preparing for classes, and ear pain resulting from use of hearing aids.  No psychiatric disorder was diagnosed.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in its March 2016 decision, referenced evidence showing that the veteran’s mental health issues were less likely than not related to hearing loss.  The Board found that the veteran was not competent as a lay person to offer an opinion as to the etiology of his depression and anxiety.  The Board noted the veteran’s claim of inability to work with students, write lesson plans, and prepare for classes, as well as referencing VA examiners notations that the veteran had difficulty in meeting, people had to repeat themselves to him, and the clinical conclusion that the veteran needed hearing aids but would not otherwise be limited in a work environment.  However, the Board ultimately concluded that the evidence does not present an unusual or exceptional disability picture that rendered the regular rating standards as impractical because the rating criteria reasonably described his disability level and symptomatology.  The Board found no indication that the veteran lost considerable time at work or experienced frequent hospitalizations due to his hearing loss.  Furthermore, the Bound found that any interference with his ability to work with his students or prepare lesson plans was proportional to the severity of his hearing loss.

The veteran argued before the Veterans Court that the Board erred in not referring his case for extra-schedular consideration because the diagnostic criteria for hearing loss contained in the rating schedule fails to account for his varied symptoms and effects.

The Court, in rejecting the veteran’s arguments, observed that it has consistently declined to treat Thun’s first step as a mechanical test that is satisfied whenever a veteran presents a symptom not expressly listed in the diagnostic code.  The Court provided the following factors for consideration as guidance principles in determining whether symptomatology is exceptional under 3.321(b)(1):

Sole focus of Thun’s first step is on the ability of the rating schedule to evaluate any impairment manifested by the veteran’s symptomatology.  Symptomatology is exceptional under § 3.321(b)(1) when it is of such nature or severity that conventional ratings tools are not adequate to evaluate it properly.  Extra-schedular consideration is not available to claims that are properly rated but ultimately denied under the schedular ratings, but only applies after conventional rating tools prove inadequate to evaluate a veteran’s symptomatology in the first place due to its exceptional nature.

Functional impairments serve as the operative focus of Thun’s first step.

A veteran’s complaints about various psychological or emotional difficulties that may be associated with a separate disability generally would not constitute a valid diagnosis of a mental disorder.  See Martinez-Bodon v. Wilkie, 32 Vet.App. 393 (2020).  Alleged psychological conditions that are not compensable in the first instance due to the lack of a formal diagnosis do not warrant extra-schedular consideration as this would be tantamount to a backdoor means to obtain compensation for conditions the rating schedule intends to exclude.

Extra-schedular consideration does not provide an exception to the rule that a disability is not compensable if it cannot be attributed to an injury or disease incurred or aggravated in the line of duty.

A decision addressing whether to refer for extra-schedular consideration is to be rendered only if raised by the veteran or reasonably raised by the evidence of record.  The requirement to assess whether a veteran’s symptomatology is exceptional does not require VA to raise and reject all possible theories of entitlement.

In applying its holding to the merits of the veteran’s case, the Court noted that it had previously held in Doucette v. Shulkin, 28 Vet.App. 366 (2016), that “the rating criteria for hearing loss contemplate the functional effects of decreased hearing and difficulty understanding speech in an everyday work environment, as these are precisely the effects that VA’s audiometric tests are designed to measure.”  Therefore, the Court held that there was no error in the Board decision because the veteran’s hearing-related symptoms are clearly covered by Doucette.

In addressing the veteran’s claim that he has reduced self-esteem and personal satisfaction, anxiety, and depression related to hearing loss that warrants extra-schedular consideration, the Court found that the Board committed no error in denying referral for extra-schedular consideration, as there was no indication that these conditions could not be rated by conventional means.  The Court noted that such symptoms are considered under regular rating criteria for mental disorders contained in 38 C.F.R. 4.130.  Therefore, the conditions could not be considered exceptional.  Furthermore, the Court found no evidence linking the veteran’s anxiety and depression to hearing loss.

The Court also rejected the veteran’s assertion that his ear pain warrants referral for extra-schedular consideration, as no competent evidence has been presented to associate his pain with his hearing loss.  Thus, without sufficient evidence that the unusual functional impairment is attributed to the hearing loss, extra-schedular consideration is foreclosed.

Lastly, the Court found that the veteran’s failure to address the second step of Thun – whether the disability picture evinces related factors such as marked interference with employment or frequent periods of hospitalization – as being fatal to his appeal, as he cannot prevail without satisfying Thun’s second step.

The Veterans Court affirmed the Board’s decision.
