Decision Assessment Document
 Alpough v. Nicholson, Jun 18, 2007, 490 F.3d 1352
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that since separation by mutual consent generally does not constitute desertion, a claimant is exempted from the continuous cohabitation requirement under [38 C.F.R. § 3.53(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=49ba3f45b2ac2fa1ab74c8092a4b6006&node=se38.1.3_153&rgn=div8) for the purpose of establishing recognition as the surviving spouse for death benefits in such cases.  The Federal Circuit held that under a proper interpretation of [38 U.S.C. § 101(3)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/101), a spouse can qualify as a surviving spouse if a separation was procured by the veteran, even if there was no misconduct by the veteran.  Therefore, under a proper interpretation of section 3.53(b), a separation by mutual agreement, without an intent to desert, does not break the continuity of cohabitation.

The Federal Circuit stated that a separation by mutual consent would constitute desertion if resulting from misconduct or communication of a definite intent to end the marriage by the surviving spouse; thus, breaking the continuity of cohabitation and precluding surviving spouse recognition.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran married Louise in 1960 and they had two children.  In 1970, the Alpoughs separated.  The veteran filed a claim for service connection for stomach cancer in October 1972 and indicated that he had been separated from Louise since 1970 because he “could not get along” with her.  He died two months later from stomach cancer.

Louise filed a claim for dependency and indemnity compensation (DIC) benefits and indicated in the application that she had not lived continuously with the veteran from marriage until death and they separated because “they could not get along as husband and wife.”  The regional office denied the appellant’s claim in September 1973, concluding that she was not the legal widow of the veteran as they mutually agreed to live apart and there was no intention of their resuming relationship as husband and wife.

The appellant made numerous attempts to reopen her claim to include submitting evidence that the reason for separation was the veteran’s illness, not incompatibility.  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) reopened the claim, but denied on the merits, concluding that the new evidence was not as probative as contemporaneous statements that the separation was due to mutual incompatibility.  The Veterans Court upheld the Board denial, holding that the appellant cannot prevail because the law and the regulation only make an exception for a separation caused by the veteran’s misconduct.

The appellant argued before the Federal Circuit that the Veterans Court misinterpreted 38 U.S.C. § 101(3) and 38 C.F.R. § 3.53(b) by requiring her to prove “misconduct” on the part of her spouse in order to be accorded surviving spouse status.

The Federal Circuit determined that the Veterans Court holding was contrary to both section 101(3) and § 3.53(b).  The Federal Circuit stated that the key determination is whether the reason for separation did or did not show intent on the part of the surviving spouse to desert the veteran.  Because “desertion” is not defined in the regulation, the Federal Circuit relied upon state supreme courts precedents and the family law definition of that term to conclude that desertion refers to an abandonment without consent and that mere separation is not a desertion except where the surviving spouse obtained consent through misconduct or a communicated intent to end the marriage.

The Federal Circuit concluded that under [38 U.S.C. § 101(3)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/101), a claimant can qualify as a surviving spouse if a separation was procured by the veteran even if there was no misconduct by the veteran.  Furthermore, under [§ 3.53(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=49ba3f45b2ac2fa1ab74c8092a4b6006&node=se38.1.3_153&rgn=div8), a separation by mutual agreement, without intent to desert, does not break the continuity of cohabitation.

The Federal Circuit vacated the Veterans Court decision and remanded the case for consideration under the proper interpretations.
