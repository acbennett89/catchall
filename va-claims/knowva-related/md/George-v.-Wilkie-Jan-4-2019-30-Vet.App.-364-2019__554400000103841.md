Decision Assessment Document
George v. Wilkie, Jan 4, 2019, 30 Vet.App. 364 (2019)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that applying a statute or regulation as it was interpreted and understood at the time a prior final decision is rendered does not become a clear and unmistakable error (CUE) by virtue of a subsequent interpretation of the statute or regulation by the Court or Federal Circuit.

Accordingly, the Court held that the Federal Circuit’s decision in Wagner v. Principi, 370 F.3d 1089 (Fed. Cir. 2004), which held that 38 U.S.C. 1111 requires clear and unmistakable evidence that a condition both existed prior to service and was not aggravated during service, does not apply retroactively to a Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) decision in 1977.  The Board, in its 1977 decision denying the claim for service connection for schizophrenia on the basis of not finding that the condition preexisted service and was not aggravated by service, was not required to find clear and unmistakable evidence that the disability was not aggravated by service under the version of 38 C.F.R. 3.304(b) in effect at that time.

The Court further held that because the veteran’s case was not open for direct review, as it was finally decided in 1977, the judicial interpretation of Wagner does not apply retroactively.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in the Manual M21-1 under III.iv.2.B.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served from June to September 1975, underwent a medical evaluation board in August 1975, which confirmed a diagnosis of schizophrenia and found that it preexisted service and was aggravated by service.  He was referred to a physical evaluation board, which found that the condition preexisted service, but was not aggravated by service, and was separated from service.  The veteran filed for service connection for schizophrenia in December 1975.

The regional office (RO), in May 1976 denied the claim, finding that the condition preexisted service and there was an acute exacerbation but no permanent aggravation during service.  The Board, in September 1977, upheld the RO decision on the basis that the schizophrenia preexisted and was not aggravated by service.

In December 2014, the veteran filed a motion to revise the 1977 Board decision on the basis of CUE.  The veteran alleged that the Board failed to correctly apply 38 U.S.C. 311 (the forerunner to section 1111, which contains identical language to its predecessor), as VA did not rebut the presumption of a sound condition with clear and unmistakable evidence that the condition was not aggravated by service.

The Board, in March 2016, found no CUE in the September 1977 Board decision, noting that the 1977 Board, in conducting a presumption of soundness analysis under 3.304(b) (1977) was not required to find clear and unmistakable evidence that the disability was not aggravated by service.  The Board acknowledged that Wagner held that section 1111 requires clear and unmistakable evidence that a condition both existed prior to service and was not aggravated by service to rebut the presumption of sound; however, the Board stated that judicial decisions that formulate new interpretations of the law subsequent to a VA decision cannot be the basis of a valid CUE claim.  Relying on Jordan v. Nicholson, 401 F.3d 1296 (Fed. Cir. 2005), the Board explained that interpretation of section 1111 articulated in Wagner does not have retroactive application in a CUE case.

The Board conceded that the 1977 Board did not discuss the presumption of soundness statute, section 311 (1977), or its implementing regulation, 3.304(b) (1977), or explain how there was clear and unmistakable evidence that the veteran’s condition existed prior to service and was not aggravated by service.  However, the Board found the error to not be outcome determinative because the 1977 Board still considered all the relevant evidence of record in its decision.  The Board concluded that the veteran’s CUE allegation was simply a disagreement with how the 1977 Board weighed the evidence, which does not constitute a CUE.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred in March 2016 in finding that, under 3.304(b) (1977), the 1977 Board was not required to find clear and unmistakable evidence that the disability was not aggravated by service.  He contended that the CUE motion specifically challenged the application of section 311, a statute, and not the VA regulation in effect in 1977.  The veteran further indicated that when a court interprets a statue, its interpretation is a statement of what the law has always been, which renders the Board’s dismissal of Wagner improper.

The Court rejected the veteran’s arguments, finding that the Federal Circuit’s announcement in Wagner in 2004 of what section 1111 means cannot defeat the finality of a 1977 Board decision.  The Court explained that this is because consideration of CUE requires that application of the law as it was understood at the time of the 1977 decision, and Wagner does not change how section 311 (now section 1111) was interpreted or understood before it was issued.  The Court noted that when VA proposed the CUE regulation, VA anticipated that an interpretation of a statute or regulation could, in light of future interpretations – whether by the General Counsel or a court – be viewed as erroneous and had indicated in the May 19, 1998, proposed rule that it would not be the kind of error required for CUE.

In applying its holding to the facts of the case, the Court stated that, in 1977, the Board was required to apply the law as it existed at that time, including § 3.304(b), which required the rebuttal of the presumption of soundness with only clear and unmistakable evidence that an injury or disease existed before service.  Thus, the Court indicated that it is was unclear how the Board could have ignored the regulation or why the Board would have been required to find clear and unmistakable evidence of aggravation in 1977.  This regulatory interpretation of soundness, requiring only clear and unmistakable evidence that a disability preexisted service to rebut the presumption of soundness, prevailed until 2003 when VA’s General Counsel issued Precedent Opinion 3-2003 holding that VA must show clear and unmistakable evidence of a pre-service disability and no aggravation to rebut the presumption of soundness.

The Court noted that while the Federal Circuit’s interpretation of the presumption of soundness statute in Wagner set forth what the statute had always meant, it was not the interpretation or understanding of the statute before the issuance of Wagner.  Because it found that Wagner did not retroactively apply to final decisions, the Court concluded that the 2016 correctly stated the law as it existed in 1977.

The Court also determined that because the veteran’s case was not open for direct review, as a final decision had been issued in 1977, the judicial interpretation of Wagner does not apply retroactively.  Thus, Wagner’s interpretation of the presumption of soundness statute issued in 2004 did not change how the law was interpreted or understood when the Board issued its final decision in September 1977.

The Court concluded that the veteran had not demonstrated that the 2016 Board decision that the 1977 Board decision did not contain CUE was arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.

The Court also concluded that the veteran failed to carry his burden of demonstrating that the 2016 Board erred in concluding that the 1977 Board’s failure to cite the relevant statutory and regulatory provisions and to explain how the evidence of record rebutted both prongs of the presumption of soundness was not outcome determinative.  Without evidence of a manifestly changed outcome, the veteran had not demonstrated that the 2016 Board’s finding of no CUE in the 1977 Board decision to be arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with the law.

The Court affirmed the Board’s March 2016 decision.
