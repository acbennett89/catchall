Decision Assessment Document
 Mangham v. Shinseki, Nov 16, 2009, 23 Vet.App. 284
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that a claim for disability compensation under [38 U.S.C. § 1151](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1151) on the basis of domiciliary care must fail as a matter of law as domiciliary care is not a component of VA hospital care, medical or surgical treatment, or an examination that is contemplated under section 1151.  The Court rejected the veteran’s argument that residing in a VA-run domiciliary is, in and of itself, hospital care, or medical or surgical treatment, under section 1151.

The Court also held that in view of the 1996 amendments to section 1151 by [Public Law 104-204](http://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/PLAW-104publ204/html/PLAW-104publ204.htm), a coincidental event during a VA hospitalization cannot lead to recovery under section 1151.  The threshold question is now whether VA’s direct actions caused harm.  The mere fact that a claimant is harmed by an event that occurs coincidentally with VA hospital care, medical or surgical treatment, or an examination is not sufficient to establish causation – and, therefore, to receive benefits – under section 1151.

**I**mpact** on VBA:**

The Manual M21-MR is being reviewed to determine if any changes are required by the Court’s opinion.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran was treated in a VA domiciliary from September through November 2001.  While at lunch in the domiciliary cafeteria in October 2001, the veteran witnessed another domiciliary resident shoot and wound three people, one mortally.  In April 2004, the veteran filed a claim for compensation under section 1151 for mental distress as a result of witnessing the October 2001 shootings.

The regional office denied compensation under section 1151 on the basis that the shooting in the domiciliary cafeteria was not part of any treatment for the veteran.  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in February 2007, determined that the October 2001 shootings in the domiciliary cafeteria did not occur during qualifying hospital care, medical or surgical treatment or an examination by a VA employee or at a VA facility, despite the veteran’s assertions that he was a resident of the domiciliary and received medical treatment during the course of his residency.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred in finding that his residency in a VA domiciliary care facility did not constitute medical care under section 1151.  He also contended that VA had not fulfilled its duty to assist with regard to releasing the “Root Cause Analysis” (RCA) report pertaining to the October 2001 shooting incident.

The Court rejected the veteran’s argument that residing in a VA domiciliary is per se medical treatment and anything occurring in the facility – to include a shooting – is part of the treatment for showing causation under [section 1151](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1151).  The Court explained that the argument failed because the statute governing domiciliary care – [38 U.S.C. § 1701](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1701) – does not establish it as a medical service that is contemplated under section 1151.  The Court also noted that in the 1996 revisions to section 1151, Congress had repudiated the notion that a coincidental event occurring during “hospitalization” could lead to recovery under section 1151, as it had deliberately replaced “hospitalization” with “hospital care.”

The Court also rejected the veteran’s duty to assist argument, finding the contents of the RCA report to be irrelevant, since it concluded that the section 1151 claim fails as a matter of law.  Therefore, the report cannot demonstrate that the shooting was part of VA’s provision of hospital care, medical or surgical treatment, or examination.

The Court affirmed the Board’s February 2007 decision.
