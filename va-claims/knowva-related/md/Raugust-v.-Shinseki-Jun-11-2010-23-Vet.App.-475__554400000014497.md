Decision Assessment Document
 Raugust v. Shinseki, Jun 11, 2010, 23 Vet.App. 475
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the minimum service requirement for eligibility for basic VA medical care under [38 U.S.C. § 5303A](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5303A) and [38 C.F.R. § 3.12a](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=835653f3036d142d0c6b61ca07979f41&node=se38.1.3_112a&rgn=div8) does not violate the Fifth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause because a rational basis exists for the requirement.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served from October 1984 to April 1986.  He was discharged for “misconduct – pattern of misconduct” and his service was characterized as under honorable conditions.  The regional office, in 2005, denied the veteran’s application for medical benefits, concluding that he was not entitled to such benefits because he had less than 24 months of continuous active military service after September 1980, nor did he fit into any exception under [§ 3.12a(d)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=835653f3036d142d0c6b61ca07979f41&node=se38.1.3_112a&rgn=div8).

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in March 2008, denied the claim as matter of law as the veteran did not have 24 months of continuous active military service.  The Board also concluded that it was unnecessary to review VA’s compliance with its duty to assist the veteran with obtaining evidence for his claim pursuant to the Veterans Claims Assistance Act (VCAA) because “no reasonable possibility exists that would aid in substantiating this claim.”  Thus, any VCAA notice deficiencies were moot.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred in finding that VA did not have a duty to obtain further military records beyond his DD-214, enlistment contract, and portions of his enlistment physical because additional records may have addressed some of the exclusions to the 24-month minimum service requirement. He also argued that the minimum service requirement to qualify for VA medical benefits was unconstitutional, as it violated the Fifth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause.

Note: Exclusions to the minimum service requirement are:  (1) Early out or hardship discharge; (2) discharge for a service-connected disability or having a service-connected disability at the time of discharge, which, in medical judgment would have justified a discharge; (3) person with a compensable service-connected disability; (4) to the provision of a benefit for or in connection with a service-connected disability, condition, or death; and (5) to VA insurance benefits.

The Court indicated that it was conceivable that additional military records would establish that one of the exceptions of the 24-month minimum service required applied; however, there was no “specific reason to believe” that the record would contain such information and the veteran had not made a specific allegation that an exception would be established if the records were obtained.  The Court held that without a credible reason to believe that obtaining the additional records would tend to establish an exception to the 24-month service requirement, the Board did not commit clear error in concluding that there was no need to obtain additional records.

In reviewing the constitutional issue, the Court noted that it is well established that the rational basis standard of review is used when legislation involving governmental payment of monetary benefits is challenged on equal protection grounds as violating the Fifth Amendment.  Under the rational standard of review, the Court will uphold a statute unless it is “patently arbitrary and irrational.”  The Court determined that VA medical care pursuant to section 5303A is a social welfare program subject to the rational basis standard of review.

The Court reviewed the legislative history of [10 U.S.C. § 977](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/977) (precursor to current section [5303A](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5303A)), which provided evidence that Congress had expressed a serious concern regarding the high level of attrition of first term military personnel, much of which occurred during the first six months of active duty, and declared that the eligibility for benefits at the six month point provided an incentive for attrition.

The Court found that the legislative history clearly showed that the purpose of the 24-month minimum service requirement was to attempt to reduce attrition among new members of the military.  Thus, the rational basis for creating the eligibility requirement was to encourage new recruits to remain in the military by withholding benefits to those who choose to leave early.

The Court found that it was neither patently arbitrary nor irrational and does not violate the Fifth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause.

The Court affirmed the Board’s decision.
