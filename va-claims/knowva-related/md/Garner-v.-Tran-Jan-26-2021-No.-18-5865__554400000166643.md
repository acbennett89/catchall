Decision Assessment Document
Garner v. Tran, Jan 26, 2021, No. 18-5865
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that where the record reflects only incidental references to a veteran’s weight or weight gain, the evidence of record is insufficient to reasonably raise the issue of obesity as an intermediate step for secondary service connection.  The Court held that to reasonably raise the issue, there must be some evidence in the record which draws an association or suggests a relationship between the veteran’s obesity, or weight gain resulting in obesity, and a service-connected disability.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in the M21-1 Manual under III.iv.5.B.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served in the Air Force from June 1972 to May 1992, was awarded service connection for various musculoskeletal disabilities by the regional office (RO) in December 2011.  In May 2014, the RO awarded service connection for a mood disorder as secondary to the veteran’s service-connected arthritis of the left shoulder.

The record reveals that the veteran sought treatment in November 2013 for a sleep disorder and was diagnosed with mild obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and poor sleep efficiency.  He filed a claim for service connection for OSA, alleging that the condition was secondary to his service-connected mood disorder.  The RO denied the claim in June 2014.  The veteran appealed the decision.

The veteran underwent a VA examination in September 2015 to address the OSA.  The examiner rendered a diagnosis of OSA and opined that the condition was less likely than not proximately due to or the result of a service-connected condition.  The examiner indicated that extensive review of the medical literature revealed that a mood disorder is not one of the known risk factors for OSA.  The examiner identified the veteran’s risk factors for OSA to include his age, male gender, and obesity.

VA mental health records from November 2015 show a diagnosis of unspecified mental health disorder.  It was noted that the veteran’s pain in multiple joints, hypertension, and OSA were relevant to the understanding and management of his mental health disorder.  In August 2016, a physician indicated that the veteran’s pain interfered with his normal activities of daily living and resulted in decreased physical capacity.  The veteran’s body mass index (BMI) was documented as 31, which is considered obese.  In June 2017, the veteran stated that the pain related to his knees, shoulders, wrists, and back impacted his mood on a daily basis.  The physician indicated that the veteran’s diminished concentration and lack of interest in doing anything were compounded by his physical limitations.

The veteran underwent another VA examination in November 2017.  The examiner opined that his OSA was less likely than not aggravated beyond its normal progression by his service-connected mood disorder.  The examiner noted that while the veteran’s mood disorder contributes to his insomnia, it did not affect the etiology or progression of OSA, which is due to collapse of oropharyngeal tissue.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeal (Board), in August 2018, denied the claim for service connection for OSA, as it concluded that there was no link between the veteran’s OSA and service-connected mood disorder.  The Board indicated that the September 2015 and November 2017 examiners made it clear that the OSA was not related to service or the service-connected mood disorder.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred in failing to consider the reasonably raised theory that his service-connected mental disorder, right ankle, or left knee conditions caused or aggravated his obesity, which caused or aggravated his OSA.  He referenced evidence of record indicating that his service-connected orthopedic conditions reduced his physical levels and that the mood disorder left him feeling “broken and worthless” lacking motivation to engage in activities that might promote fitness or weight loss.  The veteran contended that this evidence, when viewed in conjunction with the September 2015 examiner’s opinion that the veteran’s obesity was a risk factor for the development of OSA, as well as evidence that he gained weight over the same period, was sufficient to reasonably raise the theory that his OSA was proximately related to his service-connected disabilities, with obesity as an intermediate step between the service-connected disabilities and OSA.  The veteran also argued that September 2015 and November 2017 VA examinations were inadequate for rating purposes because the examiners failed to provide sufficient rationale for the conclusion that his service-connected mental disorder did not aggravate his OSA.

At the outset, the Court determined that it has jurisdiction to determine in the first instance whether an issue was reasonably raised by the record when the Board did not provide such analysis.  See Barringer v. Peake, 22 Vet.App. 242 (2008).  Accordingly, because the Board did not determine in the first instance whether obesity as intermediate step toward secondary service connection was reasonably raised, it would address the issue in the first instance.  The Court referenced VA General Counsel Precedent Opinion 1-2017 (Jan. 6, 2017) and Walsh v. Wilkie, 32 Vet.App. 300 (2020) as capably illustrating the mechanism by which obesity as an intermediate step could result in secondary service connection; however, they do not provide guidance regarding what factual circumstances would give rise to claims for secondary service connection via obesity as an intermediate step.

The Court, relying upon a survey of single-judge decisions, noted a broad array of factual circumstances that help to show the type and quality of evidence that may be sufficient to reasonably raise a theory of secondary service connection via obesity as an intermediate step.  These considerations may include, but are not limited to mobility limitations or reduced physical activity as a result of a service-connected physical disability; reduced physical activity or inability to follow a course of exercise as a result of a service-connected mental disability; side effects of medication, such as weight gain, prescribed for a service-connected disability; treatise evidence suggestion a connection between all or some combination of obesity, service-connected disability, and the claimed condition; lay statements attributing weight gain to a service-connected disability; and statements by treating physicians or medical examiners attributing weight gain or obesity to a service-connected disability.

However, the Court emphasized that it does not identify these factors in an attempt to limit any reasonably raised theory of obesity as an intermediate step to a circumscribed set of circumstances.  Rather, the Court noted that while these considerations encompass a diverse array of factual situations, they share a critical commonality:  in each case, there is some evidence in the record which draws an association or suggests a relationship between obesity, or weight gain resulting in obesity, and a service-connected condition.

In applying its holding to the veteran’s case, the Court found no such relationship in the record.  The Court noted that while there is evidence that the veteran has gained weight and is considered obese, there was no evidence linking his service-connected musculoskeletal conditions and resulting mobility limitations to weight gain.  The Court also found no evidence associating the veteran’s service-connected mental disorder to behaviors associated with weight gain or obesity.

Therefore, the Court held that, where, as in this case, the record reflects only incidental references to the veteran’s weight or weight gain, the evidence of record is insufficient to reasonably raise the theory of secondary service connection.

In turning to the veteran’s arguments that the Board erred in relying upon inadequate VA examinations, the Court agreed that the Board’s reliance upon the 2015 and 2017 examinations was inappropriate.  The Court found that the opinions provided no insight into the relevant inquiry of whether the veteran’s service-connected mood disorder aggravated any functional impairment associated with the veteran’s OSA.  The opinions either focused on the etiology of the veteran’s OSA, rather than any potential relationship between OSA and the service-connected mental disorder, or focused on the underlying cause of the veteran’s OSA (collapse of the oropharyngeal tissue), which failed to illuminate whether the veteran’s mental disorder resulted in any additional functional impairment associated with the OSA.

The Court concluded that the Board clearly erred in finding that the VA examinations adequate to adjudicate the claim for service connection for OSA.  Thus, the Court held that remand is necessary for a VA examiner to address whether the veteran’s service-connected mental disorder caused a functional increase in the severity of his OSA.
