Decision Assessment Document
Lyles v. Shulkin, Nov 29, 2017, 29 Vet.App. 107 (2017)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that evaluation of a knee disability under Diagnostic Codes (DCs) 5257 or 5260/61 or both does not, as a matter of law, preclude separate evaluation of a meniscal disability of the same knee under DC 5258 or 5259, and vice versa.  Entitlement to a separate evaluation in a given case depends on whether the manifestations of disability for which a separate evaluation is being sought have already been compensated by an assigned evaluation under a different DC.

The Court further held that in the context of evaluating musculoskeletal disabilities based on limitation of motion, a manifestation of disability has not been compensated, for separate evaluation and pyramiding purposes, if that manifestation did not result in an elevation of the evaluation under 38 C.F.R. 4.40, and 4.45 pursuant to the principles set forth in DeLuca v. Brown, 8 Vet.App. 202 (1995).

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in Manual M21-1, III.iv.4.A.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran filed a claim for increased evaluation for his left knee disability in 2001, which was rated 30 percent disabling under DC 5257 at the time.  During the course of the appeal he underwent meniscectomies of the left knee in 2005 and 2009.  Pre- and post-operative medical and lay evidence showed left knee pain, weakness, buckling, instability, swelling, locking, giving way, popping, stiffening, and grinding, which caused falls and limitation of knee extension.

Similar symptoms were reported during an April 2010 VA examination.  Instability with giving way and frequent locking were reported.  Extension was limited.  There was no additional loss of motion due to pain or any other factor during repetitive motion testing.  The examiner indicated that no comment could be provided on the extent of any additional functional loss during flare-ups without resort to speculation because the veteran was not experiencing a flare-up during the examination.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in 2011, continued the 30 percent evaluation for the left knee under DC 5257, but also awarded a separate 10 percent evaluation for limited extension under DC 5261.  The Court, in 2014, remanded the case because it had determined that the Board had not adequately addressed whether the veteran was entitled to a separate evaluation under DC 5258 or 5259.

Following the Court remand, the veteran underwent another VA examination in August 2015.  The examiner found left knee pain, tenderness, and crepitus with limited extension.  Repetitive motion testing produced no additional loss of motion or functional loss.  The examiner indicated that an opinion could not be given regarding whether pain, weakness, fatigability, or incoordination resulted in functional loss over time because there was no objective evidence relevant to the issue and that any response regarding flare-ups would be speculative.

The Board, in January 2016, found that DC 5259 applied, rather than DC 5258, because the record showed removal of semilunar cartilage instead of dislocation.  The Board observed that the veteran experienced popping, locking, grinding, pain, swelling, looseness, and giving way or falling related to the meniscus removal, but found that a separate evaluation under DC 5259 was not warranted because each of those symptoms already was encompassed by the evaluations under DC 5257 and 5261.   The Board found that the pain, swelling, looseness, and giving way or falling were related to the left knee instability rated under DC 5257 and the popping, grinding, and locking were symptoms of impaired motion contemplated under DC 5261 and had been considered with the potential further impairment of motion pursuant to DeLuca.  The Board, in considering 38 C.F.R. 4.40 and 4.45, explained that there was no clinical evidence that the veteran had further range of motion impairment due to fatigability, incoordination, pain, or flare-ups because the 2010 and 2015 VA examiners both commented that they could not say without resorting to speculation as to what such impairment would be.  The Board concluded that allowing a separate evaluation under DC 5259 would constitute impermissible pyramiding.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred in finding that his evaluations under DCs 5257 and 5261 encompassed all manifestations of his service-connected meniscal disability – namely, popping, locking, grinding, pain, and swelling – because the plain language of those DCs reflects that they cover only instability and limitation of leg extension.  The Secretary responded that the Board properly found, consistent with VA’s interpretation of the scope of DCs 5257 and 5261 in the VA Adjudication Procedures Manual (M21-1), that all manifestations of the left knee meniscal disability were accounted for in the evaluations under DCs 5257 and 5261 and that evaluating those manifestations again under a different DC would constitute impermissible pyramiding.

The Court, affording no deference to the M21-1 interpretation, rejected the Secretary’s argument, as it found that 38 C.F.R. 4.71a did not expressly prohibit separate evaluations under DC 5257 or 5261 and a meniscal DC.  The Court noted that the rating schedule was replete with rules prohibiting separate evaluation of other disabilities, such as 38 C.F.R. 4.96a, which forbids separate evaluations of certain coexisting respiratory conditions, and 38 C.F.R. 4.113, which precludes separate evaluations for certain co-existing gastrointestinal disabilities.  The Court noted that the Secretary declined to include a similar prohibition against separate evaluations in the portion of 4.71a governing evaluation of the knees.

The Court concluded that the lack of an express bar to separate evaluation of knee disabilities in 4.71a must be read as a deliberate decision by the Secretary to permit separate evaluations.  The Court held that the language and history of § 4.71a and the text of the surrounding provisions of the rating schedule unambiguously reflect that evaluation of a knee disability under DC 5257 or 5261 does not preclude, as a matter of law, separate evaluation of a meniscal disability of the same knee under DC 5258 or 5259.

The Court, in applying its holding to the facts of the case, found that the Board erred in finding that the DC 5257 compensated the veteran for manifestations of a left knee disability other than recurrent subluxation and lateral instability, such as pain and swelling, as it had read into the DC nonexistent evaluation criteria that foreclosed the possibility of a separate evaluation under a meniscal DC.  The Court stated that the Board’s finding was contrary to the Federal Circuit’s decision in Delisle v. McDonald, 789 F. 3d 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2015) and VA General Counsel Precedent Opinion 23-97 (July 1, 1997).  Accordingly, the Court reversed the Board’s finding that the pain and swelling associated with the left knee meniscal disability were compensated by the 30 percent evaluation under DC 5257.

At oral argument, the veteran had conceded that VA may consider knee pain, swelling, popping, locking, and grinding under a limitation of motion code, such as DC 5261, pursuant to DeLuca.  However, he argued that those manifestations of the meniscal disability had not been “compensated” in his case because the Board’s DeLuca analysis had not resulted in elevation of the 30 percent evaluation under DC 5261, meaning that evaluation of those manifestations under either DC 5258 or 5259 would not constitute pyramiding.  The Secretary responded that those symptoms had already been considered under DC 5261 and could not be considered under DC 5258 or 5259, even though the Board did not elevate the evaluation under DC 5261 after considering those factors.

The Court agreed with the veteran, holding that where manifestations of a musculoskeletal disability causing additional functional limitation have not resulted in elevation of the evaluation pursuant to DeLuca, those manifestations have not yet been compensated for separate evaluation and pyramiding purposes.

The Court also agreed with the veteran’s concession that the manifestations of the left knee disability not compensated by DC 5257 – pain, swelling popping, locking, and grinding – could be compensated under DC 5261 pursuant to DeLuca.  However, notwithstanding that those manifestations could be compensated under DC 5261, the Board’s reasons or bases for denying a higher left knee evaluation under DC 5261 revealed that those manifestations had not yet been compensated in this case.

The Court held that the Board’s rationale for concluding that the current 30 percent evaluation under DC 5261 compensated for the pain, swelling, popping, locking, and grinding associated with the left knee meniscal disability was inadequate.  The Court found that the Board erred in focusing solely on whether pain caused additional functional limitation sufficient to warrant an elevation of compensation under DC 5261 without discussing the veteran’s other manifestations of left knee meniscal disability that may be covered by 4.45, such as whether swelling, popping, locking or grinding caused additional functional limitation that would result in an elevation of the 30 percent evaluation under DC 5261 pursuant to DeLuca.

The Court also found that the Board’s reliance on examiner statements that they could not opine as to the presence and degree of any additional functional limitation during left knee flare-ups without resorting to mere speculation violated its recent holding in Sharp v. Shulkin, 29 Vet.App. 26 (2017).  The Court indicated that, as in Sharp, the examiners’ statements in this case reflected personal reluctance to offer the medical opinions necessary for proper evaluation of a musculoskeletal disability in accordance with DeLuca, rather than a limitation of knowledge in the medical community at large.

The Court concluded that, given the foregoing deficiencies, it was clear that the Board had not yet properly assessed whether the veteran’s pain, swelling, popping, locking, and grinding associated with his left knee meniscal disability had been compensated by his current left knee evaluation under DC 5261.  The Court held that remand of the veteran’s left knee meniscal disability claim was warranted to determine whether separate evaluation of meniscal disability under either DC 5258 or 5259 was warranted based on the facts of this case or whether such evaluation would constitute impermissible pyramiding.
