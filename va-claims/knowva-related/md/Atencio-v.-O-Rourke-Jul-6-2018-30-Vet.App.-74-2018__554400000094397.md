Decision Assessment Document
Atencio v. O' Rourke, Jul 6, 2018, 30 Vet.App. 74 (2018)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that 38 C.F.R. 3.317 specifically bars gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) from being considered as a medically unexplained chronic multisymptom illness (MUCMI).

The Court also held that 38 C.F.R. 3.317(a)(2)(ii) provides a definition of a MUCMI and not an alternative means to 3.317(a)(2)(i) for establishing what is a qualifying chronic disability.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served in the Air Force from March 1988 to May 1988 and later from January 1991 to July 1991 in support Operations Desert Shield and Desert Storm.  In December 1998, seven years after service, she was seen for a history of significant dyspepsia.  Significant GERD was diagnosed the following month.  The condition required a Nissen fundoplication procedure that was undertaken later in 1999.  The regional office (RO) denied the veteran’s claim for service connection for GERD in May 2000.  No appeal was filed.

In February 2006, the veteran filed to reopen her claim for service connection for GERD.  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in July 2013, reopened the claim and remanded for development.

The veteran underwent a VA examination in April 2014.  The examiner noted that the veteran’s service treatment records were silent as to symptoms of GERD and opined that it was less likely than not that the veteran’s GERD began in service or was due to or aggravated by service-connected sinusitis.  The examiner further stated that there was insufficient evidence to determine whether an association exists between Gulf War service and structural gastrointestinal diseases and that asthma and sinusitis are known to develop from GERD; however, the medical literature does not support a finding that sinusitis commonly results in or aggravates GERD.

The Board denied service connection for GERD in March 2015; however, the Court approved a joint motion for remand (JMR) in which the parties agreed that the Board erred by failing to consider presumptive service connection under 3.317.

The Board, in March 2016, again denied the appeal, finding that the veteran’s GERD cannot be an undiagnosed or chronic multisymptom illness and cannot fall under the presumptive service connection provisions of 3.317 because, while the regulation provides for service connection for certain functional gastrointestinal disorders, it specifically excludes gastrointestinal diseases explainable by endoscopic signs or injury or disease which is how GERD is diagnosed.  The Board noted that the veteran’s GERD diagnosis was based on an endoscopy and upper GI series.  The Board also denied service connection on a direct basis, finding no symptoms of GERD in service to outweigh the veteran’s later statements that she had heartburn in service, and relied on the VA examiner’s opinion in 2014 to conclude that GERD was not the result of or aggravated by the veteran’s service-connected sinusitis.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board misinterpreted 3.317 in concluding that GERD, which is a structural gastrointestinal disease, is categorically precluded from the presumption of service connection afforded to MUCMIs.  She contended that the Board should have considered whether her GERD qualified as a MUCMI under the definition provided in 3.317(a)(2)(ii) in addition to considering whether it was captured under 3.317(a)(2)(i).

The Court observed that § 3.317 is a complex regulation; however, it held that 3.317(a) was not ambiguous and concluded that the regulation excluded GERD from consideration as a MUCMI and does not provide two paths to the presumption under 3.317(a)(2)(i) or 3.317(a)(2)(ii).  The Court noted that under MUCMI, the regulation lists three conditions, to include functional gastrointestinal disorders that would qualify as MUCMI, but also specifically excluded structural gastrointestinal disorders, which includes GERD.

The Court rejected the veteran’s argument that GERD may be considered as a MUCMI under 3.317(a)(2)(ii), notwithstanding its exclusion under 3.317(a)(2)(i), as inconsistent with the very text and structure of the regulation.  Furthermore, the Court noted that 3.317(a)(2)(i) begins with the phrase “for purposes of this section,” indicating that what follows applies to all of the regulation.  Therefore, the exclusion of GERD that follows that phrase applies to all of paragraph (a)(2).

The Court concluded that the text and structure of 3.317(a)(2) make clear that GERD cannot be a “qualifying chronic disability” under 3.317.  Subparagraph (a)(2)(i) provides only two bases on which a disability can qualify for presumptive service connection and GERD does not fit under either basis, as it is not an undiagnosed illness and cannot be a MUCMI because it is specifically excluded.

Lastly, on one final matter concerning 3.317, the Court rejected the veteran’s argument that Congress, under 38 U.S.C. 1117, gave the Secretary the power to include disabilities that are automatically entitled to the presumption, but not the power to exclude disabilities.  The Court concluded that Congress, under section 1117, intended to give the Secretary broad authority to determine which disabilities were to be compensated, which includes the authority to determine which disabilities are not to be compensated as well.  Furthermore, the Court noted that 3.317(a)(2)(ii) stated that “chronic multisymptom illnesses of partially understood etiology and pathophysiology, such as diabetes and multiple sclerosis, will not be considered medically explained.”  Congress explicitly highlighted these two disabilities in legislative history as exclusions from qualification as MUCMIs.

The Court, finding that the terms of the 2015 JMR did not limit the issues before the Board on remand, concluded that the law of the case doctrine did not apply to preclude consideration of direct and secondary service connection and turned to those issues.  The Court rejected the veteran’s arguments that the Board erred in relying on the April 2014 VA examination regarding direct service connection and affirmed the Board’s denial of service connection for GERD on a direct basis.

However, the Court held that the Board failed to provide an adequate statement of reasons or bases for relying on the April 2014 examiner’s opinion regarding secondary aggravation.  The Court found it was unclear how and why the same rationale was applied to both the causation and aggravation elements of secondary service connection.  The Court noted that it had previously made it clear that examiners must address both elements in cases raising both theories.  See El-Amin v. Shinseki, 26 Vet.App. 136 (2013).  The Court indicated that the structure of the opinion appears to indicate that the two may theories may have been considered together.  The Court reiterated that aggravation of a condition by a service-connected disability is independent of direct causation on a secondary basis.

The Court remanded the case for the Board to explain its reliance on the April 2014 medical opinion addressing secondary service connection for GERD or to obtain a new medical opinion or clarification.  The Court affirmed the Board’s denial of service connection for GERD on a presumptive basis under 3.317 and on a direct service connection basis.
