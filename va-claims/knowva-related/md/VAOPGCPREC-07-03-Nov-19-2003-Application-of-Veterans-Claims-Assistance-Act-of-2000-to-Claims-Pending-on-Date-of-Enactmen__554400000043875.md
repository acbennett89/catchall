Decision Assessment Document
 VAOPGCPREC 07-03, Nov 19, 2003, Application of Veterans Claims Assistance Act of 2000 to Claims Pending on Date of Enactment
 Office of General Counsel Precedent Opinion

**What the case is about**

What effect does the decision of the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit in Kuzma v. Principi, 341 F.3d 1327 (Fed. Cir. 2003), have upon the rule set forth by the United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (CAVC) in Karnas v. Derwinski, 1 Vet. App. 308 (1991), concerning the applicability of changes in law?

Do the standards governing the retroactive application of statutes and regulations differ from those governing the retroactive application of rules announced in judicial decisions?

How should the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) determine whether applying a new statute or regulation to a pending claim would have a prohibited retroactive effect?

In determining the applicability of a change in law, is there a difference between claims that were pending before VA when the change occurred and claims that had already been decided by the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) and were pending on direct appeal to a court when that change occurred?

If certain provisions of the Veterans Claims Assistance Act of 2000 (VCAA) were held to be inapplicable to claims filed before November 9, 2000 (the date the VCAA was enacted) and still pending before VA on that date, would VA have authority, from sources other than the VCAA, to continue applying its regulations implementing the VCAA to claims filed before that date?

Does VAOPGCPREC 11-2000 remain viable in light of the holdings in Kuzma, Dyment v. Principi, 287 F.3d 1377 (Fed. Cir. 2002), and Bernklau v. Principi, 291 F.3d 795 (Fed. Cir. 2002)?

**Impact on VBA**

The following is a summary of the holdings in VAOPGCPREC 7-2003, which responded to six questions from the Chairman of the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board):

1.  The first holding states that the Federal Circuit’s decision in Kuzma v. Principi, 341 F.3d 1327 (Fed. Cir. 2003), overrules the CAVC’s Karnas rule, and that the Karnas rule no longer applies in deciding whether a new statute or regulation applies to a pending case.  If a new statute or regulation is silent regarding its applicability to pending claims, VA must decide whether applying the new statute or regulation to a pending case would have a “retroactive effect.”  If applying the new provision would have a retroactive effect, VA may not apply it to the pending claim.  If applying the new provision would not have a retroactive effect, VA must apply the new provision.

2.  The second holding states that the standards governing the retroactive application of statutes and regulations differ from those governing the retroactive application of rules announced in judicial decisions.  Judicial precedents always apply to claims pending when the precedent was issued, but that new statutes and regulations do not apply to pending claims unless (1) their language requires that result or (2) applying the new provision to pending claims would produce no “retroactive effects”.  As for claims pending on the date that a rating schedule provision (regulation) is changed, it will usually be appropriate to apply the old regulation in assigning ratings for periods prior to the date of the change and to apply the new regulation in assigning ratings for periods after the date of the change.

3.  The third holding states that there is no simple test for identifying a “retroactive effect,” but summarizes several factors to be considered in determining whether a new statute or regulation would have a retroactive effect as applied to a pending claim.  A retroactive effect generally involves some impact upon previously accrued rights or previously completed transactions.  Provisions affecting only prospective benefits, such as liberalizing benefit provisions, usually do not produce retroactive effects and thus may be applied to pending claims.  Statutes extinguishing a right to benefits generally would produce retroactive effects to the extent they were applied to periods before the new provision took effect. Provisions regulating procedural matters often may be applied to pending claims without producing any retroactive effects, but new provisions regulating procedures already completed in a particular case might produce a retroactive effect if applied to that case.

4.  The fourth holding stated that there may be a difference, for purposes of retroactivity analysis, between cases that were pending in different procedural postures when a new procedural provision took effect.  New provisions affecting procedural matters usually may be applied to cases pending at the procedural stage governed by the new provision (e.g., a new statute regulating regional-office adjudication procedures usually may be applied to cases pending at a regional office without raising retroactivity concerns).  However, applying procedural provisions to cases in which the relevant procedural stage was completed prior to enactment may produce disfavored retroactive effects (e.g., applying a new statute regulating regional-office adjudication procedures to require remand of cases pending at the Board or CAVC may produce disfavored retroactive effects).  There may sometimes be a distinction even between cases pending at the same procedural stage.  This merely indicates that, in considering a new procedural law such as the VCAA, the Board need not treat all cases the same, but may consider the circumstances.  The Board has broad remand authority, and a remand based on a conclusion that further proceedings under the new law may be helpful in deciding the claim would not necessarily constitute a retroactive effect.  In contrast, in cases where technical compliance with a new procedural rule would serve no apparent purpose, the Board need not remand, because pointless remands and repetition of effort may be viewed as retroactive effects.

5.  The fifth holding states that, even if the VCAA is construed not to apply retroactively to all cases pending before VA on November 9, 2000 (i.e., not to require remand of cases pending at the Board), VA may apply its VCAA regulations to all such claims.  In issuing its regulations, VA expressly stated that it would apply its VCAA regulations to all claims pending before VA on November 9, 2000.  Because VA has expressly prescribed the retroactive reach of its regulations, it is unnecessary to decide whether the regulations would produce retroactive effects.  There are limits on VA’s authority to issue regulations that have retroactive effect, but the General Counsel concluded that the VCAA regulations were within VA’s authority to issue retroactive procedural regulations that benefit claimants.

6.  The sixth holding states that VAOPGCPREC 11-2000 is withdrawn, because the Federal Circuit’s precedents have cast doubt on some aspects of that opinion and because there is no further need to rely upon that opinion.
