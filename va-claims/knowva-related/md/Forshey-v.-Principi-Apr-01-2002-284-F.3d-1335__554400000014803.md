Decision Assessment Document
 Forshey v. Principi, Apr 01, 2002, 284 F.3d 1335
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit (Court) held that under [38 U.S.C. 7292](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/7292), it has jurisdiction over (1) issues concerning the validity of statutes or regulations on which the decision of the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (CAVC) depended; (2) issues of interpretation if the CAVC elaborated the meaning of a statute or regulation and the decision depended on that interpretation; (3) issues of validity or interpretation raised before the CAVC but not decided, if the decision would have been altered by adopting the position that was urged; and (4) other “relevant” questions of law.  The Court also held that even when jurisdiction exists, prudential considerations should severely limit the exercise of the Court’s authority to consider issues not raised or decided below.  In this case, the Federal Circuit affirmed the judgment of the CAVC.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new significance.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

In this case, the Court issued this decision as a result of a rehearing by the full Court (en banc).  As a result, this opinion replaces the one found at [284 F.3d 1335 (Fed. Cir. 2002)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/284/284.F3d.1323.00-1352.00-1266.html). The appellant (who was seeking service connection for the cause of the veteran’s death), the surviving spouse of the veteran, sought to raise the following three issues:  (1) the issue of the validity of [38 CFR 3.301(c)(2)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=bad79175e1d5559351586147159d028c&node=se38.1.3_1301&rgn=div8) because it provides no standard of proof to rebut the presumption of service connection set forth in [38 USC 105(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/105) (the “validity” issue); (2) the interpretation of 38 USC 105(a) concerning the use of negative evidence to rebut the statutory presumption (the “negative evidence” issue); and (3) the interpretation of [38 U.S.C. 5107(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5107) as to whether it establishes a generally applicable "preponderance" standard of proof, or merely a rule for weighing evidence (the “burden of proof” issue).

The Federal Circuit concluded that the negative evidence issue was without merit.  Since the definition of evidence ([a]ll the means by which any alleged matter of fact, the truth of which is submitted to investigation, is established or disproved) encompasses negative evidence that tends to disprove the existence of an alleged fact,  the negative evidence was relevant to the proximate cause determination under the applicable statues and regulations.  The Court concluded that the validity and burden of proof issues should not be addressed because none of the exceptions applied:  (1) there is no new statute that governs these proceedings;  (2) while the Court did articulate new law for jurisdictional and prudential requirements in this opinion, that new law does not relate to the subject matter of the issues presented;  (3) the exception authorizing the Court to apply the correct law was not satisfied because no issue was raised in the CAVC concerning the correct standard of review; (4) finally, this is not a case that was litigated pro se in the CAVC.  Under these circumstances, the Court declined to consider the validity and burden of proof issues.
