Decision Assessment Document
Payne v. Wilkie, Aug 9, 2019, 31 Vet.App. 373 (2019)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that 38 U.S.C. 1114(k) does not limit potential entitlement to special monthly compensation (SMC) at the (k) rate, based on loss of or loss of use of one or more creative organs, to veterans with certain service-connected disabilities nor does section 1114(k) preclude a theory of entitlement based on a multi-link causal chain between a service-connected disability and the loss of use of a creative organ.

The Court also held that the plain text of 38 C.F.R. 3.155(d)(2) clearly and unambiguously does not require that a claimant file a formal claim to assert entitlement to ancillary benefits, which includes entitlement to SMC(k).

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated as guidance into the Manual M21-1 under IV.ii.2.H.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The regional office (RO), in April 1993, awarded service connection for carpal tunnel syndrome and assigned separate 10 percent evaluations for each hand from July 14, 1992.  The veteran appealed the assigned ratings.  After a lengthy procedural history, the RO issued a Statement of the Case (SOC) in August 2004, which increased the evaluation for carpal tunnel syndrome of the right hand to 30 percent and for the left hand to 20 percent from July 1992 and 50 percent for the right hand and 40 percent for the left hand from February 2003.

In March 2005, while the appeal was pending, the veteran filed a request for a total disability evaluation due to individual unemployability (TDIU), alleging that he was unable to secure or follow substantially gainful employment as a result of his upper extremity disabilities.  The following month the RO awarded a TDIU evaluation effective March 4, 2005.  The veteran did not appeal the April 2005 decision, but did continue to pursue appeals for higher evaluations for the bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome.  The RO, in July 2013, awarded 50 and 40 percent evaluations for the right and left carpal tunnel syndrome, respectively, from July 14, 1992.

Following a Court remand, the veteran’s counsel, in September 2016, submitted written argument to the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) asserting that the veteran was entitled to a TDIU rating from July 1992 and that SMC(k) was warranted for loss of use of a creative organ as an ancillary benefit to the upper extremity claims.  Counsel referenced VA medical records showing erectile dysfunction and argued that the veteran’s service-connected upper extremity disabilities materially contributed to the cardiovascular disease (service-connected with a 100 percent rating) and obesity, which is associated with erectile dysfunction.

Following a December 2016 Board remand, the RO mailed the veteran and his counsel the standardized VA claim forms for an earlier effective for the TDIU grant, erectile dysfunction as secondary to service-connected disabilities, and entitlement to SMC(k).  Neither the veteran nor his attorney responded.

The Board, in September 2017, denied entitlement to higher disability ratings for the bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome and determined that the issues of entitlement to SMC(k) and an earlier effective date for TDIU were not before it.  The Board acknowledged that the veteran explicitly argued for entitlement to SMC(k) based on loss of use of a creative organ, but determined that the issue was not before it because he did not file a formal claim for the benefit.  The Board explained that after September 2014, 3.155 requires that all claims governed by VA adjudication regulations must be filed on standard forms prescribed by VA, regardless of the type of claim or posture in which it arises.  The Board noted that the veteran never responded to the RO’s mailing of the standard form to file a formal claim; thus, since  the veteran had not formally filed new claims for benefits, no further action was necessary.  The Board determined that the TDIU issue was not before it because the veteran had not filed the necessary forms to initiate a formal claim.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred when it required that he file a formal claim for entitlement to SMC(k) for loss of use of a creative organ as ancillary to his service-connected upper extremity disabilities before it could exercise jurisdiction over the matter.  He further asserted that the plain text of section 1114(k) does not indicate that Congress intended to preclude his theory of entitlement based on a multi-link causal chain, and that the post-2014 version of 3.155 does not require claimants to file a formal claim for entitlement to ancillary benefits, such as SMC(k).

The Court held that section 1114(k) does not limit potential SMC(k) entitlement to veterans based on loss or loss of use of one or more creative organs to specific service-connected disabilities and that the section 1114(k) does not preclude a theory of entitlement based on a multi-link causal chain between a service-connected disability and the loss of use of a creative organ.

The Court observed that section 1114(k) does not specify the types of service-connected disabilities that may result in the anatomical loss or loss of use or one or more creative organs for purposes of that subsection; however, in other subsections of section 1114, Congress explicitly limited entitlement to SMC to veterans with specific service-connected disabilities.  The Court reasoned that because Congress did not explicitly include such limitations when drafting section 1114(k), the Court presumed that it did not intend to limit potential entitlement to SMC(k) only to veteran specific service-connected disabilities.

The Court also found section 1114(k)’s textual causation requirement – that the anatomical loss or loss of use of a creative organ be the result of a service-connected disability – is broad and does not indicate that Congress intended to preclude a theory of entitlement based on a multi-link causal chain between the service-connected disability and the loss of use.

The Court next turned to the matter of whether the Board erred when it determined that the issue of SMC(k) was not before it because the veteran did not file a formal claim for SMC(k) pursuant to the post-2014 version of 3.155.  The Court concluded that the plain text of 3.155(d)(2) clearly and unambiguously does not require that a claimant file formal claim to assert entitlement to ancillary benefits, including entitlement to SMC(k).  The Court noted that the plain text of the regulation explicitly provides that VA should continue to adjudicate entitlement to ancillary benefits, such as SMC(k) “as part of” the underlying claim, and the claimant “may, but need not, assert entitlement to ancillary benefits.”  See 38 C.F.R. 3.155(d)(2).

The Court rejected the Secretary’s argument that the Board did not have jurisdiction to address SMC(k) because the disability claims on appeal were not for ED or a genitourinary disability and did not immediately cause the veteran’s loss of use of a creative organ.  The Court concluded that the Board erred when it interpreted the post-2014 version of 3.155 to require that the veteran file a formal claim for entitlement to SMC(k) as an ancillary benefit to his service-connected upper extremity claims.

The Court reversed the portion of the Board’s decision that implicitly found that it lacked jurisdiction over the issue of entitlement to SMC(k) and remanded the matter for the Board to adjudicate in the first instance.

The Court rejected the Secretary’s argument that the April 2005 RO decision served to bifurcate the issue of TDIU from the appeal for benefits for the upper extremity disability.  The Court concluded that a NOD was not required to place the TDIU issue in appellate status, as the issue is controlled by its decision in Harper v. Brown, 30 Vet.App. 360 (2018).  In Harper, the Court held that a TDIU claim, which has been awarded, remains in appellate status if the TDIU award is not effective from the date of claim for the underlying disability rating that is on appeal and a NOD is not required to place the TDIU issue in appellate status.

The Court concluded that the RO’s award of TDIU effective from March 2005 served as a partial grant of benefits and did not bifurcate the issue of TDIU from the appeal of the initial disability rating for the upper extremity disabilities and, consistent with Harper, the Board had jurisdiction to consider the issue of TDIU prior to March 2005, which remained in appellate status.  The Court explained that, pursuant to Harper, once the issue of the appropriate initial disability ratings for the veteran’s upper extremity disabilities was in appellate status by virtue of the July 1993 NOD, the issue of TDIU became part and parcel of the underlying upper extremity claims when he filed an application for TDIU in March 2005.

The Court reversed the portion of the Board’s decision that implicitly found that, because the appellant did not file a formal claim for entitlement to TDIU for the period prior to March 4, 2005, it lacked jurisdiction over the issue, and remanded the matter for the Board to adjudicate in the first instance.

The Court rejected the veteran’s argument that the May 2015 VA examination was inadequate to address his right-hand disability because he developed muscle wasting in the right hand subsequent to the examination.  The Court concluded that the veteran’s assertion was insufficient to demonstrate clear error in the Board’s determination that a reexamination was not warranted.  The Court also rejected the veteran’s contention that the Board provided an inadequate statement of reasons or bases for declining to award an extra-schedular rating based on his assertion that his upper extremity disabilities caused his obesity.  The Court found that the veteran failed to raise any arguments challenging the Board’s finding that the requirements for referral for extra-schedular consideration were not met.
