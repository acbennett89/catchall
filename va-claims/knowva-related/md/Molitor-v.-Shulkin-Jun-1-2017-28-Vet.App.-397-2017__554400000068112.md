Decision Assessment Document
 Molitor v. Shulkin, Jun 1, 2017, 28 Vet.App. 397 (2017)
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that VA General Counsel Opinion 05-14 provides that the duty to assist requires that VA, in a claim for service connection for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) based on in-service personal assault, must attempt to obtain relevant records of fellow service members when the claimant adequately identifies such relevant records that may aid in corroborating a claimed personal assault or notify the claimant why it will not undertake such efforts.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served in the Army from November 1985 to January 1987, when she was discharged as a result of pregnancy.  She served as a military police officer in Germany from April 1986 to January 1987.

The veteran initially filed a claim for service connection for PTSD in December 2003.  Multiple VA examinations conducted over the next several years related PTSD due to childhood trauma, rather than in-service stressors, or rendered diagnoses other than PTSD, such as personality disorder.  VA psychiatric examiners failed to diagnose PTSD based on in-service stressors on the basis of no behavioral changes in service and/or determining that the veteran’s descriptions of military sexual trauma (MST) were not credible due to inconsistencies and contradictions.

A Vet Center counselor submitted statements that the veteran did not report the in-service assault because of fear of retaliation and it was likely as not that her PTSD was related to service.  The counselor also explained that the veteran’s medication and treatment with electroconvulsive therapy may have caused confusion and memory problems.

A sexual trauma markers worksheet was provided in 2005, which included the approximate date of the claimed assault, location, unit, and the names of several witnesses.  In an October 2012 statement, the veteran provided the names of alleged assailants.  She also identified the names of four women who served in her unit, to include one that committed suicide, which may have suffered sexual assault in service or had filed claims for residuals of MST.

The Appeals Management Center (AMC), in April 2012, issued a formal finding that the claimed in-service assaults could not be verified based on the information provided by the veteran, as there was no evidence of sexual assault markers in the service personnel or medical records.

Following the most recent Board remand in December 2014, an expert medical opinion was provided in January 2015.  A VA psychologist reviewed the claims file and determined that the record supported the current diagnoses of borderline personality disorder and PTSD based on childhood stressors.  The examiner opined that both conditions preexisted service and were not aggravated or otherwise related to claimed in-service stressors.  She also described the counselor’s treatment notes as “extremely cursory and incomplete” and rejected the diagnosis of PTSD based on MST as not supported by the record.

The Board, in May 2015, denied service connection for an acquired psychiatric disorder to include PTSD.  The Board found that VA complied with the duty to assist, but did not discuss the veteran’s request for efforts to attempt to obtain records from fellow service members.  The Board found that the veteran’s statements regarding the in-service sexual assaults were not credible because they were internally inconsistent and contradicted by other evidence of record.  The Board assigned greater probative value to the negative VA opinions and discounted the Vet Center counselor’s opinions because they did not have an adequate rationale, were based on the veteran’s non-credible statements, and there was no indication that the counselor reviewed the claims file.  The Board noted that the VA examiners agreed that the counselor’s opinions were not supported by the evidence of record.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred in determining that VA satisfied its duty to assist because GC Precedent Opinion 05-14 (GC Opinion) required VA to attempt to obtain the records from other service members that she specifically identified, including one of her alleged assailants, that may corroborate the claimed in-service assaults, or, at a minimum, explain why VA did not undertake such efforts.

The Court agreed with the veteran that the Board provided inadequate reasons or bases for finding that VA satisfied its duty to assist.  The Court held that the GC Opinion provides that the duty to assist requires VA, in a claim for service connection for PTSD based on an in-service personal assault, to make reasonable efforts to obtain records from fellow service members when those records are adequately identified by the claimant, relevant to the claim, would aid in substantiating the claim, and subject to disclosure under the Privacy Act and 38 U.S.C. 5701 and 7332.

The Court, in referencing the GC Opinion, also held that whether VA’s “reasonable efforts” to assist include seeking written consent of the third party on behalf of the claimant is a factual question to be answered on a case-by-case basis, weighing the competing interests of the claimant and the third party and considering the sensitive nature of the particular claim.

The Court, finding that the information provided by the veteran was collectively sufficient to facilitate a VA search for the service members’ records, concluded that it constituted adequate identification of outstanding records for duty to assist purposes.  The Court also was persuaded that the identified records were relevant and may have a reasonable possibility of substantiating the veteran’s claim because they relate to the claimed initiation rape and may aid in corroborating the stressor.  The Court concluded that the records may show medical treatment for injuries inflicted upon the assailant by the veteran, similar assaults upon or claims for service connection for MST residuals by female service members that could establish a rape culture on the base, and evidence confirming a female soldier’s suicide that would bolster the credibility of the veteran’s report of events.

Accordingly, the Court concluded that the General Counsel Opinion was applicable to her claim and the Board was required to consider it in assessing whether VA satisfied its duty to assist.

The Court directed that remand was required for the Board to address the applicability of the General Counsel Opinion and whether any other development is necessary in accordance with the opinion.

The Court provided additional guidance to the Board on remand, explaining that a lack of evidence of behavior changes in service does not constitute negative evidence against a claim for service connection for PTSD based on an in-service personal assault.  The Court also noted that the veteran had not received a VA examination or opinion in accordance with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-V, which is required under current VA regulations.
