Decision Assessment Document
 Haas v. Peake, May 8, 2008, 525 F.3d 1168
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that VA’s requirement that a veteran have been present within the land borders of Vietnam at some point in the course of duty is a permissible interpretation of the phrase “served in the Republic of Vietnam” in [38 U.S.C. § 1116(a)(1)(A)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1116) and its implementing regulation, [38 C.F.R. § 3.307(a)(6)(iii)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=3fbfa4e4c9bbff9f8806bd454cf6f6f5&node=se38.1.3_1307&rgn=div8).

The Federal Circuit also held that VA’s interpretation of § 3.307(a)(6)(iii) in [VA General Counsel Opinion 27-97](http://www.va.gov/ogc/docs/1997/Prc27-97.doc) and rulemaking documents printed in the Federal Register that “service in Vietnam” required physical presence on the landmass of Vietnam (or in its navigable inland waterways) was entitled to judicial deference, because the interpretation was neither plainly erroneous nor inconsistent with the language of the regulation.

The Federal Circuit also held that the pre-2002 version of the Manual M21-1 required a finding of “service in Vietnam” for a veteran who had received the Vietnam Service Medal (VSM). The Court held that the Manual M21-1 provision was an interpretive statement, not a substantive rule, and that VA had reinterpreted the Vietnam service requirement to require service within the land borders of Vietnam long before the veteran filed his claim.  Because that new interpretation was well established, and even had been expressed in rulemaking documents published in the Federal Register, VA had sufficiently notified the veteran of VA’s changed interpretation of § 3.307(a)(6)(iii).  Therefore, VA was not bound by the Manual M21-1 provision in this case.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The Federal Circuit provided an extensive recitation of the well-established facts of the case as well as the history of the legislative and regulatory measures related to herbicide exposure in Vietnam, which covered the history prior to and after the enactment of the [Agent Orange Act of 1991](http://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/STATUTE-105/pdf/STATUTE-105-Pg11.pdf).

The issues before the Federal Circuit focused on whether the phrase “service in the Republic of Vietnam” in the Agent Orange Act of 1991 was ambiguous; whether VA’s interpreting regulation (§ 3.307(a)(6)(iii)) of the phrase was ambiguous; whether VA’s interpretation of that regulation was entitled to deference, or was inconsistent with VA’s previous interpretation of that regulation; and whether the pre-2002 Manual M21-1 provision constituted a substantive rule that was binding on VA.

The Federal Circuit found the statutory phrase “served in the Republic of Vietnam,” in both the statute and § 3.307(a)(6)(iii), to be ambiguous concerning application to service in the waters adjoining the landmass of Vietnam.

Although the Federal Circuit found some inconsistency in VA’s interpretation of § 3.307(a)(6)(iii); it ruled that the inconsistency did not preclude deference to VA’s regulatory interpretation, notably in light of the fact that VA has interpreted the regulation consistently for some years, going back to a time well before the veteran filed an application for benefits related to herbicide exposure.

The 1991 version of the Manual M21-1 and later versions issued on several occasions during the 1990s did not reflect VA’s present interpretation of [§ 3.307(a)(6)(iii)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=3fbfa4e4c9bbff9f8806bd454cf6f6f5&node=se38.1.3_1307&rgn=div8); nonetheless, the Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court erred to conclude that the inconsistency of the early versions deprives VA’s current interpretation of the right to judicial deference, because the VA adopted its current interpretation of § 3.307(a)(6)(iii) in 1997, through VA General Counsel Opinion 27-97, and has reiterated the interpretation on numerous occasions to include amending the Manual 21-1 in 2002 to expressly incorporate the “foot-on-land” interpretation of the Agent Orange regulations.  The Federal Circuit concluded that VA’s position has been consistent for more than a decade, and there is no reason to suspect that the interpretation does not reflect the agency’s fair and considered judgment on the matter.

The Federal Circuit disagreed with the Veterans Court that the General Counsel opinion was legally flawed.  Noting the Veterans Court’s criticism that VA’s interpretation of the phrase “service in the Republic of Vietnam” in § 3.307(a)(6)(iii) differed from VA’s interpretation of the same phrase in [38 C.F.R. § 3.313](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=3fbfa4e4c9bbff9f8806bd454cf6f6f5&node=se38.1.3_1313&rgn=div8), which concerns awards of service connection for non-Hodgkins Lymphoma, the Federal Circuit stated that the Veterans Court failed to account for the differences in language, scientific basis, and legal authorization between the two regulations.  § 3.307 (formerly § 3.311a) was the regulatory predecessor of the Agent Orange Act that included diseases linked to herbicide exposure; whereas, § 3.313 was not based on herbicide exposure, but on a Center for Disease Control study of the occurrence of NHL in different groups of veterans, which was applied to veterans who served offshore.

The Federal Circuit determined that VA had not acted arbitrarily in drawing a line between veterans who served on land, where herbicides were used, and veterans who served at sea, where herbicides were not used.  Thus, the Federal Circuit held that substantial deference was due to VA’s interpretation of § 3.307(a)(6)(iii) as it is a plausible construction of the statutory language.

The 1991 version of the Manual M21-1, prior to its 2002 revision, provided that ordinarily the statutory and regulatory test for “service in Vietnam” would be satisfied by receipt of the VSM.  In finding the Manual M21-1 provision as an interpretive statement, rather than a substantive rule, the Federal Circuit stated that the provision did not set forth a firm legal test for “service in the Republic of Vietnam,” but simply provided guidance as how an adjudicator should go about gathering information necessary to determine whether the regulatory test had been satisfied.  Since it was not a substantive rule, it could be amended without adherence to notice-and-comment rulemaking.  The Federal Circuit elaborated that to treat the VSM as a “test” of eligibility for statutory presumption would be contrary to the Agent Orange Act, because it is indisputable that some recipients of the VSM were never either in Vietnam or its territorial waters and could never be properly characterized as having served “in the Republic of Vietnam” under any definition of the phrase.

The Federal Circuit also rejected the veteran’s argument that VA’s denial of his claim constituted an impermissible retroactive application of the 2002 amendment of the Manual M21-1 as VA’s interpretation of the statute and regulation were clear before the veteran filed his claim.

The Federal Circuit reversed the Veterans Court decision and remanded the case for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.
