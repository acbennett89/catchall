**Decision Assessment Document**

***Lynch v. McDonough*, 21 F.4th 776 (December 17, 2021)**

**United States Court of Appeals, Federal Circuit**

**What the case is about:**

The Veteran appealed final decision of the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (CAVC), which affirmed denial by the Board of Veterans’ Appeals of his claim for disability rating greater than 30% for service-connected post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). The Federal Circuit Court of Appeals (Federal Circuit) held in an en banc decision that the benefit-of-the-doubt rule is triggered when there is nearly equal evidence and that evidence is not in “approximate balance” or “nearly equal,” and therefore the benefit-of-the-doubt rule does not apply, when the evidence persuasively favors one side or the other.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Federal Circuit’s decision will be referenced in the Manual M21-1 at V.ii.1.a.5.e.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The Veteran served on active duty in the United States Marine Corps from July 1972 to July 1976. In March 2015 he presented for counseling upon the recommendation of his veteran peer group and was evaluated on two separate occasions by a private psychologist who reported that his symptoms supported a diagnosis of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). In March 2016, the Veteran filed a claim for service connection for PTSD and in August of 2016 he underwent a VA PTSD examination. The VA examiner reviewed the private psychiatrist’s report, confirmed the diagnosis of PTSD, and reported that PTSD did not result in symptoms that were severe enough to interfere with occupational or social functioning or to require continuous medication. The Regional Office (RO) of the VA acknowledged service connection for PTSD and assigned a 30% disability rating.

In October 2016, the Veteran filed a Notice of Disagreement disputing the 30% disability rating. In support, he submitted two additional psychological evaluations conducted by another private psychiatrist. In July 2017, the Veteran underwent a second VA PTSD examination, which documented his symptomatology and addressed the conflicting medical opinions regarding the severity of his symptoms, noting, for example, that some of the conclusions reached by his private psychiatrist “were more extreme than what was supported by available evidence.” In August 2017, the RO continued the 30% disability rating.

The Board of Veterans Appeals (Board) subsequently denied the Veteran’s appeal for a higher PTSD evaluation, finding that based on the record the Veteran “does not have social and occupational impairment manifested by reduced reliability and productivity” that would warrant a disability rating greater than 30% for PTSD. The Board noted that “private examiners have described more severe impairment than that identified by the VA examiners; however, those findings are not supported by the subjective symptoms” reported by the Veteran. The Board concluded that “the preponderance of the evidence is against the claim and entitlement” for a disability rating greater than 30% for PTSD.

On appeal to the CAVC the Veteran argued that the Board misapplied 38 U.S.C. § 5107(b) and wrongly found that he was not entitled to the “benefit of the doubt.” The CAVC rejected the Veteran’s assertion that he was entitled to the benefit of the doubt and affirmed the Board’s decision, reasoning that “the doctrine of reasonable doubt ... d[oes] not apply here because the preponderance of the evidence is against the claim.” In support of its reasoning the CAVC relied on *Ortiz v. Principi,* 274 F.3d 1361, 1364 (Fed. Cir. 2001) which stated that “the benefit of the doubt rule is inapplicable when the preponderance of the evidence is found to be against the claimant.”

Continuing his appeal to the Federal Circuit, the Veteran argued that *Ortiz* was wrongly decided because it sets forth an “equipoise of the evidence” standard to trigger the benefit-of-the-doubt rule and that this decreased his chance of receiving a disability rating greater than 30% for PTSD. According to that argument Ortiz read the modifier “approximate” out of the term “approximate balance” set forth in 38 U.S.C. §5107(b) by requiring an equal or even balance of the evidence to give the benefit of the doubt to the claimant. The Veteran’s argument first suggested that *Ortiz* expressly requires equipoise of the evidence for a claimant to receive the benefit of the doubt. Second, the Veteran contended that *Ortiz’s* statement that “the benefit of the doubt rule is inapplicable when the preponderance of the evidence is found to be against the claimant” is contrary to the statutory “approximate balance” standard.

In contrast to the Veteran’s suggestion the Federal Circuit determined that *Ortiz* sets forth an equipoise-of-the-evidence standard to trigger the benefit-of-the-doubt rule. The Federal Circuit determined that *Ortiz* explicitly gives force to the modifier “approximate” as used in  §5107(b), and necessarily requires that the benefit-of-the-doubt rule may be triggered in situations other than equipoise of the evidence—specifically, situations where the evidence is “nearly equal” (i.e., when an “approximate balance” of the positive and negative evidence as set forth in  §5107(b) and 38 C.F.R §3.102). In its discussion the Federal Circuit conceded that the CAVC’s recitation of the equipoise-of-the-evidence standard in *Chotta v. Peake*, 22, Vet App. 80 (2008) was incorrect, but ultimately determined that misstep did not negatively affect the claimant in that case because that case was ultimately remanded to the Board.

The Federal Circuit made clear that §5107(b) as interpreted by *Ortiz* necessarily includes scenarios where the evidence is not in equipoise but is nevertheless in approximate balance. Put differently, the Federal Circuit stated that if the positive and negative evidence is in approximate balance (which includes but is not limited to equipoise), the claimant receives the benefit of the doubt. The Federal Circuit also affirmed that *Ortiz* correctly established that the benefit-of-the-doubt rule does not apply when a factfinder is persuaded by the evidence to make a particular finding. However, to “eliminate the potential for confusion going forward” the Federal Circuit departed from *Ortiz’s* “preponderance of the evidence” language and determined that the benefit-of-the-doubt rule simply applies if the competing evidence is in “approximate balance,” which *Ortiz* correctly interpreted as evidence that is “nearly equal.” As a corollary, the Federal Circuit specified that evidence is not in “approximate balance” or “nearly equal,” and therefore the benefit-of-the-doubt rule does not apply when the evidence persuasively favors one side or the other.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the ruling of the CAVC.
