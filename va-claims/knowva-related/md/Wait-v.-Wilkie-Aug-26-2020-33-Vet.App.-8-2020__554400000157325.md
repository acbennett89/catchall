Decision Assessment Document
Wait v. Wilkie, Aug 26, 2020, 33 Vet.App. 8 (2020)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that to establish a disability, under 38 U.S.C. 1110, pursuant to Saunders v Wilkie, 866 F.3d 1356 (Fed. Cir. 2018), there must be competent evidence specific to the veteran tending to show that his or her impairment rises to a level causing functional impairment of earning capacity, which may include showing manifestations of a disability of similar severity, frequency, and duration as those VA has determined through the rating schedule would cause impaired earning capacity in an average person.

Although the Court held that the rating schedule may serve as a proxy for determining whether certain manifestations may impair earning capacity, the veteran must show that his or her manifestations are of sufficient severity, duration, and frequency that they effect the veteran’s ability to function under the ordinary conditions of life.  Merely pointing to a diagnostic code that recognizes a symptom or manifestation as potentially affecting earning capacity is not sufficient to establish a disability with the same manifestations.  VA must make a factual determination as to whether the manifestations rise to the level of a functional impairment in earning capacity.

(The Federal Circuit held in Saunders that pain alone, even in the absence of a diagnosis, can serve as a functional impairment and qualify as a disability under section 1110, irrespective of the underlying cause.)

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in the Manual under III.iv.5.B.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served from January 1986 to January 1989, was treated for right hip pain and stiffness during service.  He filed for service connection for a right hip disability in January 2013.  VA treatment records showed complaint of right hip pain that worsened with sitting and standing too long and interfered with his quality of life by impacting sleep, general activity, and mobility.  The regional office, in July 2013, denied service connection for a right hip disability on the basis of an absence of evidence of a diagnosed disability.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in July 2018, denied entitlement to disability compensation for a right hip disability.  The Board acknowledged that, under Saunders, pain may constitute a disability if resulting in functional impairment that affects earning capacity; however, the Board found that the veteran’s right hip pain did not rise to the level of a disability under Saunders.  The Board concluded that without a current disability, there is no valid claim for service connection.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board provided inadequate reasons or bases for concluding that he does not have a current right hip disability because it failed to consider favorable evidence reflecting that his hip pain was so severe that it impaired his ability to sit, lay down, and sleep, and did not adequately explain why his hip pain did not cause a functional impairment of earning capacity.  He contended that the Board’s failure to appropriately account for the evidence of functional loss was prejudicial because VA has recognized that sitting and sleeping impairments impact earning capacity.  The veteran further argued that VA’s regulations contemplate, as a matter of law, the level of functional impairment of earning capacity necessary to establish a disability under Saunders and that the rating schedule may serve as an indicator that VA generally recognizes functional impairments as affecting earning capacity.

The Court, in addressing what role, if any, VA’s rating schedule has in assessing the presence of a disability under section 1110, held that the rating schedule may serve as a proxy for determining whether certain manifestations may impair earning capacity.  The Court held that where VA has determined by regulation that the average person with certain manifestations would suffer an impairment of earning capacity, that may be relevant and instructive with regard to whether an individual veteran with similar manifestations has functional impairment of earning capacity.

The Court, although holding that the rating schedule could serve as a proxy for determining impairment of earning capacity, found that the veteran had not provided any persuasive reason to conclude that merely pointing to a diagnostic code that recognizes a symptom or manifestation as potentially affecting earning capacity demonstrates, as a matter of law, the level of functional impairment of earning capacity necessary to establish a disability in a particular claimant with the same manifestations.  The Court held that VA must make a factual determination as to whether the manifestations rise to the level of a functional impairment in earning capacity.  Pursuant to Saunders, there must be competent evidence specific to a claimant tending to show his or her impairment rises to a level to affect earning capacity.

In applying its holding to the case, the Court concluded that the veteran cannot rest solely on VA’s regulations to establish that the Board erred when it found no current disability.  The veteran had argued that diagnostic codes for fibromyalgia, sleep apnea, and mental health disorders (DCs 5025, 6847, and 4.130), which he alleged contemplate sleep difficulties or disturbances, and 38 C.F.R. 4.45 and 4.59, which recognize painful motion, demonstrated that his hip pain and resulting effects constituted a disability under Saunders.  Although the Court indicated that although the veteran may generally rely on VA’s regulations to assert that VA recognizes difficulty sitting and sleeping as the types of manifestations that cause functional impairment in earning capacity, he must also show that the manifestations are of sufficient severity, duration, and frequency that they affect his ability to function under the ordinary conditions of life.

The Court, noting that the veteran had conceded that his treatment records do not clearly attribute difficulty sitting or sleeping to right hip pain (the records also referred to back pain) and that the overall record does not contain evidence regarding the extent of any functional limitation, found no prejudicial error in the Board’s failure to explicitly discuss certain evidence or that the outcome would have been any different if the Board had done so.

The Court affirmed the Board’s decision.
