Decision Assessment Document
 McDowell v. Shinseki, Oct 13, 2009, 23 Vet.App. 207
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the definition of “illegitimate child” under [38 C.F.R. § 3.210(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=a2ba4fce8d9f6af9cabb35e8987c88cd&node=se38.1.3_1210&rgn=div8) inherently requires proof of a biological relationship between the veteran and child.  The Court also held that VA may consider the results of a DNA test when determining whether a child is an “illegitimate child” for the purposes of the regulation.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who was service connected for post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), was hospitalized for treatment of the mental disorder from January 14, 1992, through February 18, 1992.  Erectile dysfunction was also diagnosed during the hospitalization.  The veteran died the following month due to pneumonia complicated by dehydration.  He was not married at the time and no dependents were identified.

The appellant, on November 19, 1992, gave birth to a daughter, T.M.  She filed a claim for DIC benefits in February 2003, asserting that T.M. was the veteran’s child.  She acknowledged that the veteran was not listed on her daughter’s birth certificate and having no documentation establishing the veteran as father.  She submitted multiple letters from friends and family attesting that the veteran had acknowledged or accepted T.M. as his child and had plans to take care of the child.

The appellant provided the regional office (RO) with T.M.’s birth certificate bearing the veteran’s name, which was handwritten in to identify him as the father.  All other information on the birth certificate had been typewritten.  She later told the RO that she had written the veteran’s name on the birth certificate to demonstrate how the document should have been completed.

The appellant also obtained DNA testing from an independent agency to establish T.M. as the veteran’s child.  The report provided to the RO concluded that, based on the sample provided by T.M. and the veteran’s biological sisters, there was a 0.208 percent probability that he veteran was the biological father of the appellant’s daughter.

The RO concluded that the evidence failed to establish that the veteran was T.M.’s biological father.  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in March 2007, examined the evidence in accordance with [38 C.F.R. § 3.210](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=a2ba4fce8d9f6af9cabb35e8987c88cd&node=se38.1.3_1210&rgn=div8) and found that there was no legal documentation that the veteran was T.M.’s father.  The Board concluded that the statements of the appellant and others were of less probative value than the results of genetics testing, which rendered it extremely unlikely that the veteran was T.M.’s father.

The appellant argued before the Court that the Board was prohibited from considering DNA test results to determine whether T.M. was the veteran’s child and that there was no requirement under [§ 3.210(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=a2ba4fce8d9f6af9cabb35e8987c88cd&node=se38.1.3_1210&rgn=div8) that her daughter be biologically the veteran’s “child” for VA purposes.

The Court agreed with the Secretary’s argument that the phrase “illegitimate child,” as used in § 3.210(b), encompassed a biological relationship between the parent and child.  The Court determined requiring a biological relationship was consistent with the regulation’s provisions of “sufficiency of evidence” or the evidence listed as “proof of … relationship.”

The Court held that because it concluded an illegitimate child must be biologically related to his parents, it was appropriate for the Board to consider evidence – DNA tests – related to the question of that biological relationship.  The Court noted that the vast majority of cases it reviewed indicated that courts are strongly in favor of determining biological paternity by use of DNA tests.  The Court emphasized that it was not holding that VA must always rely exclusively on the result of a DNA test, as the relationship may be proven by other evidence, nor was it holding that VA can require a claimant to submit to a DNA test.

The Court affirmed the Board’s May 2007 decision.
