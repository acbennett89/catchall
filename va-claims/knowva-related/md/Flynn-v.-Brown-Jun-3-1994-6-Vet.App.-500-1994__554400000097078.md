Decision Assessment Document
Flynn v. Brown, Jun 3, 1994, 6 Vet.App. 500 (1994)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that rules of hearsay evidence are not applicable to the VA decision-making process.   Double hearsay evidence (in this case, an oral statement by a physician relayed through the written statement of another physician) can be used to establish well groundedness.  The Court also held that in order for a VBA Circular to have the force and effect of law, it must prescribe substantive rules and conform to certain procedural requirements.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court's holding has been incorporated into the Manual as guidance under III.iv.5.A.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran filed a claim seeking secondary service connection for hypertension resulting from service-connected diabetes mellitus in April 1990.  During a VA examination, a VA physician diagnosed diabetes mellitus and hypertension.  The physician noted that he had consulted with an endocrinologist who felt "that if there were no kidney manifestations shown by the laboratory tests and the KUB that it was unlikely that the diabetes was the cause of the hypertension."  The claim was denied by the regional office and by the Board of Veterans Appeals (Board) and was appealed to the Court.  The Court granted the Secretary's unopposed motion for remand in May 1992.  Following readjudication, the Board affirmed the denial of secondary service connection.

The Court noted that, contrary to veteran's assertion, the Board panel did not rely upon its own unsubstantiated medical conclusions, but rather relied on a VA medical examination which indicated that hypertension was not related to the service-connected diabetes.  The veteran also argued that the Boarddecision rested on hearsay because the endocrinologist gave his opinion verbally and did not actually examine appellant or his medical records.  The Court discounted this argument on the basis that the VA adjudication process is paternalistic and nonadversarial.  Normal rules of evidence do not apply, and there is no case law standing for the proposition that, in general, evidence should be ruled inadmissable on hearsay grounds.

The veteran also argued that the Board erred by failing to apply VBA Circular 21-91-1, Recently Discovered Army Records (Jan. 14, 1991).  This circular contains examples of effective dates for the grant of service connection when warranted by recently recovered service records, including one where service connection is established for diabetes and for hypertension on a secondary basis.  The Court stated that not all agency policy pronouncements, including circulars and handbooks, can be considered regulations enforceable in federal court.  In order for a circular to have the force and effect of law, it must prescribe substantive rules and conform to certain procedural requirements.  In this case, the Board was not bound to grant secondary service connection for hypertension because the circular concerned the recent discovery of certain service medical records, not a presumption that service connection should always be granted for hypertension as secondary to service-connected diabetes, and because the circular is a procedural guide for the consideration of claims based on newly discovered service medical records.

In consideration of the evidence submitted on behalf of the veteran, the Court noted that a medical treatise was quoted for the proposition that diabetes "is universally accepted as a cause of cardiovascular disease [(CVD)] and hypertension is often the first sign of CVD."  The Board rejected this testimony on the basis that no objective evidence had been submitted which established a medical relationship in this particular case between the service-connected diabetes and hypertension.  The medical treatise quoted listed several components of CVD, i.e., congenital heart disease, rheumatic heart disease, coronary heart disease, and cerebrovascular disease, but not hypertension.  Thus, the Board's reasons or bases for rejecting the medical opinion were not clearly erroneous, and the Board decision was affirmed.
