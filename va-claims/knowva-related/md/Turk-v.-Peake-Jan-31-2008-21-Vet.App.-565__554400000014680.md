Decision Assessment Document
 Turk v. Peake, Jan 31, 2008, 21 Vet.App. 565
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that when a veteran fails to report for a VA examination after appealing the initial disability rating, such claim is considered an “original compensation claim”, rather than a claim for increase, and should be rated on the evidence of record in accordance with [38 C.F.R. § 3.655(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=44178dba248b16734ebd5a93fc31a231&node=se38.1.3_1655&rgn=div8).

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual revision.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran filed a claim for service connection for post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in September 2003.  In support of his claim, he submitted a letter from a private psychologist that disclosed multiple Axis I diagnoses to include PTSD and other disorders.  A Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) score of 41 was given.  The regional office (RO) granted service connection for PTSD with a 30 percent evaluation.  The veteran appealed the disability rating.  The RO later increased the evaluation to 50 percent.  The private psychologist provided another letter in November 2004.

The RO, in December 2004, advised the veteran that the private psychologist’s letters were inadequate for rating purposes because they did not include a discussion of the symptomatology attributable to PTSD and reflected multiple diagnoses without providing a separate GAF score for PTSD or explaining why separate GAF assignments could not be given.  The RO further informed the veteran that a VA examination was being scheduled and that his claim would be denied if he failed to report.  In a letter to the veteran the following month, the RO informed him that an examination had been scheduled at the Omaha, Nebraska, VAMC and that failure to report may have adverse consequences, to include possible denial of his claim.

The veteran’s counsel requested an examination closer to the veteran’s residence, citing his inability to travel long distances unassisted due to a variety of reasons to include medical conditions and advanced age.  The veteran failed to report to the examination scheduled at the Omaha VAMC.

In February 2005, the private psychologist provided another letter in which he discussed the veteran’s PTSD symptomatology.  The RO notified the veteran the same month that it had scheduled another VA examination at the Grand Island, Nebraska, VAMC.  The veteran’s counsel responded with a statement that the veteran was unable to travel without assistance the 25 miles to the Grand Island VAMC and that he had submitted private medical evidence that is sufficient for rating purposes.  Despite counsel’s letter to the RO, outpatient treatment notes from the Grand Island VAMC indicated the veteran appeared “in person” at the Grand Island VAMC for treatment unrelated to PTSD, but failed to report for the PTSD examination scheduled on the same day at the same facility.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) denied the veteran’s appeal in September 2005.

The Court, noting that correspondent to VA’s duty to assist is a duty on the part of the veteran to cooperate with VA in developing a claim, concluded that the veteran did not cooperate and is accountable for his willful failure to cooperate with VA in developing his claim.  The Court found that the veteran was indisputably present at the VAMC on the day the VA examination was scheduled to take place, but did not attend, giving an excuse that he was unable to drive to the VAMC.

The Court indicated that previous case law established that where a party appeals an original assignment of a disability rating, the claim is classified as an original claim, rather than as one for increased rating.  Thus, the Court determined that the Board should have classified the veteran’s PTSD claim as an original claim in appellate status, rather than as a freestanding claim for increased rating.  As such, the claim must be rated on the evidence of record in accordance with [§ 3.655(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=a01f8f15c9009585851719c6cc477a5e&node=se38.1.3_1655&rgn=div8).

Although the Court determined that the Board committed legal error, it found that the error was nonprejudicial because the Board proceeded to make findings and determinations to explain why the medical evidence of record did not support a higher disability rating.  Although the Board considered the veteran’s claim as an increased rating claim, it nevertheless complied with § 3.655(b) and evaluated the medical evidence of record and explained why it was insufficient to warrant a higher evaluation.

The Court also took notice of the Board’s comment that the veteran may have intentionally elected not to attend the scheduled VA examinations.  The Court indicated that if the veteran failed to appear at the examination based on counsel’s advice, he made an informed evidentiary choice and assumed the risk of such choice as explicated in § 3.655.

The Court affirmed the Board’s September 2005 decision.
