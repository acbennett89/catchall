Decision Assessment Document
 Taylor v. McDonald, Nov 25, 2014, 27 Vet.App. 158 (2014)
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the application of 38 C.F.R. 3.307(d) to veterans who were exposed to herbicides during active service in the Korean DMZ is a valid exercise of the Secretary’s authority to promulgate evidentiary regulations under 38 U.S.C. 501(a)(1).

The Court also held that 3.309(e), insofar as it establishes a rebuttable presumption of service connection for veterans exposed to herbicides in service outside of Vietnam, does not conflict with 38 U.S.C. 1113 and represents a reasonable interpretation by VA of its authority under sections 501, 1113, and 1116 that is not arbitrary and capricious.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served from September 1967 to May 1969, which included 13 months in Korea.  Treatment records from 2009 showed a diagnosis of “Non-Hodgkin’s B-cell lymphoma secondary to Hepatitis C.”  The veteran filed a claim for service connection for non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL), due to Agent Orange exposure, in August 2009.  The regional office denied the claim on the basis of lack of evidence of Vietnam service.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in July 2012, requested a VA specialist’s opinion based on a finding that the evidence indicated that the veteran had been exposed to herbicides “while working in and around” the Korean DMZ.  The Board noted that the herbicide exposure created a presumption of service connection for the veteran’s lymphoma unless there is affirmative evidence to the contrary.

A VA oncologist, in August 2012, indicated that the absence of past studies would make determination of etiology of the veteran’s disorder impossible.  The physician opined:  “I believe that the evidence suggests that the hepatitis C infection led to the development of the lymphoma.”

The Board, in December 2012, found that the veteran was exposed to Agent Orange in service, but also noted that the August 2012 VA medical opinion confirmed that it was likely that the veteran’s intervening hepatitis C infection led to the development of lymphoma and determined that this evidence was sufficient to rebut the presumption of service connection.  The Board considered the positive opinions submitted by the veteran, but found the August 2012 VA medical opinion to be more probative.  The Board also found that the evidence was insufficient to establish a nexus to herbicide exposure on a direct basis, in the absence of an applicable presumption.

The Board denied the appeal, finding that the most probative evidence indicated that the veteran’s NHL resulted from hepatitis C, and not herbicide exposure in service, and the evidence did not show that the NHL was otherwise related to service.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board was statutorily precluded from rebutting the regulatory presumption of service connection for his lymphoma because the authority to promulgate a rebuttal provision as to Korean DMZ herbicide claimants because such claimants are not referenced in section 1113, which is the specific authority for the Secretary to promulgate presumption-rebuttal regulations based on geographic locale and disability type.  He contended that VA’s regulations, 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.307(a)(6)(iv) and 3.307(d), providing for rebuttal of the presumption are ultra vires, or constituted administrative action beyond and outside of the Secretary’s express or implied delegated authority in section 1113, as applied to veterans like himself who are entitled to a presumption of service connection based on their service in the Korean DMZ.

At the outset, the Court noted that § 3.307(a)(6)(iv), promulgated by VA in 2011, provides for a presumption of exposure to herbicides for qualifying veterans who served in the Korean DMZ; it does not establish a presumption of service connection.  In contrast, VA established a presumption of service connection for NHL in 1994 when it extended 38 C.F.R. § 3.309(e) beyond veterans who served in Vietnam to include all other veterans exposed to herbicides during active service, including those who served in the Korean DMZ.  Similarly, VA’s promulgation of § 3.307(d) did not create a rebuttal framework specifically for Korean DMZ claimants with presumed herbicide exposure, but applies to any claimant with herbicide exposure – presumed or actual – and already existed when VA created the presumption of herbicide exposure for qualifying Korean DMZ veterans in 2011.

The Court agreed with the veteran that section 1113 does not reference the service-connection presumption applicable to NHL; however, VA did not rely solely on section 1113 when it promulgated § 3.309(e), as it also designated 38 U.S.C. § 501(a) as the authority for the rule, which provides the Secretary with the authority to “prescribe all rules and regulations which are necessary or appropriate to carry out the laws administrated by VA, as well as regulations with respect to the nature and extent of proof evidence in order to establish the right to benefits under such laws.”

The Court held that the provision of § 3.309(e) providing for the rebuttal of the presumption of service connection applicable to NHL does not conflict with section 1113.  The Court explained that section 1113 does not purport to limit the Secretary’s authority; rather, it provides for the potential rebuttal of the specific presumptions of service connection established in sections 1112 and 1116-1118.  Section 1113 is silent with respect to other presumptions of service connection crated by the Secretary under the general authority granted by section 501(a).  The Court held that § 3.309(e), insofar as it establishes a rebuttable presumption of service connection for veterans exposed to herbicides in service outside of Vietnam, does not conflict with section 1113 and represents a reasonable interpretation by VA of its authority under sections 501, 1113, and 1116, that is not arbitrary and capricious.

The Court did agree with the veteran that the Board failed to provide an adequate statements or reasons or bases for its finding that the evidence of record was sufficient to rebut the presumption of service connection for NHL.  The Court found that the Board erred in determining that the August 2012 VA examiner’s opinion met the second rebuttal standard under § 3.307(d) – “affirmative evidence to the contrary” – as the examiner did not explicitly opine that the veteran’s NHL was not incurred in service.  The Board focused on the examiner’s discussion regarding the effect of the veteran’s hepatitis C on his NHL and mischaracterized the opinion by confusing the possibility that the hepatitis C led to the development of lymphoma with the probability that it did so.  Although the VA examiner stated that the evidence suggested that the hepatitis C infection led to the development of NHL, the Board cited the opinion for the proposition that it was likely that the hepatitis C led to the development of the NHL.

The Court indicated that the distinction between possibility and probability is important, particularly in the context of the rebuttal of a regulatory presumption of service connection, and the Board’s failure to recognize the distinction frustrated judicial review.  The Court also found that the Board failed to discuss the examiner’s concurrent findings that the absence of past studies would make a determination of the etiology of the NHL impossible and the risk of developing a tumor is greater in those exposed to Agent Orange, even for those with a normal immune system.  The Board also neglected to discuss the opinion’s lack of specificity as to the probability that hepatitis C was the sole cause of the NHL, to the exclusion of Agent Orange exposure as a contributory cause.

The Court vacated and remanded the Board’s decision for readjudication consistent with its decision.
