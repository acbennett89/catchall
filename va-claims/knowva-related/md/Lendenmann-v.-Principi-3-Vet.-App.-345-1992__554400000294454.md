**Decision Assessment Document**

***Lendenmann v. Principi*, 3 Vet. App. 345 (1992)**

**U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)**

**What the case is about:**

The Veteran appealed a Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) decision that denied a claim for increased compensable evaluations for right ear deafness and balance disorder. The Court held the Veteran failed to establish that the Board’s denial of the claim for a compensable evaluation for service-connected hearing loss was clearly erroneous. The Court also held that the Board’s failure to provide adequate reasons or bases for its choice of diagnostic code for the Veteran’s unlisted balance disorder warranted remand.

**Impact on VBA:**

The M21-1 Adjudication Procedures Manual will be updated to reference this decision.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The Veteran reported a reduced sense of balance after a service-connected coronary artery bypass graft surgery. A Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) neurological examination noted that the Veteran’s hearing in the right ear was markedly decreased to air conduction. A VA examiner attributed the Veteran’s balance problems to midline cerebellar degeneration. A May 1989 rating decision denied service connection for midline cerebellar degeneration secondary to treatment for service-connected heart disease. Subsequent medical information from a private physician indicated the Veteran’s balance problems were related to an infarction of a part of the medulla oblongata, and a private physician’s medical opinion indicated that the Veteran had a cerebellar injury likely caused during his cardiac surgery.

On September 5, 1990, the Board issued two separate decisions on the Veteran’s claims. The Board remanded a claim for increased evaluation for right ear deafness because the Veteran had not had an evaluation under the new rating criteria. The Board also granted service connection for a balance disorder that was proximately due to or the result of a service-connected disease or injury.

In September 1990 rating decision, the regional office (RO) assigned a noncompensable evaluation for the balance disorder secondary to coronary artery bypass surgery under diagnostic code 8099-6204; thus, under the historical rating schedule, the RO rated the balance disorder analogous to chronic labyrinthitis. (*See* 38 C.F.R. § 4.87a, DC 6204 (1991)). The Veteran timely filed a Notice of Disagreement (NOD). After receiving a Statement of the Case (SOC), the Veteran filed an appeal to the Board with a comment that the SOC appeared to attribute his balance problem to the right ear condition. The Veteran maintained that his balance problem was the result of his open-heart surgeries and not the result of an operation to his right ear.

Regarding the September 1990 Board remand regarding right ear deafness, the VA administered additional examinations in November 1990. The RO confirmed a noncompensable evaluation for right ear hearing loss in a November 1990 rating decision.

In a January 1991 Rating Decision on Appeal, VA confirmed the decisions denying increased (compensable) evaluations for the balance disorder and for right ear hearing loss. Subsequently, VA received an undated Informal Hearing Memorandum wherein the Veteran’s service representative suggested the Veteran’s balance disorder would be more properly rated by analogy to diagnostic codes 8007-8009, which would recognize the cardiovascular basis for service connection and require a minimum rating of 10 percent. The representative also expressed general disagreement with the rating criteria for hearing loss evaluations.

The Court first considered the denial of the claim for a compensable evaluation for service-connected hearing loss and found no error by VA or the Board in applying the rating schedule to the results of the Veteran’s audiometric examination. The Court affirmed the Board decision denying a compensable evaluation for the Veteran’s hearing loss.

The Court next reviewed the Board’s rationale that diagnostic codes 8007 and 8009 were not representative of the actual manifestations associated with the cerebral ischemic injury and the Board’s conclusion that, after exploring other possible options for rating the balance disorder, the diagnostic code for labyrinthitis provided the most accurate representation of disability. Because the Board did not provide reasons or bases for favoring one diagnostic code over others, the Court found that effective judicial review was frustrated. The Court noted that all medical evidence of record described the cause of the Veteran’s balance disorder as something that happened inside his brain during coronary artery surgery, and it noted that none of the evidence pointed to any problems with the Veteran’s ears as the cause of the balance disorder. The Board chose an analogous diagnostic code for an ailment with symptoms analogous to the Veteran’s symptoms, rather than a diagnostic code for an ailment analogous to the cause of the Veteran’s ailment. However, the Board did not explain the regulatory authority upon which it based its decision to use a diagnostic code analogous to symptoms rather than a diagnostic code analogous to the ailment itself.

The Court considered 38 C.F.R. § 4.20, which provides that it is permissible to rate an unlisted condition under “a closely related disease or injury in which not only the function affected, but the anatomical localization and symptomatology are closely analogous.” According to the Court, section 4.20 allows VA to rate an unlisted ailment under the criteria related to a closely related listed disease or injury. In deciding if an ailment is “closely related,” VA may consider three factors: (1) whether the functions affected by the ailments are analogous, (2) whether the anatomical location of the ailments is analogous, and (3) whether the symptomatology of the ailments is analogous. While the Board considered analogous symptomatology of the ailments, it did not explain how the anatomical location of labyrinthitis is more analogous to an ischemic brain injury than is a brain embolism or hemorrhage. Nor did the Board explain whether it considered the functions affected by the ailments and whether those functions were analogous. The Board did not explain why it gave more weight to one factor in the regulation than the other two factors, nor did it provide reasons or bases for why its regulatory analysis showed labyrinthitis, an ailment of the ear, to be a more closely related disorder for the ischemic brain injury than a brain embolism or brain hemorrhage would be. Because the Board failed to provide adequate reasons for its choice of an analogous diagnostic code, the Court remanded the matter for readjudication of the Veteran’s balance disorder.
