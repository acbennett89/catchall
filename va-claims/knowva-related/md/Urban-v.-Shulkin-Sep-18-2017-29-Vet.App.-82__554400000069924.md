Decision Assessment Document
Urban v. Shulkin, Sep 18, 2017, 29 Vet.App. 82
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court, holding the language of 38 C.F.R. § 4.96(a) to be ambiguous, deferred to the Secretary’s interpretation that VA is to evaluate coexisting service-connected respiratory conditions, which are covered by § 4.96(a), under the criteria enumerated in the predominant disability’s diagnostic code (DC).

The Court found the plain language of the regulation’s phrase “with elevation to the next higher evaluation” to be reasonably interpreted as referring to the next higher evaluation level of the predominant disability DC and that, to attain such elevation, the criteria listed in the in that evaluation level are key to assessing the severity of the overall disability from both respiratory conditions.  The Court concluded that the Secretary’s interpretation of the phrase allows VA, having determined the predominant respiratory disability, to assess the overall symptoms of the coexisting respiratory conditions against the criteria listed in the predominant disability’s diagnostic code.

The Court concluded that the Secretary’s interpretation was consistent with the language of the regulation, not plainly erroneous, and represented his fair and considered judgment on the matter.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran is service connected for obstructive sleep apnea with asthma, rated 60 percent disabling under DC 6602, as asthma has been identified as the predominant disability.  The record discloses that the asthma was treated with twice daily use of Symbicort (a combination of steroid and long-lasting beta-agonist) and Albuterol as needed.  A VA examination from March 2012 showed that treatment for the asthma required intermittent use of oral or parenteral corticosteroids of four or more courses in the last year and daily use of inhaled medications.  Regarding sleep apnea, it was shown that the veteran required continuous use of a continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) machine but still had occasional episodes of gasping for air.  The examiner determined asthma to be the predominant disability.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in September 2015, denied entitlement to an evaluation higher than 60 percent for the coexisting asthma and sleep apnea.  The Board defined asthma as the predominant disability because it provided the higher evaluation and noted that the March 2012 VA examination had found asthma to be the predominant disability.  The Board stated that it could not provide separate evaluations for asthma and sleep apnea under § 4.96(a), but had to assign a single evaluation based upon the predominant disability.  The Board considered the symptoms associated with both disabilities and concluded that, when considering the veteran’s respiratory symptoms as a whole, the severity of his overall respiratory disability did not warrant an increase of the 60 percent evaluation to the next higher evaluation of 100 percent under DC 6602.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board misapplied § 4.96(a) in that the specific criteria for a higher evaluation listed in the predominant disability diagnostic code need not be met before assigning the next higher evaluation.  The Secretary contended that the plain language of § 4.96(a) provides that a higher evaluation will be warranted only where the specific criteria and symptoms listed in the predominant disability’s diagnostic code are met.  The veteran also argued that the Board erred in relying on the fact that the veteran did not meet the criteria for a higher evaluation under DC 6602 to deny extra-schedular referral and that the Board failed to account for the use of Symbicort for asthma treatment, which is not listed under DC 6602.

The Court concluded that the plain language of the phrase “with elevation to the next higher evaluation” under § 4.96(a) to be ambiguous and that both parties had presented interpretations consistent with the regulatory language.

 The Court, in deferring to the Secretary’s interpretation, held that the language was reasonably interpreted as referring to the next higher evaluation level of the predominant disability diagnostic code and that to attain such evaluation, the criteria listed in that evaluation level are key to assessing the severity of the overall disability from both respiratory conditions.  The Court concluded that the Secretary’s interpretation of the phrase allows VA, having determined the predominant disability, to assess the overall symptoms of the coexisting respiratory conditions against the criteria listed in the predominant disability’s diagnostic code.

The Court also accepted the Secretary’s argument that the interpretation of § 4.96(a) as shown in the Manual 21-1 illustrated that Secretary’s fair and considered view on the matter.

The Court, in applying its holding to the facts of the case, found no error in the Board’s determinations that asthma was the veteran’s predominant disability, separate evaluations could not be assigned for asthma and sleep apnea in accordance with § 4.96(a), and a higher evaluation under DC 6602 was not warranted when considering the severity of his respiratory symptoms “as a whole.”

The Court rejected the veteran’s argument that the Board erred in not referring the case for extra-schedular evaluation based on his assertions that Symbicort showed marked interference with employment, as the Board had determined that referral for extra-schedular consideration was not warranted because the first element – showing an exceptional or unusual disability picture – under Thun v. Peake, 22 Vet.App. 111 (2008) because the diagnostic criteria encompassed all symptoms and related functional impairment of the disability shown during the applicable time frame and was therefore not inadequate.  The Court stated that there was no need to address the second Thun element, whether Symbicort caused marked interference with employment, as the Board had properly determined that the first Thun element had not been met.

The Court affirmed the Board’s decision.
