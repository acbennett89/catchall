Decision Assessment Document
 Petitti v. McDonald, Oct 28, 2015, 27 Vet.App. 415 (2015)
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that 38 C.F.R. 4.59 does not require objective evidence of painful motion for assignment of a minimal compensable evaluation for a joint.

The Court held that 38 C.F.R. 4.71a, Diagnostic Code (DC) 5002 does require objective evidence of painful motion.  However, satisfactory evidence of pain must be objectively confirmed, under DC 5002, from a person other than the veteran or claimant, either a medical examiner or a lay person.

**Impact on VBA:**

Manual M21-1, III.iv.4.A will be revised in accordance with the Court’s opinion to provide that § 4.59 – not requiring objective evidence of painful motion for a compensable evaluation – is applicable to all musculoskeletal disabilities, irrespective of the diagnostic code that has already been assigned to the disability.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, whose rheumatoid arthritis (RA) had been rated 20 percent disabled under Diagnostic Code (DC) 5002 since 1995, filed a claim for increased evaluation in February 2007.  During an April 2007 VA examination, the veteran reported pain and stiffness in his hands and that he had experienced more than six flare-ups in the past year.  The veteran had full range of motion in his hands, feet, and ankles, which did not decrease with repetitive motion.  The examiner did conclude that the RA significantly affected his occupational ability because of decreased mobility, manual dexterity, lack of stamina, and increased tardiness and also affected his daily activities.  The RO denied a higher evaluation.

Following a Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) remand in August 2011, the veteran underwent another VA examination in October 2011.  The examiner noted pain and stiffness of the joints, to include the ankles, knees, hands, and back.  However, the veteran had full range of motion of joints without painful motion during range-of-motion testing or following repetitive testing.  The examiner did indicate that the veteran had painful joints and that the RA caused decreased mobility, weakness or fatigue, and pain.  The RO, in November 2012, increased the disability rating for RA to 40 percent from October 11, 2011.

Following the Court’s granting of a joint motion for remand, the veteran submitted an affidavit attesting that arthritis affected every joint in his body, but was worse in his feet, ankles, knees, hips, fingers, and wrists.  He stated that he had joint pain, flare-ups and described limitations with walking, sitting, and standing due to his RA.  The veteran’s daughter also submitted an affidavit that attested to his difficulties with functioning due to joint pain.

The Board, in its November 2013 decision, found that the veteran’s complaints of joint pain were credible and competent.  However, the Board denied entitlement to a higher evaluation for RA.  The Board concluded that because the VA examinations did not show limited motion on range of motion testing or objective evidence of pain on movement even following repetitive motion testing, the veteran was not entitled to a 10 percent disability rating for each joint under § 4.59.

The veteran and Secretary agreed that § 4.59, and, in this case DC 5002, work in tandem to authorize a minimum compensable rating of 10 percent for each joint.  However, the parties did not agree as to what constitutes painful motion and what evidence the Board is required to consider when making its determination.

The veteran argued that the Board was required to consider all lay and medical evidence regarding pain; therefore, the Board’s decision should be reversed due to failure to account for the lay evidence regarding his joint pain.  The Secretary argued that painful motion is required under § 4.59 and the mere presence of joint pain is not sufficient.  Thus, since the VA examinations did not show painful motion during range-of-motion testing, the Board decision should be affirmed.

The Court, at the outset, observed that, reading § 4.59 and DC 5002 together, it could not conceive of a situation in which an “actually painful” joint would not necessarily connote a joint that was painful on motion.  The Court then referred to Lichtenfels v. Derwinski, 1 Vet.App. 484 (1991), which it described as holding that § 4.59 serves as a bridge linking painful motion and limitation of motion, which results in painful motion being  considered limited motion under DC 5003 even though actual motion is not limited.

The Court, because of the similarities between DCs 5002 and 5003 pertaining to the assignment of a minimum rating for noncompensable limitation of motion, concluded that Lichtenfels holding of the effect of § 4.59 on DC 5003 also applies to DC 5002.  The Court held that when DC 5002 is read in conjunction with § 4.59, painful motion of a joint is deemed to be limited motion of that joint, and evidence of painful motion satisfies the requirement for limited motion under DC 5002.  Thus, a claimant with RA who demonstrated painful motion of a joint is entitled to the minimum disability rating for that joint under DC 5002 and § 4.59, even though the claimant does not have actual limitation of motion.

The Court next turned to the question of what constitutes painful motion and what type of evidence is sufficient under DC 5002 and § 4.59 to verify painful motion.  The Court agreed that § 4.59 does not require objective evidence of painful motion; however, it found that DC 5002 requires that limitation of motion must be corroborated by a person other than the veteran based upon that person’s observations, which may be satisfied by lay statements or evidence.  The Court explained that because “satisfactory evidence of painful motion” under the regulation is broad, it not only includes a doctor’s observations of painful motion, but also encompasses lay descriptions of a veteran’s painful motion.

The Court held that since DC 5002 requires that “satisfactory evidence of pain” be “objectively confirmed,” a veteran’s testimony, alone, is not enough.  There must also be objective confirmation from a person other than the veteran or claimant, be it a medical examiner or a lay person, of a veteran’s joint pain.  The Court agreed with the Secretary that DC 5002 requires objective evidence of painful motion, but rejected the argument that confirmation is limited to range-of-motion tests.

In applying its holding to the facts of the case, the Court observed that the record was replete with medical evidence and lay testimony regarding the veteran’s painful joints, and that the Board found the lay evidence that RA is manifested by dull and throbbing joint pain to be “competent and credible.”

The Court held that the Board had erred in concluding that painful motion may be established only by an examiner’s findings on range-of-motion tests.  The Court also held that Board clearly erred in determining that the veteran did not have objective evidence of painful motion in view of the Board’s finding that the veteran was credible, its acceptance of the lay evidence from his daughter regarding her observations as to the effects of the veteran’s joint pain on his daily activities, and its notation as to the objective findings made by several VA examiners.

The Court reversed the Board’s finding that the veteran did not have objective evidence of painful motion and remanded the case to the Board to determine the specific joints affected by RA and whether the veteran would receive a higher disability rating for the chronic residuals of his RA.
