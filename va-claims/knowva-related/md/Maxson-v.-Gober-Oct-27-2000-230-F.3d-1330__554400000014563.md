Decision Assessment Document
Maxson v. Gober, Oct 27, 2000, 230 F.3d 1330
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

Rebutting presumption of aggravation:  [Section 1154(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1154) permits consideration of a lengthy period without medical complaint when determining whether a condition has been aggravated by service.

**Impact on VBA:**

Significant.  Consider adding this case to the Summary of Significant holdings – under “Evidence, Combat” for the following.  When rebutting the presumption of aggravation of a condition incurred during combat, section 1154(b) permits consideration of the veteran’s entire medical history, including a lengthy period of absence of complaint (following service) about the claimed condition.

**Summary of the facts and Court's reasons:**

The veteran had a partial colectomy prior to his entry into service in 1939.  During service, the veteran participated in combat in Guadalcanal, where service medical records (SMRs) showed that he was treated for malaria and suffered “four episodes of colicky abdominal pain accompanied by nausea, much vomiting”.  SMRs also showed the veteran had a bowel obstruction in January 1942, and was given a barium enema (for colon x-rays) in March 1943 after being hospitalized for diphtheria.  A July 1945 separation examination noted the history of a partial colectomy, and stated that there was “no abdominal tenderness”, and listed no symptoms or disability relating to the colectomy.  The veteran was discharged in September 1945.

The veteran filed his first claim seeking benefits for service-related aggravation of his colon problems in 1989.  VA applied the provisions of 38 U.S.C.§ 1154(b) (relating to a presumption of service connection for combat veterans) to the veteran’s case, but denied the claim, finding that the presumption of aggravation was rebutted by clear and convincing evidence.  The Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (CAVC) affirmed the denial finding it decisive that despite numerous pre-1944 entries regarding the veteran’s symptomatology, the evidence of record reveals that from 1944 to 1989 there is no record of any complaint, let alone treatment, involving the veteran’s colon condition.  The veteran appealed, arguing that, as a matter of law, the absence of evidence of treatment or complaint concerning his colon condition after 1944 cannot be clear and convincing evidence that he did not suffer a service-connected aggravation of his colon condition.   The veteran asked the Federal Circuit to establish the rule of law, interpreting 38 U.S.C.§ 1154(b), that only positive evidence of non-aggravation can rebut the presumption of service-connected aggravation.

The Federal Circuit (Court) affirmed the holding of the CAVC.  The Court noted that it was not in dispute that the veteran established a presumption of service-connected aggravation of his colon condition.  This presumption of aggravation could only be overcome by clear and convincing evidence to the contrary.  The veteran asked the Court to rule, as a matter of statutory interpretation of section 1154(b), that the presumption could be overcome only by positive medical evidence of non-aggravation.  The Court agreed with the CAVC that evidence of a prolonged period without medical complaint can be considered, along with other factors concerning the veteran’s health and medical treatment during and after military service, as evidence of whether a pre-existing condition was aggravated by military service.  Whether an evidentiary presumption has been rebutted is a matter of the weight of all of the evidence, in light of the particular facts and circumstances.  [Section 1154(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1154) permits consideration of the veteran’s entire medical history, including the lengthy period of absence of complaint about the claimed condition.
