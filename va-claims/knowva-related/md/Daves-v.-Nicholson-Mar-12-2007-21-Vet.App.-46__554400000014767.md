Decision Assessment Document
 Daves v. Nicholson, Mar 12, 2007, 21 Vet.App. 46
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that if a medical examiner specifically states that a medical opinion cannot be provided without information currently unavailable, VA’s duty to assist requires that VA determine whether that information may be reasonably obtained.  If so, VA is to make efforts to obtain it and seek an additional medical opinion which considers the relevant information.

The Court, noting that communication between VA and a claimant is a critical component of the adjudication process, also held that due process requires VA to notify the claimant prior to the adjudication of a claim of VA’s inability to obtain evidence, such as evidence necessary to facilitate a medical opinion, that it has undertaken to obtain.  This notification is required so that the claimant has a fair and reasonable opportunity to try and secure the evidence or procure alternative evidence at a time when it would be most useful to the adjudicator.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran was service connected for post traumatic stress disorder, rated 70 percent disabling; migraine headaches, rated 50 percent disabling; diabetes mellitus, rated 20 percent disabling; and low back syndrome, rated 10 percent disabling, for a combined schedular evaluation of 90 percent at the time of his death in July 2002.  A total evaluation on the basis of individual unemployability had been in effect since September 1997.

In May 2002, the veteran sought treatment for chest pain at a private medical facility.  The cause of the pain was not determined; however, abnormally high blood sugar was found.  The medical report noted that uncontrolled high blood sugar leads to early heart disease, strokes, and damage of the nerves, eyes, and kidneys.  In July 2002, the veteran was found dead in his car at a highway rest stop in Texas with the keys in the ignition.  The responding police officer opined that the veteran seemed to know something was wrong and “pulled into the rest stop, parked the vehicle and passed away.”  The police report also indicated that blood had pooled to the veteran’s lower legs and left hand.  Cause of death was described as of undetermined origin and manner of death was characterized as natural.  No autopsy was performed.

The surviving spouse, in her claim for DIC, contended that her husband suffered debilitating effects of diabetes to include cardiovascular involvement and requested an independent medical opinion.

A VA medical examiner, in January 2003, upon review of the claims file, death certificate, and police report, opined that the cause of death was unknown, “and without an autopsy, cannot even be speculated upon.”  The examiner stated that the death described in the police report was consistent with dozens of different causes (both natural and unnatural) and concluded that there was no evidence to support or rule out a relationship between the veteran’s death and a service-connected disability.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) upheld the regional office denial in October 2004.  The appellant argued before the Court that VA failed in its duty to assist by not obtaining an autopsy after the VA medical examiner opined that the cause of death could not be determined without an autopsy and the Board erred by not addressing whether the duty to assist included obtaining further medical opinion, including an autopsy report.

The Court found that the Board’s reasons or bases were inadequate by not clarifying whether a current post-burial autopsy would be helpful to the medical examiner and by not addressing whether providing an autopsy falls under the duty to assist in [38 U.S.C. § 5103A](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A).  The Court noted that the VA examiner’s statement could be read to mean either (1) that an autopsy conducted immediately after the veteran's death would be required to make a determination and therefore an opinion could not ever be rendered as to the cause of death, or (2) an opinion could be given as to the cause of death if an autopsy were presently conducted.

The Court held that, when a medical examiner specifically states that a medical opinion cannot be provided without information not currently available, the duty to assist requires VA to determine whether the information may be reasonably obtained, and if so, to make efforts to obtain such information and seek another medical opinion considering the relevant information.

Furthermore, as the appellant was not informed by VA that the medical opinion could not be procured due to the absence of an autopsy, the Court further held that due process required VA to notify the claimant of this inability to obtain such evidence prior to adjudication so the claimant has a fair and reasonable opportunity to try and obtain such evidence or procure alternative evidence.

The Court labeled as confusing the Board’s characterization of the medical opinion as speculative and “non-evidence” while at the same time concluding that the medical opinion was the “only medical evidence” of record that bears directly on the question of what constituted the cause of death.  Also, in light of the examiner’s opinion that there was no evidence to rule out or support a relationship between death and a service-connected disability, the Court found the Board to have not adequately explained its conclusion that a preponderance of the evidence was against a finding that the cause of death was incurred in service or is otherwise related thereto.

The Court vacated the Board decision and remanded for further adjudication.
