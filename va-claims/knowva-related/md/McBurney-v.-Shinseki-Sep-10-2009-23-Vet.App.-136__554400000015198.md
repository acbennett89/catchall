Decision Assessment Document
 McBurney v. Shinseki, Sep 10, 2009, 23 Vet.App. 136
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that for the purpose of determining Prisoner-of-War (POW) status for a veteran, the definition of “detain” under [38 U.S.C. § 101(32)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/101) must include elements of “restraint” or “custody.”

The Court also held implicit or express agreements between VA claimants, which do not involve the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) or courts, remain subject to the Board’s de novo review on appeal.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served from 1943 to 1946, was participating in his unit’s operation to recapture territory in 1944 from German forces. During the operation, he was ordered by his commander to leave his weapon, go to a nearby meadow, and take three enemy soldiers prisoner whom the commander believed were seeking to surrender. The veteran made contact with the German soldiers and advised that he would take their surrender. The soldiers responded that they were not surrendering and, instead, that the veteran was their prisoner. After further verbal exchanges, a German soldier attempted to take hold of the veteran; however, he wrestled away, diving behind a tree stump as a German soldier fired at him. After dusk, the veteran was approached by American soldiers, and re-integrated into friendly lines.

The veteran argued before the Court that these events established his POW status before VA and the Board had erred in denying such status. He contended that the Board overlooked and overturned six prior favorable VA findings, including “stipulations” that he was “forcibly detained,” and impermissibly reconsidered that issue, which had already resolved by the parties or by VA. The veteran stated that the Board erred in readdressing these issues sua sponte when it had not been raised or briefed to the Board.

The Court rejected the veteran’s argument that the Board must limit its analysis to issues raised before it in the Substantive Appeal, briefs, and other arguments. The Court indicated that once the Board has jurisdiction over a claim, it has the authority to address all issues related to that claim.

Likewise, the Court also dismissed the veteran’s argument that the Board was prohibited from overturning or reconsidering matters favorably decided by the RO or agreed between the parties. The Court indicated that the Board, as the final trier of fact, is not constrained by favorable determinations below. The Court referenced [*Anderson v. Shinseki*, 22 Vet.App. 423 (2009)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000015164/), in deciding that the Board did not act outside its scope of authority in implicitly overturning findings below. The Court found that the Board thoroughly explained why the undisputed facts precluded a finding that the veteran was forcibly detained. In doing so, it had implicitly rejected any of the terms allegedly agreed to by the RO and the veteran on the issue of forcible detention.

Note: In *Anderson*, the Court had rejected the veteran’s argument that the Board could not disregard favorable findings made by a decision review officer as to the three elements that must be established prior to the assignment of an extraschedular rating. The Court noted that [38 U.S.C. § 7104](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/7104) contained no limitations on the Board’s ability to review favorable finings and that the Board was permitted to review the entirety of the proceedings below.

The Court, in concluding that the definition of “detain” under [section 101(32)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/101) must include elements of “restraint” or “custody,” found that the Board’s analysis that the veteran had not been forcibly detained or interned was not erroneous. The Board had analyzed the various stages of the encounter between the veteran and the German soldiers and had concluded that there was no actual detention and the encounter did not constitute detainment or internment under the statute.

The Court observed that the detrimental effects associated with POW internment (malnutrition, lack of adequate medical care, unsanitary conditions, etc.) had concerned Congress and concluded that the circumstances surrounding the veteran’s encounter with the German soldiers was clearly distinguishable from the circumstances that Congress envisioned when enacting the POW statutes authorizing a presumption of service connection for those deemed POWs.

The Court affirmed the Board’s July 2008 decision.
