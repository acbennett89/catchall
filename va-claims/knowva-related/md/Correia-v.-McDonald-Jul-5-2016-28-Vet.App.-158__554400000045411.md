Decision Assessment Document
 Correia v. McDonald, Jul 5, 2016, 28 Vet.App. 158
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the final sentence of 38 C.F.R. § 4.59requires that certain range of motion testing be conducted whenever possible in evaluating joint disabilities.

Note: The last sentence of § 4.59 reads:  “The joints involved should be tested for pain on both active and passive motion, in weight-bearing and nonweight-bearing and, if possible, with the range of the opposite undamaged joint.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.  However, in compliance with the Court’s opinion, it must be ensured that the joint disability at issue was evaluated for pain in both active and passive motion, to include weight-bearing as well.  This range of motion testing must include the contralateral joint as well.  For example, if the examination is conducted in conjunction with a claim for a left knee condition, the same testing must be applied to the right knee as well during the examination.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, in October 2007, filed for increased evaluation for his bilateral knee disabilities.  A 30 percent evaluation for post-operative residuals of a right knee meniscectomy/degenerative joint disease and 20 percent evaluation for degenerative joint disease of the left knee were in effect at the time.  Following a December 2007 VA examination, the regional office (RO) denied the claim.  The RO, in April 2011, reduced the evaluation for the left knee disability to 10 percent.

Following a Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) remand, the veteran underwent another VA examination in September 2012.  The examiner reviewed the veteran’s medical history, described the effect of the knee disabilities on his gait, noted the effects of flare-ups, conducted various range of motion studies and muscle strength tests, and considered the functional impairment caused by the knee disabilities.

The Board, in September 2013, determined that the reduction in evaluation for the left knee disability was in error and restored the 20 percent evaluation.  However, the Board also determined that higher evaluations for both knee disabilities were unwarranted and denied the claims.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board’s determination that the September 2012 VA examination report was adequate was clearly erroneous because the examination report did not contain all of the range of motion findings, as required by § 4.59, for the Board to competently determine the appropriate disability rating.

The Secretary argued that § 4.59 does not establish a rigid set of protocols for VA examinations.  The Secretary also maintained that if the Court found the regulation to be ambiguous, substantial deference should be given to his interpretation because it is reasonable and not contrary to the plain meaning of the regulation.

The Court, at the outset, indicated that it read the final sentence as explaining the kinds of test results that should be obtained to permit an adjudicator to assess the effect of painful motion – range of motion tests for both active and passive motion, and in both weight-bearing and nonweight-bearing circumstances – and that the question before it is what “should” means in the final sentence of § 4.59.

The Court, founding that, in the context of § 4.59, “should” is susceptible to more than one meaning, concluded that this portion of the regulation is ambiguous.  However, the Court held that no deference was due to the Secretary’s interpretation that the regulation creates no requirement regarding kinds of range of motion testing to be performed in evaluating joint disabilities.  The Court’s decision to not extend deference was based upon the Secretary having regularly argued a contrary position before the Court consistent with the veteran’s argument that § 4.59 creates a testing requirement for which VA examiners must comply.

The Court, in rejecting the Secretary’s argument for deference for his interpretation, held that the final sentence of § 4.59 creates a requirement that certain range of motion testing be conducted whenever possible in cases of joint disabilities.  The Court stated that it was left with the inescapable conclusion that, to be adequate, a VA examination of the joints must, whenever possible, include the results of the range of motion testing described in the final sentence of § 4.59.

Note: The Court indicated in footnotes that it would leave it to medical professionals to determine whether the listed range of motion testing under § 4.59 can be performed on the joints at issue in a particular case.  The Court, in responding to the Secretary’s arguments that upper body joints cannot be tested for weight-bearing capacity because the upper extremities are not weight-bearing joints, accepted that whether upper extremities are or can be weight-bearing is a medical question that it is not competent to answer.  The Court indicated that it trusted its decision will be taken as requiring the range of motion testing listed din the final sentence of § 4.59 in every case in which those tests can be conducted.

The Court also held that § 4.59 outlines three things examiners should do to ensure that VA adjudicators have all of the information necessary to properly evaluate a joint disability:  (1) carefully note facial expression or wincing on pressure or manipulation and relate that to the affected joint: (2) carefully note crepitation in the soft tissues or joint structures; and (3) test for pain throughout range of motion in various ways.

The Court observed that the Secretary did not dispute that none of the medical evidence of record contained results of all of the range of motion testing required by the final sentence of § 4.59.  Therefore, in applying its holding to the facts of the case, the Court concluded that the Board’s determination that VA examinations of record were adequate and VA had satisfied its duty to assist to be clearly erroneous.

The Court vacated the Board decision and remanded the case with instructions that the veteran be afforded a new examination that will record the results of range of motion testing for pain non both active and passive motion and in weight-bearing and nonweight-bearing, as well as the necessary findings to evaluate functional loss during flare-ups.  The examiner will be expected to provide a clear explanation if unable to conducted the required testing or concludes it to be unnecessary.
