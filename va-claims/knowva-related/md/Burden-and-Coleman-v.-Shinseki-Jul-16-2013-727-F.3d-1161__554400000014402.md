Decision Assessment Document
Burden and Coleman v. Shinseki, Jul 16, 2013, 727 F.3d 1161
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court correctly determined that [38 U.S.C. § 103(c)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/103) requires VA to apply state law, including state law evidentiary burdens, in determining whether the criteria for a valid common law marriage has been satisfied.  The benefit-of-the-doubt standard under [38 U.S.C. § 5107(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5107) is inapplicable where a statute or regulation specifically dictates a different evidentiary standard, such as the state law in Alabama which includes a different evidentiary burden that VA must use  in determining the validity of a common law marriage.  Note: [38 U.S.C. § 5107(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5107) directs that the Secretary shall give the benefit of the doubt to a claimant “[w]hen there is an approximate balance of positive and negative evidence regarding any issue material to the determination of a matter.”

**Impact on VBA:**

Manual [M21-MR, III.iii.5.C](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000015795/), will be amended to clarify that state law evidentiary standards must also be applied in determining the validity of a common law marriage.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

In Burden, the veteran and appellant married in a ceremonial marriage in April 2004, two months prior to the veteran’s death.  The regional office (RO) denied the appellant’s claim for dependency and indemnity compensation (DIC) benefits because she had not been married to the veteran for at least one year prior to his death.  The appellant later provided a “Statement of Marital Relationship” and supporting lay statements to the effect that she and the veteran had a common law marriage for over five years prior to this death.  The RO continued the denial, finding that a common law marriage did not exist under Alabama law prior to the ceremonial marriage.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), having determined that the veteran and appellant were Alabama residents during the entire period that it was asserted that they had entered into a common law marriage, found that a common law marriage under Alabama law, which required “clear and convincing proof” of such marriage, had not existed.  Note: Under Alabama law, a party attempting to demonstrate a common law marriage has to establish four elements:  (1) At least 14 years old and mentally competent; (2) a present agreement or mutual consent to enter into the marriage relationship; (3) a public recognition of the existence of the marriage; and (4) cohabitation or mutual assumption openly or marital duties and obligations.

The Veterans Court affirmed the Board’s decision and held that the Board was required by [section 103(c)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/103) to apply the State of Alabama’s requirement of “clear and convincing proof” instead of applying the benefit of the doubt under [section 5107(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5107) in determining whether the appellant and veteran had entered into a common law marriage under Alabama law.  Note: [38 U.S.C. § 103(c)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/103) requires that the validity of a marriage must be determined under the law of the place in which the parties resided at the time of the marriage or the law of the place where the parties resided when the right to benefits accrued.

In Coleman, the veteran divorced his wife in 1982.  The veteran and his wife were also residents of Alabama.  However, the surviving spouse alleged that she reconciled with the veteran after their divorce and lived together in a common law marriage until the veteran’s death in June 2001.  After his death, the RO denied the appellant’s claim for death pension and accrued benefits, concluding that she was not married to the veteran at the time of his death.

The Board, in affirming the RO decision, acknowledged that the couple had lived together for periods after the divorce and that the veteran’s death certificate indicated that he was married at the time of his death, but found that a common law marriage had not been established under the clear and convincing proof evidentiary standard required by Alabama law.  The Board referred to evidence of record in which the veteran, after the 1982 divorce, had stated that he was divorced and lived alone or with his grandmother, and the appellant’s statement in conjunction with a 1994 apportionment claim that she was the veteran’s ex-wife.  The Veterans Court, finding no error by the Board, affirmed the decision.

The appellants, in arguing before the Federal Circuit, acknowledged that VA must follow Alabama law in determining the validity of a common law marriage, but they asserted that VA should have applied the benefit of the doubt standard under [section 5107(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5107) rather than Alabama’s clear and convincing proof standard when determining whether they had met the prerequisites for establishing a valid common law marriage.  They further contended that Alabama’s clear and convincing proof standard has no place in the uniquely pro-claimant VA adjudication system.

The Federal Circuit rejected the appellant’s arguments, finding that the Veterans Court had correctly concluded that [section 103(c)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/103) requires VA to apply state law, to include state evidentiary burdens, in determining whether the criteria for a valid common law marriage has been satisfied.  The Federal Circuit, finding nothing in the text of the statute that would permit VA to disregard the state evidentiary burden of proof for establishing a valid common law marriage, stated that failure to apply the clear and convincing proof standard would eviscerate an essential element of state law.  The Federal Circuit observed that the plain language of section 103(c), which requires VA to look to state law to determine the validity of a marriage, reflects the fact that matters related to marriage and domestic relations have long been considered to be the domain of the states.

The Federal Circuit, in rejecting the contention that the “benefit of the doubt” rule contained under [section 5107(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5107) precludes VA from applying state law in determining the validity of a marriage, noted that it had previously held that the benefit of the doubt rule was inapplicable where a statute or regulation specifically dictates a different evidentiary standard.  Under section 103(c), Congress specifically directed that state law governs questions related to the validity of marriage; thus, Alabama’s clear and convincing standard of proof to establish a valid common law marriage must be applied.  However, section 5107(b) may be applied once the validity of a marriage under state law has been established to matters regarding entitlement determination.

The Federal Circuit also concluded that the interplay between [38 U.S.C. § 103(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/103) and 103(c) reinforces the conclusion that section 103(c) requires the application of state law evidentiary standards, as section 103(a) provides a limited exception to section 103(c)’s requirement that the validity of marriage must be established under state law, which can be invoked only in situations in which a claimant was unaware that there was a legal impediment to his or her own marriage. Note: [Section 103(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/103) provides that, under certain circumstances, a marriage can be deemed valid when a claimant was unaware that there was a legal impediment to an otherwise valid marriage.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court decision.
