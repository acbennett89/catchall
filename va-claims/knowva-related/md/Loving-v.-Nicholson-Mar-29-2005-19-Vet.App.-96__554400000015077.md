Decision Assessment Document
 Loving v. Nicholson, Mar 29, 2005, 19 Vet.App. 96
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

In this case, the CAVC found that the Board of Veterans’ Appeals did not err in concluding that the veteran’s additional disability, which was found not to have been incident to examination or treatment, was not “caused” by the VA examination.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court's reasons**:

In this case a “metal ceiling grate or panel” fell on the veteran’s leg while he was being examined by a VA physician.  The veteran explained that, while he was being examined, he and the doctor heard “three loud booms” and that the “third boom shook the building” causing the ceiling grate to fall on his knee.  The Court found that the Board did not err in concluding that the veteran’s additional disability was not “caused” by the VA examination.  The Court referenced *Sweitzer v. Brown*, 5 Vet App. 503 (1993) in which the Court denied benefits under section 1151 to an appellant who, while waiting to undergo a VA examination in a VA hospital, was injured when he was struck by a patient in a motorized wheelchair.  The Court in *Sweitzer* concluded that section 1151 does not address disabilities that are merely coincidental with the receipt of VA treatment.

The facts in this case differ from those in *Sweitzer.* In this case, the veteran’s injury was sustained during the course of a VA examination.  In the Court’s view, however, that amounted to a distinction without a difference because, although the veteran sustained an injury during the course of a VA examination, that injury was coincidental to the examination and was not, based upon the evidence of record, caused by it.

Allegations regarding VA’s failure of its duty to assist: The Court found “both of Mr. Loving’s contentions of error with respect to the duty to assist to be unavailing”.  Regarding the veteran’s first contention – that VA failed to comply with the duty to assist because the BVA made a statement regarding the possible existence of VA medical records that were not before the Board – the Court observed that the BVA did not state that such records actually existed and that at no time before VA or the Court did Mr. Loving even contend that such records might exist.

The Court construed the veteran’s second contention – that an M21-1 “instruction that adjudicators not request quality assurance records” should be invalidated because it contravenes [38 USC 5103A](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) – as an argument that VA, in deciding the veteran’s claim, should have obtained and considered any existing quality assurance records.  The Court pointed out that, in addition to the fact that the veteran raised the issue of quality assurance reports for the first time on appeal to the Court, the veteran did not explain how such reports, assuming that they exist and in light of his testimony, could possibly be relevant to aiding him in proving that the examination caused the ceiling grate to fall on him.  The Court explained that because, at no time during the pendency of his claim before VA, did Mr. Loving ever identify any additional medical records or quality-assurance reports or request VA to provide them or explain how they might be relevant to his claim under section 1151, the court could not conclude that the Board erred in connection with VA’s duty to assist.

Note:  The Court did not reference [38 USC 5705](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5705), the provisions of which protect the confidentiality of medical quality assurance records.
