Decision Assessment Document
 Delisio v. Shinseki, Aug 24, 2011, 25 Vet.App. 45
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that a disability that is secondarily service-connected could have an effective date earlier than the date a claim was filed explicitly for the primary service-connected disability, but only under certain, limited circumstances.

The Court held that when a claim is pending and information reasonably indicates that the claimed condition is caused by a disability that may be associated with service, VA generally must investigate the possibility of secondary service connection; and, if that causal disability is, in fact, related to service, the pending claim reasonably encompasses a claim for benefits for the causal disability, such that no separate filing is necessary to initiate a claim for benefits for the causal disability, and the effective date of benefits for the causal disability can be as early as the date of the pending claim.

The Court clarified that it did not hold that a claim for benefits reasonably encompasses a claim for unclaimed disabilities that are not a cause of the condition for which benefits are sought, or for unclaimed disabilities that arise as a result of the condition for which benefits are sought.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.  The Court did not hold that an effective date ultimately assigned for a secondary service-connected disability could be earlier than the causal service-connected disability.

However, the Court’s decision does expand VA’s duty-to-assist obligations, which stems from language in the decision requiring VA to investigate whether a causal disease or condition (to include a causal disease or condition not expressly raised by the veteran) is related to service in order to determine whether the claimed condition is secondarily related to service.

The investigatory obligation may require VA to obtain an examination to determine whether the causal disease is in fact related to service so that VA can determine whether the claimed condition is secondary to service.  Pursuant to [*McLendon v.* *Nicholson*, 20 Vet.App. 79 (2006)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014871/), the first three elements of [38 U.S.C. § 5103A(d)(2)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) must be met before VA is required to schedule an examination.  Thus, the evidence still must indicate that the causal disease or condition, whether expressly claimed by the veteran or reasonably raised by the record, may be associated with service before VA must comply with the duty to assist and obtain an examination.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served from 1966 to 1969, to include Vietnam service.  In October 1980, he filed two claims for “Agent Orange” related conditions and reported multiple symptoms, including skin problems.  In a supplemental claim filed October 31, 1980, he listed symptoms including among other things, left-leg numbness.  The regional office (RO) informed the veteran that his claims would be processed upon return of his file from the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) for another matter; however, the claims remained open until June 1992, when he wrote to the RO, requesting adjudication for his claim for Agent Orange exposure.

During a September 1992 hearing before the Board, the Board member identified 15 issues – not including the 1980 claim for left-leg numbness – that required adjudication and asked the veteran if they were correct.  The veteran responded, “I think so[,] sir.”  The Board, in March 1995, adjudicated the issues identified at the hearing and denied service connection for a skin disorder claimed as due to Agent Orange exposure.  The Court, in April 1996, approved a joint motion for remand for posttraumatic stress disorder and a low back disability and dismissed the other issues.

Independent of the appellate matters, the veteran had been diagnosed with bilateral lower extremity peripheral neuropathy secondary to a low back disorder in September 1993.  He filed a claim for service connection for peripheral neuropathy in January 1994.  This claim remained unadjudicated until June 5, 2006, when he filed a claim for service connection for diabetes mellitus and peripheral neuropathy.  The RO, in May 2007, granted service connection for diabetes mellitus and peripheral neuropathy as secondary to diabetes mellitus, effective June 5, 2006.

The Board, in December 2008, finding that the veteran’s diabetes was first diagnosed in 2000 and peripheral neuropathy in 2001, granted an earlier effective date of June 5, 2005, pursuant to [38 U.S.C. § 5110(g)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5110), for both disabilities.  The Board found that the October 1980 claim did not include a claim for diabetes because the veteran is precluded from claiming a disorder that was not diagnosed.  Although the January 1994 claim for peripheral neuropathy remained open until 2007, the Board concluded that evidence showed that the veteran was not entitled to service connection for peripheral neuropathy on any basis other than secondary to diabetes.  Thus, the Board determined that the veteran’s entitlement to benefits for peripheral neuropathy was tied to his claim for diabetes.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred by limiting the effective date for his peripheral neuropathy to the date of his claim for benefits explicitly for diabetes.  He contended that the October 31, 1980, claim for left-leg numbness related to Agent Orange exposure reasonably encompassed a claim for benefits for both peripheral neuropathy (as the cause of his left-leg numbness) and diabetes (as the cause of his peripheral neuropathy and left-leg numbness); thus, the claim remained open until the May 2007 RO decision, such that an October 31, 1980, effective date is warranted.  Alternatively, the veteran also argued that a January 1994 effective date is warranted because his January 1994 claim for benefits for peripheral neuropathy reasonably encompassed a claim for benefits for diabetes (as the cause of his peripheral neuropathy).

The Secretary argued that the effective date for diabetes can be no earlier than one year prior to June 5, 2006, the date the veteran filed a claim explicitly for diabetes; the effective date for peripheral neuropathy cannot be earlier than the effective date for diabetes because the veteran could not have been entitled to benefits for a disability secondary to diabetes until he was entitled to benefits for diabetes; and the October 31, 1980, claim for left-leg numbness did not encompass either diabetes or peripheral neuropathy, was adjudicated in the March 1995 Board decision and became final with the April 1996 court order dismissing all matters on appeal except PTSD and a low back disorder, or was withdrawn at the 1992 hearing.

The Court rejected the Secretary’s argument that a claimant must explicitly file a claim for the causal disease or disability to establish entitlement to secondary service connection for the claimed condition as overly technical and incompatible with the veteran-friendly, nonadversarial, claims system.  The Court stated that the Secretary’s position contravened applicable case law that (1) a claimant need not file a claim for benefits for the precise medical causes of his condition; (2) the Secretary must sympathetically read claimant’s filing and investigate potentially applicable theories of service connection; and (3) a claim for benefits for one disability reasonably may encompass other disabilities.

The Court held that the law and evidence support that the veteran’s January 1994 claim for benefits for peripheral neuropathy reasonably encompassed a claim for benefits for diabetes.  The Court explained that because the January 1994 claim for peripheral neuropathy remained open when the medical evidence determined that the veteran’s peripheral neuropathy was caused by diabetes and the Secretary service connected diabetes, the January 1994 claim reasonably encompassed a claim for benefits for diabetes.  Thus, the Board’s finding that the effective date of claim for diabetes could be no earlier than June 5, 2006, is clearly erroneous.

In reaching its conclusion, the Court summarized its interpretation of the law governing the scope of claims and inferred claims in the context of a claim for secondary service connection.  The Court held that if (1) a claimant files a claim for benefits for a condition, (2) the claim remains open and not finally adjudicated, and (3) the cause of that condition ultimately is determined to be a disease or disability incurred in or aggravated by service, then the effective date for both the secondarily service-connected condition and the service-connected causal disease or disability can be as early as the date of the open claim, depending on when each disability manifested and when law or regulation otherwise authorized benefits.

Regarding the scope of the October 31, 1980, claim for benefits, the Court observed that entitlement to benefits for a disability or disease does not arise with a medical diagnosis of the condition, but with the manifestation of the condition and the filing of a claim for benefits for the condition.  Thus, if the veteran’s October 31, 1980, request for benefits remained pending and it was determined that the left-leg numbness was caused by his service-connected diabetes, then his October 31, 1980, claim for left-leg numbness reasonably would encompass a claim for diabetes.

In addressing the Secretary’s argument that the appellant’s October 31, 1980, claim was previously adjudicated by the Board in a 1995 decision and disposed of in a 1996 joint remand motion, the Court noted that the March 1995 Board decision – which included adjudication of a claim for benefits for a skin disorder due to Agent Orange – did not mention left-leg numbness or otherwise inform the veteran that all of his claims for benefits for Agent Orange-related disabilities had been adjudicated.  Thus, the matter was not encompassed in the April 1996 Court order and remained unadjudicated.

The Court also found that the veteran had not withdrawn his October 1980 claim for left-leg numbness during the 1992 Board hearing because the transcript reflected neither an explicit discussion of withdrawal nor any indication that the veteran understood that he might be withdrawing claims for any disabilities not discussed.  The Court concluded that because the claim remains open, the full scope of the claim had not yet been determined, as the cause of the veteran’s left-leg numbness had not yet been established.

The Court held that because the veteran is a *Nehmer* class member and his 1994 claim (and possibly his October 1980 claim) encompassed a claim for diabetes, the effective date is not limited to the date diabetes was added to the list of diseases presumptively associated with Agent Orange exposure.  Although the veteran was not diagnosed with diabetes until 2000, an effective date should not be mechanically assigned based on the date of diagnosis.  The facts should be examined to determine the date that diabetes first manifested, to include any necessary development and assessment of the symptoms the veteran suffered prior to the 2000 diabetes diagnosis and whether such symptoms were attributable to diabetes.  The Court directed that the Board, on remand, conduct this assessment in this first instance.

The Court also held that because the October 31, 1980, claim remains open and since the veteran is a *Nehmer* class member, on remand, the Board must ensure that the claim is developed properly and adjudicated with regard to whether peripheral neuropathy, diabetes, or both are the cause of his left leg numbness.  If the evidence supports a causal connection, the veteran would be entitled to an effective date of either October 31, 1980, or the date the left-leg numbness first manifested in accordance with the facts found.

The Court observed that although the Board correctly notes that the effective date for benefits for peripheral neuropathy can be no earlier than the effective date for his diabetes, the Board clearly erred in its determination that the earliest date for benefits for diabetes was June 5, 2005.  The Court stated that the proper effective date for benefits for peripheral neuropathy can be as early as January 1994 (the date of his claim for benefits explicitly for peripheral neuropathy) or October 1980 (the date of his claim for benefits for left-leg numbness), should it be determined that his left-leg numbness is caused by peripheral neuropathy or diabetes or both.

The Court affirmed the Board’s decision involving issues pertaining to a low back disability, but set aside the remainder of the decision and remanded for readjudication consistent with its decision.
