Decision Assessment Document
 Wood v. Derwinski, Mar 28, 1991, 1 Vet.App. 190 (1991)
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that those who adjudicate claims of incarcerated veterans to be certain that they tailor their assistance to the peculiar circumstances of confinement.

The Court also held that here the opportunity for face-to-face assistance is greatly reduced, if not eliminated (such as when the claimant is incarcerated) VA has an obligation to ensure that all written communication is helpful and clear in explaining to a veteran what evidence is needed to support the claim together with advice and help in obtaining it.

The duty to assist is not a one-way street.  If a veteran wishes help, he cannot passively wait for it in those circumstances where he may or should have information that is essential in obtaining the putative evidence.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court's holding has been incorporated into guidance contained in Manual M21-1, III.iv.3.F.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served in the Republic of Vietnam as a cannoneer from December 20, 1970, to June 17, 1971.  He was honorably discharged on June 30, 1971, with no indication in his records, or, indeed, from any source, of any disability, mental or physical. The veteran alleged that, during his tour of duty, he witnessed some emotionally traumatic incidents which eventually resulted in PTSD.  Although the traumatic incidents were never verified in any manner by separate objective evidence, he argues that the nature of his service, the fact that he served in Vietnam, and the clinical diagnoses of PTSD by a civilian doctor and social worker should be dispositive of his contention that his condition was service connected.

Three years after his service in Vietnam, the veteran initiated counseling sessions with a clinical social worker.  He initiated counseling because of marital difficulties, including violent episodes, which the veteran felt were related to his experiences in Vietnam.  The veteran was seen by the social worker from May 1975 to January 1976 in regular counseling sessions. In 1979, appellant was convicted of second-degree murder.  He was  sentenced to fifteen years to life.
 In December 1985, the murder conviction was reversed and remanded for a new trial. However, the veteran was apparently convicted again after a re-trial, and remained incarcerated. A psychiatrist involved in the murder case wrote to the veteran's public defender in May 1986 with a final diagnostic summary of the veteran's psychological condition at the time of the homicide.  In that letter, the psychiatrist stated, "[Wood] definitely showed Post Traumatic Stress Disorder of the Chronic Type complicated by substance abuse and/or chemical dependency initiated in Vietnam."   It is not clear whether this statement means that it was the PTSD that began in Vietnam or whether the psychiatrist was referring only to the substance abuse and chemical dependency.

From February through June of 1986, the veteran saw the same social worker weekly. On June 18, 1989, the social worker provided a statement to the BVA giving his professional opinion that Wood suffered from borderline personality and chronic-type PTSD and that the social worker believed that these conditions coexisted.
 In February 1988, the veteran filed his claim for service connection for PTSD.  In June 1988, the veteran filed a statement in support of the claim that outlined the traumatic events he contends caused his PTSD.  The VA sent this information to the U.S. Army and Joint Services Environmental Support Group (Environmental Support Group, which responded that its research was unsuccessful because the lack of specific combat dates, places and types of incidents made the research impossible.
 The VA made a second request for information from the Environmental Support Group in November 1988.  This time, at the Environmental Support Group's suggestion, veteran's DA Form 20 and his service record were forwarded.  These documents showed the units to which appellant was assigned, his duty specialty, and the dates he was in Vietnam.  The Environmental Support Group's response was again negative, citing the vagueness of the veteran's description of events, the lack of specific locations and dates and, for some of the events, the type of some of the incidents involved (civilian deaths).  A VA rating decision was issued on January 4, 1989, denying service connection for PTSD.

In June 1989, the veteran appealed to the Board of Veterans Appeals, which also denied service connection for PTSD.

The issue was raised as to whether the VA adequately assisted the veteran in developing his claim?  Complicating this issue is the fact of the veteran's incarceration. Obviously, in such situations the opportunity for face-to-face assistance is greatly reduced, if not eliminated; the corollary of this is the necessity of ensuring that all VA written communication is helpful and clear in explaining to a veteran what evidence he needs together with advice and help in obtaining it.  In the context of this case, as noted above, the sine qua non of establishing a claim is some corroboration of the alleged "stressors" that were service connected.  The veteran obviously needed help in obtaining this evidence.  The Department did, in a reasonably clear way, advise the veteran that independent evidence was needed and that it was obtainable only if the veteran could furnish some concrete data as to time, place, and witnesses.  Two attempts were made to get such information despite the sparsity of the data furnished by appellant.  His service record was produced in an attempt to obtain corroboration.  The veteran failed twice to be sufficiently specific about the stressful events he had alleged.

While the VA's help and advice in this case was not a model to be followed, an examination of the record satisfies us that the veteran was adequately on notice that more was required of him if there was to be a successful search for the necessary evidence.  The factual data required, i.e., names, dates and places, are straightforward facts and do not place an impossible or onerous task on the veteran.  The duty to assist is not always a one-way street.  If a veteran wishes help, he cannot passively wait for it in those circumstances where he may or should have information that is essential in obtaining the putative evidence.
 The Court cautioned that those who adjudicate claims of incarcerated veterans to be certain that they tailor their assistance to the peculiar circumstances of confinement.  Such individuals are entitled to the same care and consideration given to their fellow veterans.
