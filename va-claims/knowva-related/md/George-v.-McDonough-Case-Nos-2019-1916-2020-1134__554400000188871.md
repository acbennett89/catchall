**Decision Assessment Document**

**George v. McDonough, Case Nos: 2019-1916, 2020-1134**

**United States Court of Appeals, Federal Circuit**

**991 F.3d 1227 (March 16, 2021)**

**What the case is about:**

In this consolidated appeal the Federal Circuit Court of Appeals (Federal Circuit) held that revision of final VA decisions based on clear and unmistakable error (CUE) cannot be based on new interpretations of law, including new judicial interpretations. The Court clarified that the opposite result reached in one line of judicial decisions was non-precedential and incorrect as a matter of law.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s decision will be referenced in guidance contained in the Manual M21-1 under III.iv.2.B.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

This case consolidated the appeals of two Veterans whose respective claims for disability benefits were denied several decades ago in final decisions of VA. Each filed a motion for revision of those denials alleging that VA had committed clear and unmistakable error (CUE). VA's denials had been based in part on a straightforward application of a then-existing regulation, 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(b) (“Presumption of soundness”), that years later was overturned. The Veterans argued VA's reliance on a subsequently invalidated regulation in its denials of their original claims established CUE.

The United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (CAVC) affirmed the Board of Veterans’ Appeals’ (Board) denials of the Veterans’ CUE motions. The Board reasoned that VA did not commit a clear and unmistakable legal error when it faithfully applied the version of the presumption of soundness regulation that existed at the time of the denials. (CAVC’s decision in *George v. Wilkie*, 30 Vet.App. 364 (2019) is discussed in a prior Decision Assessment Document). The Federal Circuit affirmed the CAVC decision because controlling case law establishes that a legal-based CUE requires a misapplication of the law as it was understood at that time, and cannot arise from a subsequent change in interpretation of law by the agency or judiciary.

The Federal Circuit began by discussing the statutory presumption of soundness as it was codified in statute and specified in regulation in 1970, since those provisions governed when the alleged CUEs occurred. The Federal Circuit noted that in 1970 the statute concerning the presumption of soundness (38 U.S.C. § 311, now codified as 38 U.S.C. § 1111) provided that a Veteran is presumed to have been in sound condition at entry to service as to disorders that are not identified on the veteran's entrance medical examination. The presumption, however, could be rebutted by “clear and unmistakable evidence” that the disorder “existed before acceptance and enrollment and was not aggravated by service.” VA's old implementing regulation for 38 U.S.C. § 1111 did not require clear and unmistakable evidence of lack of aggravation by service for rebuttal. In other words, for the VA to rebut the presumption of soundness, the old version of § 3.304(b) required only clear and unmistakable evidence that the disorder “existed prior [to service].” This version of the regulation prevailed until 2003, when the VA invalidated the regulation for conflicting with the language of 38 U.S.C. § 1111 (see VA Gen. Counsel Prec. 3–2003, July 16, 2003), and subsequently amended the regulation to require evidence of both preexisting condition and no aggravation.

The Federal Circuit confirmed VA's changed understanding of the statute in *Wagner v. Principi*, 370 F.3d 1089 (Fed. Cir. 2004). In that case the Federal Circuit determined that Congress intended for the presumption of soundness to apply “even when there was evidence of a preexisting condition, [so long as] the government failed to show clear and unmistakable evidence that the preexisting condition was not aggravated” by service. Wagner thus held that the VA must show “clear and unmistakable evidence of both a preexisting condition and a lack of in-service aggravation to overcome the presumption of soundness.”

In this case, the Veterans argued that VA’s application of 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(b) as it existed when their claims were originally decided constituted CUE, since that regulation was later determined to be contrary to the controlling statute and congressional intent. They essentially contended that while VA may have correctly applied the regulation as it was written when it originally decided their claims, that regulation itself was erroneous and therefore the original decisions constituted CUE. They argued that *Wagner* merely provided an authoritative statement of what 38 U.S.C § 1111 had always meant, and contended their reasoning comported with the Federal Circuit  nonprecedential decision in *Patrick II* (242 F. App'x at 698, 2007), where the Federal Circuit permitted a CUE claim to proceed based on the argument that the VA had misapplied 38 U.S.C. § 1111.

The Federal Circuit’s analysis cited controlling precedential case law and noted that CUE is a “very specific and rare type of error” which must be based on “the record and the law that existed at the time of the prior adjudication in question,” such that “[e]ither the correct facts, as they were known at the time, were not before the adjudicator or the statutory or regulatory provisions extant at the time were incorrectly applied.” CUE must also be an “undebatable” error that would have “manifestly changed the outcome at the time it was made.”

The Federal Circuit disagreed with the Veterans because their argument overlooked the significance of the VA's regulation that existed at the time of the original decisions and failed to account for caselaw. The Federal Circuit reasoned that case law established that CUE allegations must be analyzed based on the law as it was understood at the time of the original decision and cannot arise from a subsequent change in the law or interpretation thereof to attack a final VA decision. The Federal Circuit found the arguments based in the *Patrick* line of cases unpersuasive because *Patrick II* is a nonprecedential decision, and although *Patrick III* (668 F.3d at 1334, 2011) was precedential it merely summarized *Patrick II’s* reasoning in dicta while resolving an issue other than whether the decision in *Wagner* can serve as a basis for CUE. The Federal Circuit also considered and rejected an argument that certain Supreme Court decisions support the position that retroactive application of judicial pronouncements is warranted in cases such as this.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the CAVC’s decisions.
