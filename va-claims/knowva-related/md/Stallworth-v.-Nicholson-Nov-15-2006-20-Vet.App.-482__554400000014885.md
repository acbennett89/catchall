Decision Assessment Document
 Stallworth v. Nicholson, Nov 15, 2006, 20 Vet.App. 482
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that severance of service connection based on any standard less than that established by [38 C.F.R. § 3.105(d)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=5095b4bdf79877164dad44dfc1739623&node=se38.1.3_1105&rgn=div8) is erroneous as a matter of law.

In determining whether a decision severing the prior grant of service connection was the product of clear and unmistakable error (CUE), an analysis is required of whether VA properly applied the stringent regulatory procedure for severance established by 38 C.F.R. § 3.105(d).

The Court determined that use of the “plausible basis” standard is irrelevant in determining whether a final decision was the product of CUE.  Although the Court has equated the standards for severance of service connection and for demonstrating CUE in a prior final VA decision, there are critical differences that render equating those standards an oversimplification.  A decision determining that severance of service connection is warranted normally focuses not on whether the original decision *was* clearly erroneous, but on whether *current* evidence establishes that the grant of service connection was clearly erroneous.

The requirement to find the diagnosis upon which the original award of service connection was based as clearly erroneous is not a requirement that CUE be shown in the original decision in the same manner as required under sections [38 U.S.C. §§ 5109A](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5109A) or [7111](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/7111).  Specifically, the VA is not limited to the law and the record that existed at the time of the original decision as 38 C.F.R. § 3.105(d) - through its provision that a change in diagnosis may be accepted as a basis for severance - necessarily contemplates that the VA is not limited in the same manner as a claimant attempting to demonstrate CUE in a prior final decision. See Daniels v. Gober, 10 Vet.App. 474 (1997). VA may consider medical evidence and diagnoses that postdate the original award of service connection to demonstrate that the diagnosis on which service connection was predicated was clearly erroneous.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting Manual change or regulatory revision.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served on active duty from May 1974 to July 1975.  He was diagnosed with schizophrenia in service.  A medical board found him unfit for service and directed transfer to a VA hospital.  A June 1975 record from the VA hospital indicated that it was initially believed that the veteran’s psychosis was due to drug use; however, the diagnosis was changed to schizophrenia after a relapse after a short period of hospitalization.  It was also noted that the veteran had been mentally ill and would require anti-psychotic drugs for an indefinite period.

In October 1975, the VA regional office (RO) awarded service connection for schizophrenia with a temporary total evaluation from July to August 1975 and subsequent 50 percent rating effective thereafter.

Several VA hospital reports from 1975-77 yielded diagnoses of “no mental disorder.”  The evaluation for schizophrenia was subsequently reduced to zero percent.

A March 1977 VA hospital summary concluded that there was no evidence of mental illness at that time and included an assessment that the diagnosis of schizophrenia, for which service connection was granted, was erroneous as the diagnosis should have been psychosis with drug or poison intoxication.  Based on that report, the following month the RO proposed to sever.  The veteran was subsequently hospitalized in June 1977 and diagnosed with chronic undifferentiated schizophrenia.

The RO, concluding that the assignment of service connection for an acute episode of schizophrenia was clearly and unmistakably erroneous, severed service connection in November 1977.

The veteran made a request for restoration of service connection in August 1978.  He was hospitalized from November 1978 through January 1979 with chronic undifferentiated schizophrenia.  A psychiatric examination conducted in August 1979 included a diagnosis of paranoid schizophrenic reaction.  The RO, in December 1979, considered this evidence and informed the veteran that the severance of service connection could not be changed.  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in 1981, upheld the RO severance on the grounds that the severance action was supportable.  The Board further found that the reports of hospitalization since 1977 did not definitely establish that the veteran had a chronic acquired psychosis in service or one that became manifest within the presumptive period.

In December 1990, the Board granted service connection for schizophrenia based on new and material evidence that established that a psychosis was incurred in service.  In October 2002, the veteran, through counsel, filed a Motion for Revision of the 1981 BVA decision on the grounds of CUE.  The Board, in January 2004, determined that the 1981 Board decision did not contain CUE.

The veteran brought several arguments before the Court to include contending that the 1981 Board decision failed to allege any facts not brought before the original adjudicator or any violation of law or regulations in the decision authorizing the award and that [38 C.F.R. § 3.105(d)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=5095b4bdf79877164dad44dfc1739623&node=se38.1.3_1105&rgn=div8) should be read to require that a change in diagnosis may be a basis for severance only when all other criteria for CUE have been met; thus, a physician’s opinion certifying that a prior diagnosis was clearly erroneous is merely another opinion added to the mix and consequently does not establish the degree of indisputability required to establish CUE.

The Court stated that § 3.105(d) does not focus on whether the original decision was clearly erroneous, but on whether the current evidence establishes that service connection is clearly erroneous.  The Secretary is not limited to the law and record that existed at the time of the original decision.

The Court, finding that the Board’s January 2004 decision was unclear as to whether the correct standard of review was applied, concluded that its ability to review the Board decision on appeal is impaired and directed a reasons-or-bases remand.

The Board, while determining that there was a “plausible” basis for the 1981 Board decision, also concluded that CUE was not demonstrated in the 1981 decision.  Also, the Board’s raising of the possibility that there may have been another view of the evidence is ambiguous language that requires that the Board, in conducting a CUE review, render a decision that reflects that it understands and applies the correct analysis in the first instance.

Finding that the “plausible standard” is irrelevant in determining whether a prior decision is the product of CUE, the Court directed that the Board apply a three-part test, which it created in previous precedent decisions, to determine whether the 1981 Board decision was the product of CUE. (Note: This test, originally crafted in Russell v. Principi, 3 Vet.App. 310 (1992), requires the following three elements for CUE:  (1) Whether the correct facts, as they were known at the time, were not before the adjudicator (i.e., more than a simple disagreement as to how the facts were weighed or evaluated) or the statutory or regulatory provisions extant at the time were incorrectly applied; (2) whether the existence of an error is undebatable; and (3) whether the error is of the sort which, had it not been made, would have manifestly changed the outcome at the time it was made. )

The Court also noted that this standard is equally applicable when the issue is severance of service connection based on CUE.

The Court vacated the January 2004 Board decision and remanded with instructions that the Board determine whether the 1981 Board decision severing the prior grant of service connection was the product of CUE, with such determination requiring an analysis of whether VA properly applied the stringent regulatory procedure for severance established by § 3.105(d).
