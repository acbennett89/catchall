Decision Assessment Document
 AZ and AY v. Shinseki, Sep 30, 2013, 731 F.3d 1303
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that VA may not treat the absence of a service record documenting an unreported sexual assault as pertinent evidence that the sexual assault did not occur.

The Federal Circuit also held that VA may not rely on a veteran’s failure to report an alleged in-service sexual assault to military authorities as pertinent evidence that the sexual assault did not occur. The Federal Circuit did indicate that where a veteran claims to have reported a sexual assault to military authorities, but no report is found, the absence of the report may be pertinent to the evaluation of his or her claim.

**Impact on VBA:**

We have revised the Manual M21-1 at IV.ii.1.D.5.c to incorporate the Court’s holding.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

AZ, who served from March 1973 to July 1974, filed a claim for service connection for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in 2004, claiming that her PTSD was the result of sexual and physical abuse by a non-commissioned officer (NCO) of superior rank in service.  She also stated that the assailant was the father of her child born in October 1974 after leaving service.  AZ did not report the alleged sexual assaults to military authorities, and her service records did not reflect any report of an assault.

The regional office (RO) denied the claim, noting that AZ’s service records did not document that a sexual assault had occurred.  She requested reconsideration, and submitted lay statements from her three siblings who stated that AZ told them during her pregnancy in 1974 that she had been sexually assaulted, verbally abused, and beaten by [an NCO] and did not report the assaults to military authorities because she was afraid and did not think that she would be believed.  AZ also indicated in a lay statement that she did not report the incidents because she “was a young girl, sexually assaulted, verbally abused and beaten by a superior [officer] and she was in fear of her life.”  The RO, although acknowledging that AZ’s siblings had reported that she told them about the assaults during her pregnancy, denied the claim because there was no evidence of verification of the assaults or any comments made by the veteran or physicians regarding episodes of beatings or sexual trauma in the service records.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) found that the service records showed no evidence of any psychiatric disorder, reports of injuries from a personal assault during service, or that the veteran reported having been personally assaulted, or instigated proceedings against her alleged attacker.  The Board acknowledged the lay statements; however, it concluded that they were insufficient to overcome the absence of documentation of an in-service assault, as the contemporaneous service records, which showed no in-service assault, were more probative.

The Board, in denying the appeal, relied upon the absence of service records of the assault, records of a disciplinary problem predating the alleged assault, a service medical record indicating that the veteran planned on getting married, post-service stressors such as unemployment, and other evidence, and concluded that the evidence of record was insufficient to confirm that the alleged assault occurred.

The Veterans Court affirmed the Board decision, finding that the Board was permitted to weigh the absence of corroborating records and documents against the lay evidence of record and did not err by weighing the lay statements against the other evidence of record and finding them less probative.

AY served from July 1980 to July 1983.  She filed a claim for service connection for PTSD in 2004, claiming that the PTSD resulted from a sexual assault committed by another solider during military training.  She submitted a lay statement from her ex-husband in support of her claim.  AY’s service records contain no record of a report of a sexual assault or treatment for a sexual assault or psychiatric problem.

The RO denied the claim.  The veteran filed a claim to reopen and she submitted three additional lay statements from individuals who knew her during service, attesting that she told them about the assault, became despondent and either discussed or attempted suicide.  The RO also considered the veteran’s ex-husband statement that she told him about the alleged assault while they were in service together.  The RO, in again denying the claim, acknowledged the lay statements, but concluded that there was no evidence to corroborate the alleged assault because the service medical and personnel records had no evidence to support an in-service rape or suicide attempt.

The Board, in October 2009, also acknowledged the lay statements but found that they were directly contradicted by other evidence of record, which strongly diminished their probative value.  The Board observed that the veteran did not report the alleged assault to police at the time that it occurred or to anyone immediately, the service records showed no treatment for a psychiatric disorder, there was no record that the veteran had reported that she was sexually assaulted to medical personnel, and her service records were inconsistent with lay statements that she experienced severe depression.  Thus, the Board, in denying the appeal, found that the lay statements were insufficient to corroborate her claimed stressor.

The Veterans Court disagreed with the veteran’s contention that the Board had improperly found the lay evidence not credible merely because it was unaccompanied by contemporaneous medical evidence.  The Veterans Court found that the lay statements were not merely uncorroborated, but contradicted by the absence of records documenting treatment for the suicide attempt.  The Veterans Court also observed that service records commending AY’s duty performance and demeanor contradicted the lay statements reporting that AY suffered from depression and other psychiatric problems after the sexual assault.  The Veterans Court affirmed the Board’s finding that there was no credible supporting evidence corroborating the in-service assault.

AZ and AY argued before the Federal Circuit that the absence of contemporaneous service records of unreported in-service sexual assaults is not pertinent, because it is not reasonable to expect that such assaults would have been reported to superior officers, or that records of unreported assaults would exist.

The Federal Circuit noted that VA did not dispute that in the great majority of sexual assault cases, records do not exist, as incidents are not reported to military authorities, and VA had, in part, enacted 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(5) to address the fact that many incidents of in-service personal assault are not officially reported.  The Federal Circuit cited studies and reports documenting non-reporting of in-service sexual assaults and also referred to various courts and the Federal Rules of Evidence in applying a common-law rule of refusing to admit evidence of the absence of a record to show that an event did not occur, where it was not reasonable to expect the event to have been recorded.

The Federal Circuit determined that the well-established common-law principle concerning the inadmissibility of unreliable record evidence should be followed in these cases, as the absence of a report of an unreported sexual assault is too ambiguous to have probative value.  Because the alleged assaults were not reported to military authorities, no reasonable person could expect records documenting the assaults to exist, or infer that the absence of such records tends to prove the assaults did not occur.  Accordingly, the Federal Circuit held that the absence of records was not pertinent evidence, one way or the other, to whether an in-service assault occurred.

The Federal Circuit summarized that basic evidentiary principles preclude treating the absence of a record of an unreported sexual assault as evidence of the nonoccurrence of the assault.  The Federal Circuit stated that it agreed with the veterans that where an alleged sexual assault, like most sexual assaults, is not reported, the absence of service records documenting the alleged assault is not pertinent evidence that the assault did not occur. The Federal Circuit indicated that it did not suggest that service records cannot otherwise be relied on by the VA, for example, to show AZ had a disciplinary problem prior to the alleged assault, or to establish that AY was never treated for attempted suicide at a military hospital.

In addressing whether the veteran’s failure to report the sexual assaults to superiors in the chain of command is itself pertinent evidence that the assault did not occur, the Federal Circuit observed that courts recognize that many victims are afraid to report the crime because they fear stigma, humiliation, or the ordeal of testifying about the attack.  The Federal Circuit held that the common law theory that it was inevitable that a rape victim would make an immediate complaint was inapplicable in this case, because the theory was considered relevant only where the victim failed “to make any complaint” to anyone, not where the victim failed to make an official report to a supervisor, an employer, or the police.

The Federal Circuit noted that courts have generally held in both criminal and civil contexts, that where there is no reason to suspect that no report or other statement would have been made, testimony about the failure to make a report or statement is inadmissible.  The Federal Circuit also noted that the veteran’s benefits system is based on solicitude for the claimant, which argues against the use of evidence against a clamant when it has doubtful pertinence.

Accordingly, the Federal Circuit held that, for the aforementioned reasons, VA may not treat a claimant’s failure to report an alleged sexual assault to military authorities as pertinent evidence that the sexual assault did not occur.

The Federal Circuit remanded the cases for the Veterans Court to consider to what extent the Board improperly relied on the absence of service records documenting the alleged sexual assaults, or on the veterans’ failure to contemporaneously report the alleged sexual assaults to military authorities, as pertinent evidence that the alleged assaults did not occur.  The Federal Circuit further stated that the Veterans Court should consider whether remand to the Board is required so that the cases can be readjudicated in the light of the correct standard.
