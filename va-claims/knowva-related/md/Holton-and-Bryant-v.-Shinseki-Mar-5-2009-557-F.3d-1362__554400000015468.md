Decision Assessment Document
 Holton and Bryant v. Shinseki, Mar 5, 2009, 557 F.3d 1362
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that [38 U.S.C. § 105(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/105) and [1111](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1111) cannot substitute as a medical nexus and section 105(a) does not create a presumption of injury in service connection claims.  Section 105(a) only creates a presumption that an injury or disease incurred during service was incurred in the line of duty and did not result from the veteran’s own misconduct.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The Federal Circuit consolidated the appeals because both veterans put forth similar arguments that required the Federal Circuit to clarify the elements that a veteran must satisfy to receive VA disability compensation.

 In Holton, the veteran, who served in the Coast Guard from 1968 to 1972, claimed that his pelvic fracture was the result of slipping and falling on slick metal stairs while working in ships’ engine rooms in service.  The service medical records (SMRs) contained no evidence of a pelvis injury.  He began to experience pain in the right hip and buttocks many years after service and a 2002 X-ray showed an avulsion fracture of the right inferior pubic ramus.  In support of his claim he provided a statement from another veteran who served with him aboard a Coast Guard icebreaker, stating that the ship was frequently in rough seas, its ladders were often slippery, and that it was highly likely that the veteran sustained injuries on the ship.

Following denials by the regional office (RO) and Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), the Veterans Court remanded for a medical nexus opinion.  The veteran was examined in 2005 and the VA examiner concluded that it was “speculative at best” that the pubic fracture was caused by service activity or fracture in view of the time frame of 25 to 30 years.  The RO and Board again denied the claim, with the Board stating that the record did not even include a “scintilla” of competent medical evidence showing a nexus between any current disability of the pelvis and the falls the veteran sustained in service.

In Bryant, the veteran filed a claim for service connection for glaucoma.  He served honorably in the Army from 1943 to 1947 and from 1948 to 1963.  In September 1962 he was examined after complaining of eye irritation and seeing halos around light.  The examination was negative, except for a tonometer measurement that showed increased intra-ocular pressure in both eyes.  It was indicated that the tonometer may be defective.  A diagnosis of acute glaucoma was given; however, in a follow-up examination the next week, the examining physician concluded that the first tonometer reading was erroneous and that the veteran did not have glaucoma.  There was no evidence of glaucoma throughout the remainder of service or in any post-service examinations over the next two decades.  A VA ophthalmologist diagnosed the veteran with uncontrolled open-angle glaucoma in 1990.

The RO and Board denied the veteran’s claim; however, the Veterans Court remanded for a medical nexus opinion.  The veteran was examined in 2003 and the VA examiner concluded that the veteran’s glaucoma did not result from any disease incurred during service because post-service medical examinations showed normal intra-ocular pressures and no indication of any abnormalities of the optic nerves.  The Board again denied the claim, finding that the 1962 glaucoma diagnosis resulted from a faulty tonomoter reading because the contemporaneous follow-up examination and the 2003 medical opinion ruled out any in-service glaucoma.

Both veterans argued before the Veterans Court that the Board either misinterpreted or failed to apply sections 105(a) and 1111.  The Veterans Court affirmed the Board’s decisions in both cases.

The Federal Circuit indicated that section 105(a) contains two important components to the line-of-duty presumption.  First, any injury or disease will be deemed to have been incurred in the line of duty if it occurred at almost any time during a veteran’s active service, to include authorized leave.  A veteran is not required to show that an injury occurred while performing service-related duties, because for purposes of VA disability compensation, a service member’s workday never ends.  Second, section 105(a) creates a presumption that a veteran’s in-service injury or disease did not result from his own misconduct.  The Federal Circuit concluded that while section 105(a) presumption establishes that an injury or disease that was incurred during service was incurred in the line of duty, it is irrelevant to the question of whether that in-service injury or disease is causally related to the veteran’s current disability.

Regarding the application of section 1111, the Federal Circuit stated that the statute is irrelevant if there is not a question as to whether a disease or injury existed before a veteran’s entry into service or a preexisting condition was aggravated in service.  Section 1111 presumption of soundness does not relieve a veteran of the obligation to show a current disability, a nexus between that disability and the in-service incurrence or aggravation of the disease or injury, or that the veteran suffered from a disease or injury in service.

The Federal Circuit, in affirming the Veterans Court decisions, found that both veterans misinterpreted the statutory presumptions of sections [105(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/105) and [1111](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1111) and how they operate in connection with the three elements that a veteran must prove to receive compensation.

The Federal Circuit rejected Mr. Holton’s claim that the VA examiner in 2005 should have been instructed to presume that he suffered an in-service pelvis injury in rendering his opinion.  The Federal Circuit found no authority to compel an examining physician to presume the existence of an in-service injury when providing a medical nexus opinion and that section 105(a) was irrelevant to the nexus inquiry.  The Federal Circuit also rejected Mr. Holton’s argument that section 1111 was relevant to his claim, as he had indisputably entered service in sound condition.  In this case, the Board instead denied the claim because there was no medical nexus relating the current disability to service, and therefore, the presumption of soundness was irrelevant to that inquiry.

Mr. Bryant argued that the 1962 glaucoma diagnosis constituted affirmative evidence of in-service disease, which triggered the presumption of in-service disease, and placed the burden on VA to rebut the evidence that he incurred the disease in the line of duty.  He also argued that it was enough for a veteran to put forth some evidence of an in-service injury or disease – no matter how little or how credible – to trigger section 105(a) and to satisfy the requirement that he incurred or aggravated a disease or injury in service.

The Federal Circuit rejected this interpretation, explaining that section 105(a) created a presumption that an injury or disease incurred during service was incurred in the line of duty and did not result from the veteran’s own misconduct, but it did not create a presumption of injury.  Section 105(a) does not relieve the veteran of the obligation to show incurrence of a disease or injury during active service in the *first place*.  The Federal Circuit stated that section 105(a) cannot serve as a substitute for affirmative evidence that a veteran incurred an injury or disease during service.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court decisions.
