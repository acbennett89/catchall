Decision Assessment Document
 Singleton v. Shinseki, Aug 8, 2011, 659 F.3d 1332
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that assignment of a retrospective staged rating, to include a period of total disability followed by a period of lower rating, does not deny a veteran’s constitutional property interest without due process.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in December 2007, assigned the following staged ratings for the veteran’s service-connected mental disorder:  50 percent from April 11, 1980, to December 9, 1980; 100 percent from December 10, 1980, to October 31, 1991; 70 percent from November 1, 1991, to December 28, 2000; and 100 percent from December 29, 2000.

The veteran argued before the Veterans Court that because he was assigned a 100 percent staged rating for more than five years (December 1980 to October 1991), the disability rating was stabilized under [38 C.F.R. § 3.344(c)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=8534e547f6bda2793816ea2a3f17956b&node=se38.1.3_1344&rgn=div8), and could not be reduced without VA first providing him a full and complete medical examination that supported such a reduction.

The Veterans Court, in rejecting the veteran’s arguments, deferred to VA’s position that § 3.344 was inapplicable to the staged disability-ratings context, and affirmed the Board’s decision.

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that failure to require application of § 3.344, when retrospectively assessing his disability, effectively denied him of “property” without constitutional due process.  Specifically, the veteran identified the subject “property” as the 100 percent disability rating beginning December 1980 and the denial of property occurred when the 100 percent evaluation was reduced to 70 percent in November 1991.  He maintained that the due process clause required a separate evidentiary proceeding before the Board could conclude that a period of 70 percent disability should follow one of total disability.

The Federal Circuit, applying Supreme Court guidance for determining the constitutional sufficiency of a process that results in deprivation of property, found that the process applied by the Board and Veterans Court was sufficient to meet constitutional muster.  Note: The Supreme Court, in [*Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319, 333 (1976)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/424/319) held that due process regarding deprivation of property requires the following factors to be considered:  (1) The private interest that will be affected by official action; (2) the risk of an erroneous deprivation of such interest through the procedures used, and the probable value, if any, of additional or substitute procedural safeguards; and (3) the Government’s interest, including the function involved and the fiscal and administrative burdens that the additional or substitute procedural requirement would entail.

The Federal Circuit previously recognized a constitutionally-protected property interest in a veteran’s entitlement to disability benefits.  See [*Cushman v. Shinseki*, 576 F.3d 1290, 1298 (Fed. Cir. 2009)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000015531/) .  However, the Federal Circuit stated that the interest in this case extends only so far as the law creates it.  Although [§ 3.344](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=8534e547f6bda2793816ea2a3f17956b&node=se38.1.3_1344&rgn=div8) confers a measure of additional security to veterans whose disability rating has stabilized, there is no regulation or caselaw suggesting that this extra security is available when establishing staged ratings retrospectively.

The Federal Circuit observed that the veteran participated in a 2004 Board hearing, specifically requested the Board to consider staged ratings, and appealed to the Veterans Court.  Thus, the Federal Circuit concluded that the process applied to the veteran’s case was sufficient to minimize the risk of being erroneously deprived of benefits.  The Federal Circuit also found that the Government had a straightforward interest in the speedy resolution of the veteran’s claim, as adding further rounds of review would require yet more hours of labor and additional adjudication costs.

The Federal Circuit, in affirming the Veterans Court decision, concluded that there was no constitutional deprivation in the veteran’s case, as the veteran had his day in court concerning staged ratings and the legal process was sufficient to satisfy his Fifth Amendments rights.
