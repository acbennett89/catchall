Decision Assessment Document
 McNair v. Shinseki, Nov 18, 2011, 25 Vet.App. 98
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the presumption of regularity does not apply to the scope of the information provided to a patient by a doctor with regard to the risks involved with any particular treatment.  However, a failure to provide information to a patient about a potential adverse effect does not defeat a finding of informed consent if a reasonable person faced with similar circumstances would have proceeded with the treatment.

The Court held that [38 C.F.R. § 3.361(d)(1)(ii)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=83b1780657a806973dd5ac0d417eb152&node=se38.1.3_1361&rgn=div8) and the general common law understanding of informed consent provides that failure to advise a patient of a foreseeable risk can be considered a minor, immaterial deviation under the regulation if a reasonable person in similar circumstances would have proceeded with the medical treatment even if informed of the foreseeable risk. Note: The regulation provides that minor deviations from the requirement of [38 C.F.R. § 17.32](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=e4660406d7e91bbad894c377491670bc&node=se38.1.17_132&rgn=div8) (regulation governing informed consent) that are immaterial under the circumstances of a case will not defeat a finding of informed consent.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who was discharged from service in May 1995, underwent bilateral reduction mammoplasty at a VA hospital in June 1998.  Note: Plastic reconstruction of the breast to reduce size.  Dorland's Illustrated Medical Dictionary 1116 (31st ed. 2007). The veteran and her physician signed an authorization for medical procedures form that reflects that she was advised as to the nature of the surgery, attendant risks involved, and expected results.  The form was general in nature and did not state the specific attendant risks that were discussed.  A contemporaneous treatment note described the veteran’s current symptoms to include neck, back, and shoulder pain, significant “bra strapping,” and decreased physical activity due to the size and position of the breasts.  Another treatment note detailed the surgeon’s conversation with the veteran regarding the risks and tradeoffs of the surgery to include scarring and excessive skin, adipose or glandular tissue, and potential complications of hematoma, partial or complete nipple-areolar complex, graft loss, and irregular pigmentation during healing.  The veteran acknowledged being informed of the complications and instructed the surgeon to proceed.

The veteran, in November 1998, filed a claim for compensation under [38 U.S.C. § 1151](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1151) for continual neuralgia (pain along the course of one or more nerves) from the breast reduction surgery.  The regional office denied the claim, finding that the evidence showed that pain from nerve regeneration after the elective surgery was an expected consequence of the surgery.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in denying the appeal in January 2009, found that the neuralgia was a foreseeable risk of surgery but that the veteran was not entitled to disability compensation because there was no evidence of negligence or similar instance of fault on the part of VA in furnishing surgical treatment, and the veteran provided informed consent for treatment.  Regarding the informed consent finding, the Board found that there was substantial compliance with VA’s informed consent regulation, [38 C.F.R. § 17.32](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=66563ac9c99b3d84dea01ae4b71cf275&node=se38.1.17_132&rgn=div8).  The Board stated that there was no regulatory requirement that every foreseeable risk be documented in the record.  To the extent that the record did not document that neuralgic scar pain was a possible risk, such omission was a minor deviation from the requirement of § 17.32 that was immaterial under the circumstances of the case because “in this case a reasonable person could assume that the surgeon’s detailed discussion addressing possible residual scarring included associated neuralgic pain.”

The veteran argued that that the specific risk of neuralgia was not disclosed to her and the only affirmative evidence of record on that point – her own lay statements asserting that she was not so informed – were not weighed by the Board.  She also argued that the [§ 3.361(d)(1)(ii)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=1cae6bff1d8efcba19a83298e7923069&node=se38.1.3_1361&rgn=div8) reference to “minor” and “immaterial” deviations includes only minor mistakes in documenting the consent, such as ascribing the wrong date to an otherwise properly executed consent form.

The Secretary argued that the Board’s findings were not clearly erroneous because the veteran signed a consent form in which she attested that she understood the nature of the proposed procedure, attendant risks involved, and the expected results.  The Secretary further argued that the minor and immaterial deviations under § 3.361(d)(1)(ii) include a failure to disclose a risk that, had it been known to a reasonable person in the veteran’s circumstances, would not have deterred a reasonable person from undergoing surgery.  The Secretary also averred that the presumption of regularity applied to the facts of this case and the veteran’s statements alone were insufficient to rebut it.

The Court held that the presumption of regularity does not apply to the scope of the advice and information given by a doctor to his patient in a signed, generic consent form if contested by the claimant because the diversity of patients, procedures, and circumstances counsels against recognizing a presumption that a doctor has fully informed a particular patient about a particular consequence of a particular medical procedure simply because a generic consent form has been filled out properly.  The Court indicated that if there is a dispute concerning what information a doctor provided to his patient, a factual issue is raised whether a generic consent form indicating the patient was advised of the risks of surgery is more probative than the claimant’s statements that a specific risk of surgery was not discussed.  The Board must weigh the veteran’s contentions, signed consent form, and all relevant evidence in the record in the first instance.

The Court found the Board’s conclusion that “a reasonable person could assume that the surgeon’s detailed discussion addressing possible residual scarring includes neuralgic pain” to be inadequate as no rationale was provided for concluding what a reasonable person could assume.

The Court further found that the Board failed to provide a rationale for its determination that any failure to document neuralgia was a minor deviation that was immaterial under the circumstances of the case.  However, the Court agreed with the Secretary’s interpretation of [§ 3.361(d)(1)(ii)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=1cae6bff1d8efcba19a83298e7923069&node=se38.1.3_1361&rgn=div8) that failure to inform a patient about a reasonably foreseeable consequence of treatment is a minor, nonmaterial deviation if a reasonable person under the circumstances would have consented to the treatment anyway.  The Court indicated that the text of § 3.361(d)(1)(ii) and its placement in the overall regulatory scheme demonstrate that the term “minor deviations” includes substantive as well as technical or procedural errors.

The Court, referencing common law that generally holds that the “test for determining whether a particular peril must be divulged is its materiality to the patient’s decision,” noted that the adjudicator not only look to the likelihood of an undisclosed risk materializing, but also recognize that some foreseeable risks may be minor when compared to the foreseeable consequences of continuing without undergoing the treatment.

The Court found that the Board provided no discussion as to whether a reasonable person in the veteran’s situation would have proceeded with the surgery even if advised of the risk that she could suffer from neuralgia as a result of that surgery.  The Board also did not discuss the consequences of proceeding with the surgery versus foregoing surgery, which are primary factors to evaluate whether a reasonable person would have proceeded with the surgery.

Accordingly, the Court set aside the Board’s decision and remanded the case for the Board to develop, as needed, facts related to the scope of information provided to the veteran regarding the foreseeable risks of her treatment, foreseeable consequences of failing to undergo the treatment, and whether a reasonable person would have proceeded with the surgery even if advised of the risk of neuralgia pursuant to [§ 3.361(d)(1)(ii)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=1cae6bff1d8efcba19a83298e7923069&node=se38.1.3_1361&rgn=div8).
