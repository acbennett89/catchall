Decision Assessment Document
 Cline v. Shinseki, Aug 16, 2012, 26 Vet.App. 18
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

VA amended [38 C.F.R. § 3.156(c)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=3a77cec38b5c011cb54976b1ff82c1c0&node=se38.1.3_1156&rgn=div8) on October 6, 2006, to add [38 C.F.R. § 3.156(c)(2)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=3a77cec38b5c011cb54976b1ff82c1c0&node=se38.1.3_1156&rgn=div8), as an exception to the general provisions of § 3.156(c).  Section 3.156(c)(2) created a bar to reconsideration of a final decision based on newly associated service department records if the claimant had not provided information that would have enabled VA or another federal agency to identify and search for relevant records at the time of the final decision.  The purpose of the amendment was to establish clearer rules regarding reconsideration of decisions on the basis of newly associated service records.

The Court held that the addition of § 3.156(c)(2) in October 2006 was a substantive change that had an impermissible retroactive effect; thus, it could not be retroactively applied to cases pending on the provision’s effective date of October 6, 2006.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.  In claims filed prior to or pending at the time of the October 2006 addition of § 3.156(c)(2), the version of § 3.156(c) in effect prior to the regulatory revision should be applied.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, whose July 1970 to March 1972 service included duty in Vietnam, filed a claim for service connection in January 1993 for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).  In a PTSD stressor questionnaire, he identified several stressors, to include an unnamed friend that drowned in Vietnam.  During a May 1993 VA examination, the veteran related stressors such as rocket/mortar attacks and casualties to Vietnam service.  The examiner concluded that the veteran may have had a mild case of PTSD, but no longer had sufficient symptoms to support the diagnosis.  The RO denied the claim the following month.  The veteran did not appeal the decision.

In May 1999, the veteran attempted to reopen the claim; however, the RO, not finding new and material evidence, denied reopening of the claim in September 1999.  A March 2000 VA progress note contained a diagnosis of PTSD and reported that the veteran recalled seeing the body of his friend, “Butch,” who had drowned while on a mission in Vietnam.  He stated that he could not forget his friend’s disfigurement.

In a July 2000 PTSD questionnaire, the veteran reported that his friend “Butch” was killed two weeks before his tour of duty ended.  He later failed to respond to the RO’s request to “better identify the soldier” and the RO denied the claim in January 2002.  The veteran appealed the decision.

In an August 2003 stressor statement, the veteran identified “Butch” as Robert Clifton.  The Center for Unit Records Research (CURR), in July 2005, confirmed that Specialist Robert Clifton drowned in October 1971 and was assigned to the same unit as the veteran during his tour in Vietnam.  A VA examiner, in September 2005, confirmed the veteran’s diagnosis of PTSD based on the verified stressor of a fellow soldier’s drowning.

The RO, in February 2006, granted service connection for PTSD with a 30 percent rating, effective April 2000.  The effective date was later revised to May 6, 1999, with a 50 percent evaluation from that date and a 70 percent evaluation from January 2007.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in denying the veteran’s claim in July 2010, found that the veteran failed to provide sufficient information in January 1993 for VA to attempt to corroborate his claimed stressor – seeing a friend or fellow soldier drown – with the CURR.  The Board concluded that while the CURR response regarding Specialist Clifton’s death was likely the type of information contemplated by § 3.156(c)(1), the veteran’s claim was more properly considered under § 3.156(c)(2), which precludes an earlier effective date based on additional service department records if, as here, the “claimant failed to provide sufficient information for VA to identify and obtain the records.”  The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred in concluding that the § 3.156(c)(2) exception applies to his case.

The Court, in rejecting the Secretary’s argument that the addition of subsection (c)(2) was only intended as a clarification of past practice, found that the proposed rule change for § 3.156(c)(2) did not indicate that it was VA’s regular practice to decline to reconsider prior claims where incomplete evidence was provided.  See 70 Fed. Reg. at 35,389.  The Court indicated that the plain language of § 3.156(c)(2), when compared to the plain language of pre-amendment § 3.156(c), created a bar to reconsideration based on newly associated service department records in particular circumstances where absolutely no bar previously existed.  Thus, the Court held that the addition of subsection (c)(2) was a substantive rule change.

The Court next considered whether or not the rule change may be applied retroactively.  Noting that “retroactivity is not favored by the law” and that statutes and regulations are not to be construed to have retroactive effect unless required by their language, the Court applied the *Princess Cruises* test to determine if § 3.156(c)(2) was intended to have retroactive effect.  Note: In *Princess Cruises, Inc.v. United States*, the Federal Circuit held that three factors must be weighed to determine whether a regulation should be given retroactive effect:  (1) the nature and extent of the change of the law; (2) the degree of connection between the operation of the new rule and a relevant past event; and (3) familiar considerations of fair notice, reasonable reliance, and settled expectations.

The Court found that the nature and extent of the change in law to be substantial, which weighed strongly against giving retroactive affect to § 3.156(c)(2).  The Court also found a high degree of connection between the new rule and a relevant past event, which is the veteran’s submission of information in connection with his claim to reopen his previously denied claim for benefits for PTSD.  Lastly, the Court found that familiar considerations of fair notice, reasonable reliance, and settled expectations counseled against giving the amended regulation retroactive effect.

The Court concluded that because all three *Princess Cruises* factors weighed against giving retroactive effect to amended § 3.156(c)(2), the Board erred in retroactively applying the regulation to deny the veteran entitlement to an earlier effective date for service connection for PTSD.

The Court vacated the part of the Board decision that denied entitlement to an earlier effective date for service connection of PTSD and remanded the case for the Board to apply [38 C.F.R. § 3.156(c)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=3a77cec38b5c011cb54976b1ff82c1c0&node=se38.1.3_1156&rgn=div8) (2005) in determining the correct effective date for establishment of service connection for PTSD.
