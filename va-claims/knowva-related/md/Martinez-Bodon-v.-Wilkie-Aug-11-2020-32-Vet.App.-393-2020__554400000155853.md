Decision Assessment Document
Martinez-Bodon v. Wilkie, Aug 11, 2020, 32 Vet.App. 393 (2020)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that Saunders v. Wilkie, 886 F.3d 1356 (Fed. Cir. 2018), which defined a disability under 38 U.S.C. 1110 as a functional impairment of earning capacity, applies broadly to include more than pain, but includes any condition that results in functional impairment of earning capacity.

However, with respect to psychiatric disabilities, since VA, under 38 C.F.R. 4.125 and 4.130, has expressly limited compensation to disabilities conforming to a diagnosis under the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), a psychiatric condition must have a DSM-5 diagnosis to be eligible for compensation.  Furthermore, the Court held that it lacks jurisdiction to review the Secretary’s decision to require such a diagnosis as set out in the Rating Schedule.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in the Manual under III.iv.5.B.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, in April 2016, filed for service connection for diabetes mellitus and a mental condition he claimed was secondary to the diabetes mellitus.  During the subsequent VA examination in September 2016, the examiner noted the veteran’s reports of not sleeping well and feeling anxious about trembling in his right eye.  The examiner stated that since there were no criteria for a mental condition per DSM-5, she could not answer whether the veteran’s mental condition was due to diabetes.  The regional office (RO) awarded service connection for diabetes mellitus, but denied service connection for a mental disorder.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in June 2018, denied service connection for an anxiety disorder, finding that the veteran did not have a current diagnosed disability.  The Board relied upon the September 2016 VA examination that found that the veteran did not meet the DSM criteria for a mental condition.  The Board, although finding that the veteran was competent to report symptoms observable to a lay person, also found that the veteran was not competent to provide a diagnosis.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred, in contravention of Saunders, in requiring a diagnosis of a psychiatric disability in order to establish service connection.  He contended that the holding in Saunders extends to more than just pain and encompasses any undiagnosed disability that results in functional loss.

The Court, in determining whether Saunders was limited to pain or whether its analysis extends to include other manifestations of a condition without a diagnosis, held that Saunders was not limited to pain, as the definition of disability in section 1110 includes any conditions that results in functional impairment of earning capacity.

The Court held that this definition applies to any case that implicates section 1110, not just those involving pain, to include a psychiatric condition.  However, the Court held that 4.125(a) and 4.130 require that a psychiatric condition must have a DSM-5 diagnosis; thereby, limiting compensation for a psychiatric condition to those disabilities that have a diagnosis conformable to DSM-5.  If a diagnosis of a mental disorder does not confirm to DSM-5 or is unsupported by the findings of examination, 4.125(a) requires that the report be returned to the examiner to substantiate the diagnosis.  In assigning ratings for mental disorders, 4.130 requires that a mental condition be “formally diagnosed, but the symptoms are not severe enough either to interfere with occupational and social functioning or to require continuous medication.”

The Court, in analyzing 4.130, to include in the context of its requirement that rating agencies be thoroughly familiar with the DSM-5 to properly implement the regulatory directives and apply the rating criteria, observed that the upwardly cascading nature of the rating criteria plainly support that 4.130 requires a DSM-5 diagnosis in order to rate mental conditions at every rating level.  Furthermore, the Court indicated that to read 4.130 as requiring a diagnosis for a zero percent rating but not for higher ratings would lead to absurd results.  The Court, in considering § 4.125(a), found that the language of the regulation, when read in context with 4.130, clearly shows the Secretary’s intent to require a DSM-5 diagnosis as a precondition to compensate mental conditions.

The Court, in addressing the veteran’s argument that it should consider whether VA’s limitation on mental conditions that are diagnosed is consistent with section 1110 as the Federal Circuit defined disability in Saunders, held that it is precluded in reaching that question by Congress.  38 U.S.C. 7252(b) precludes the Court from reviewing the Rating Schedule adopted under 38 U.S.C. 1155 or the Secretary’s action in “adopting or revising the schedule.”  The Court indicated that it and the Federal Circuit have interpreted this statute as precluding its jurisdiction to review Rating Schedule regulations for consistency with section 1110.  Thus, the Court explained that it is without jurisdiction to review the propriety of the Secretary’s requirements for a DSM-5 diagnosis to compensate mental conditions.

The Court affirmed the Board’s decision.
