Decision Assessment Document
Morton v. West, Jul 14, 1999, 12 Vet.App. 477 (1999)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that VA has no duty to assist a claimant in developing a claim until a well-grounded claim was submitted.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court's holding was the catalyst for the Veterans Claims Assistance Act (VCAA), which was signed into law on November 9, 2000.  The clear intent of the VCAA was to overrule Morton and eliminate the well-grounded claim requirement from Title 38.  The law was intended to provide clear specifications concerning VA's duty to notify a claimant of the evidence necessary to substantiate a claim and VA's duty to assist a claim in obtaining evidence to substantiate a claim, as well as when VA would conduct an examination in compensation claims.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) determined that the veteran’s claims for service connection of multiple disabilities were not well grounded.  The Board found no evidence of nexus with respect to claims for hearing loss and a back disability.  Regarding a claim for varicose veins, the Board found no evidence, other than the veteran’s own testimony, that the condition existed in service and no evidence linking the condition to service.  The Board decision also noted that there was no medical evidence presented to suggest that the shortening or lengthening of the right upper extremity was due to the surgery the veteran had for his service-connected tuberculosis.  On appeal to the Court, the veteran did not address whether those claims were well grounded; in fact, he appeared to concede they were not.  Instead, the veteran argued that VA was required to assist him by fully developing the facts pertinent to his claim even though he had not submitted a well-grounded claim.  He argued that support for this requirement was found in 38 CFR 3.103(a), 3.159(a); M21-1, Part III, para. 1.03(a) and Part VI, para. 2.10(f); and policies set forth in two documents; a May 24, 1996, letter (All-station letter 95-56) from the director of the VA Compensation and Pension Service and VBA Circular 21-94-2, Change 2, Appendix A.

The Court found that, since the veteran had virtually conceded that his claims were not well grounded and, in any event, provided no argument to his brief to the contrary, the issue was abandoned.  The Court and the U. S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit have held that 38 U.S.C. 5107(a) requires that, if a well-grounded or plausible claim has not been submitted, there is no duty on the part of VA to assist in the claim’s full development.  The Court noted that rules fall into two main categories:  substantive or interpretive.  A rule is substantive in nature when it “has the force of law and narrowly limits administrative action.”  In contrast, an interpretive rule, “is one that merely clarifies or explains an existing rule or statute.”  The distinction between a substantive rule and an interpretive one can be significant for two reasons.  First, substantive rules are subject to the general requirement of prior notice and opportunity to comment while “interpretive rules, general statements of policy, or rules of agency organization, procedure, or practice” are excepted.  Second, substantive rules may confer enforceable rights, while internal guidelines and interpretive statements of a federal agency, that are mandated neither by a statute nor by the Constitution, cannot confer substantive rights on a claimant.  The Court then stated that the regulations, M21-1 provisions, and C&P policy relied upon by the veteran are, at most, administrative directions to the field containing guidance as to the procedures to be used in the adjudication process.  The Court stated that the interpretation of the claims development procedures of the referenced regulations M21-1, and the C&P policy stand in direct contravention of the command of 38 U.S.C. 5107.
