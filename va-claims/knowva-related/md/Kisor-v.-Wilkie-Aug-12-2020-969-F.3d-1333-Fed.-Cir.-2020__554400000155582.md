Decision Assessment Document
Kisor v. Wilkie, Aug 12, 2020, 969 F.3d 1333 (Fed. Cir. 2020)
United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit, concluding that the term “relevant” under 38 C.F.R. 3.156(c)(1) is not ambiguous, held that Auer deference was not appropriate in this case.  The Federal Circuit held that “relevant” has only one reasonable meaning, which is the meaning attributed to it by the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), which determined that, under 3.156(c)(1), in order to be “relevant,” a record must speak to a matter at issue, or, in other words, a matter in dispute, such as the basis for the denial in a prior VA decision.

(Under Auer v. Robbins, 519 U.S. 452 (1997), an agency’s interpretation of its own regulation is controlling unless the interpretation is plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation.)

**Impact on VBA:**

The Federal Circuit’s holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in the Manual M21-1 under III.iv.2.B.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served in the Marine Corps from 1962 to 1966, filed a claim for service connection for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in December 1982.  The RO received a February 1983 letter from a Vet Center counselor describing the veteran’s symptoms and indicating that the symptomatic pattern has been associated with PTSD.

The veteran underwent a VA examination in March 1983.  The examiner noted that the veteran had participated in Operation Harvest Moon in Vietnam, had come under enemy attack, and reported several contacts with snipers and occasional mortar rounds.  The veteran also was involved in a large ambush that resulted in 13 deaths in a Marine company.  The examiner diagnosed the veteran with intermittent explosive disorder and atypical personality disorder, rather than PTSD.  In the absence of a PTSD diagnosis, the RO denied the claim in May 1983.  The decision became final as the veteran did not appeal.

The veteran, on June 5, 2006, submitted a requested to reopen his PTSD claim.  While his claim was pending, he submitted supportive evidence to include a psychiatric evaluation diagnosing PTSD, his DD Form 214, a combat history concerning his participation in Operation Harvest Moon, and a copy of the February 1983 Vet Center letter.  The RO subsequently made a Formal Finding of Information Required to Document the Claimed Stressor based on the veteran’s statements, his service records (which verified service in Vietnam with the 2nd Battalion, 7th Marines) and a daily log from his unit, which detailed the combat events the veteran described in connection with his claim.

A September 2007 VA examination yielded a diagnosis of PTSD.  The RO reopened the claim and granted service connection for PTSD with a 50 percent evaluation from June 5, 2006.  The RO indicated that the decision was based upon the July 2007 psychiatric evaluation report, September 2007 VA examination, and the formal stressor finding.  The RO explained that service connection was warranted because the VA examination diagnosed PTSD due to Vietnam experiences and the record showed the veteran was a combat veteran, due to receipt of the Combat Action Ribbon.  The veteran appealed the effective date.

The veteran argued before the Board that the proper effective date for service connection for PTSD was the date of his initial claim in December 1982.  He contended that the RO committed clear and unmistakable error (CUE) in its May 1983 decision, which denied service connection for PTSD.

The Board found no CUE or duty to assist failures in the 1983 RO decision.  The Board also addressed whether an earlier effective date was warranted under § 3.156(c)(1).  Although acknowledging that the RO received service records documenting the veteran’s participation in Operation Harvest Moon after the May 1983 decision, the Board concluded that the records were not relevant for purposes of 3.156(c)(1) because the RO, in May 1983, denied service connection for PTSD due to the lack of a PTSD diagnosis, and service connection can only be granted if there is a current disability.  The Board indicated that the basis of denial in May 1983 was that a diagnosis of PTSD was not warranted, not whether or not the veteran engaged in combat with the enemy in service.

The veteran argued before the Veterans Court that the Board erred in failing to consider and apply the provisions of 3.156(c).  The Court rejected the argument, noting that the service records contained after the 1983 decision did not contain a diagnosis of PTSD, as the absence of such diagnosis was the basis of the 1983 RO decision.

The Federal Circuit, holding in its September 2017 decision that the meaning of the term “relevant” is ambiguous under 3.156(c)(1), upheld the Board’s determination that the supplemental personnel records were not relevant because they contained information that (1) was already known, acknowledged, and undisputed in a previous final regional office (RO) decision, and (2) did not purport to affect the outcome of that decision.

The Supreme Court, in Kisor v. Wilkie 139 S. Ct. 2400 (2019), held that the Federal Circuit was too hasty to extend Auer deference to the Board’s interpretation of “relevant” under 3.156(c)(1).  The Supreme vacated the Federal Circuit’s decision and remanded the case for the Federal Circuit to decide whether Auer deference applies to the agency interpretation at issue.  The Supreme Court advised that a court should not afford Auer deference unless the regulation is generally ambiguous.

In response to the Federal Circuit’s request to the parties as how to proceed following the Supreme Court remand, the parties agreed that the term “relevant” under  3.156(c) is not ambiguous; thereby, rendering Auer deference inappropriate.  The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that the only reasonable reading of the regulation is that a service department record is “relevant” if it has any tendency to make the existence of any fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action more probable or less probable than it would be without the evidence.  The government’s position was that the relevant record must affect the outcome of the proceeding, meaning that it must speak to the basis for the prior VA decision.

The Federal Circuit, agreeing with the government, concluded that the term “relevant” under 3.156(c)(1) is not ambiguous and that, in the context of the regulation, the only reasonable meaning is that, for a record to be relevant, it must address a dispositive issue and therefore affect the outcome of the case.

The Federal Circuit observed that the veteran’s claim for service connection for PTSD was denied in 1983 because he had not been diagnosed with PTSD, not because of an absence of a stressor, which has never been disputed.  For this reason, the Federal Circuit again affirmed the decision of the Veterans Court that affirmed the Board’s decision denying the veteran an effective date prior to June 5, 2006, for service connection for PTSD.

The Federal Circuit stated that duplicative records and records directed to an undisputed fact would not be a basis for retroactive award under 3.156(c).  A veteran filing such records could not obtain a award “based all or in part on” newly identified records under the regulation.  In this case, the records at issue did not speak to the basis for the VA’s prior decision, which was the absence of a PTSD diagnosis, not because of any dispute as to whether he had an in-service stressor.

The Federal Circuit concluded that the Board did not err in holding that the records cited by the veteran were not “relevant” because they did not pertain to the basis of the 1983 denial, the lack of a PTSD diagnosis.  The Federal Circuit found that the records added nothing to the case because they addressed a matter relating to service connection for PTSD, the in-service stressor, which was not in dispute.

The Federal Circuit upheld the Veterans Court decision that affirmed the Board’s decision denying the veteran an effective date earlier than June 5, 2006, for service connection for PTSD.
