Decision Assessment Document
 Miller v. Shulkin, Mar 6, 2017, 28 Vet.App. 376 (2017)
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the note  in 38 C.F.R. 4.124a concerning the evaluation of peripheral nerve disabilities with wholly sensory manifestations provides only a maximum disability rating for wholly sensory manifestations of incomplete paralysis of peripheral nerves.  As the note does not contain mention of non-sensory manifestations, the Court declined to read into the regulation a corresponding minimum disability rating for non-sensory manifestations.

(The note in § 4.124a reads:  “When the involvement is wholly sensory, the rating should be for the mild, or at most, the moderate degree.”)

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran is rated 10 percent disabling for diabetic peripheral neuropathy of the left foot under 38 C.F.R. 4.124a, Diagnostic Code (DC) 8620.

VA examinations and private medical evidence from 2009 through 2015 showed the left foot peripheral neuropathy to be manifested by such symptoms as numbness, tingling, and burning.  The veteran’s gait was abnormal and he used a cane to assist with ambulation due to the left foot neuropathy.  He stated that he could not feel the floor beneath him and often felt as if he was going to fall.   No muscle weakness or atrophy or other organic changes were shown.  VA examiners in 2014 and 2015 assessed the peripheral neuropathy as consistent with mild incomplete paralysis of the left sciatic nerve.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), June 2015, denied the appeal for entitlement to an evaluation in excess of 10 percent for the peripheral neuropathy of the left foot.  The Board found that the record showed pain and intermittent sensory deficits of the left foot.  The Board found that the veteran does not frequently fall due to the left foot peripheral neuropathy and ultimately concluded that there was no basis for a higher evaluation, as there was no muscle atrophy, loss of reflexes, or absent sensation found on objective examination.

The veteran argued before the Court that because 4.124a states that peripheral nerve disorders that are wholly sensory are to be rated as mild or moderate at most – 10 and 20 percent ratings, respectively, under DC 8620 – a peripheral nerve disorder that is more than wholly sensory must be rated at least moderately severe and assigned an evaluation of 40 percent under DC 8620.  He contended that because the Board did not addres this theory, it failed to adequately explain why he was not entitled to a disability rating in excess of 10 percent.

Both parties agreed that the veteran’s peripheral neuropathy was more than wholly sensory in nature; however, the Secretary argued that the “wholly sensory” language of 4.124a creates a ceiling for a maximum disability for wholly sensory conditions, rather than a floor – or minimum disability rating – for conditions that are more than wholly sensory.

The Court held that the plain language of the 4.124a note provides only a maximum disability rating for wholly sensory manifestations of incomplete paralysis of a peripheral nerve.  Since the note contained no mention of non-sensory manifestations, the Court declined to read into the regulation a corresponding minim disability rating for non-sensory manifestations.  However, the Court indicated that it does not logically follow that a veteran who also exhibits non-sensory manifestations must necessarily be rated at a higher level, as there was no support for such proposition in the regulation.

The Court found that the Board’s reasons or bases were sufficient, as itconsidered both the objective and subjective evidence of record and concluded that, on the whole, the veteran’s disability was properly evaluated at 10 percent.

The Court affirmed the Board’s decision.
