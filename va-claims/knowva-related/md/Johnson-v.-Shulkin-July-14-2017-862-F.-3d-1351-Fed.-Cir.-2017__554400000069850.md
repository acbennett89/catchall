Decision Assessment Document
Johnson v. Shulkin, July 14, 2017, 862 F. 3d 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2017)
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court erred in in giving an overly broad reading of the term “systemic therapy” in Diagnostic Code (DC) 7806 that encompasses any and all forms of topical corticosteroid treatment.

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court incorrectly read DC 7806 as unambiguously elevating any form of corticosteroid treatment, including any degree of topical corticosteroid treatment, to the level of systemic therapy.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change, as the Federal Circuit agreed with VA’s position concerning the interpretations of “systemic therapy” and “topical therapy” under DC 7806.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran is service connected for a skin condition that is rated 10 percent disabling under DC 7806.  A November 2013 VA examination disclosed that the veteran had used topical corticosteroids (triamcinolone cream) constantly for a period of 12 months.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in its April 2014 decision, denied a higher evaluation on the bases that the skin condition affected less than 20 percent of exposed areas of the body and that the veteran had not used systemic therapy for a duration of six weeks or more during any 12 month period.

The Veterans Court reversed the Board decision, holding that the plain wording of DC 7806 is that systemic therapy includes topically applied corticosteroids.

The Secretary argued before the Federal Circuit that the Veterans Court erred by interpreting DC 7806 to unambiguously define all forms of administering corticosteroids –including topical application – to be “systemic therapy.”  The Secretary contended that this interpretation would allow a veteran with a skin condition affecting less than five percent of his body or his exposure area, otherwise entitled to a rating of zero percent, to increase the rating to 60 percent by using a topical over-the-counter hydrocortisone treatment constantly or near constantly.

The Federal Circuit agreed with the Secretary that the Veterans Court incorrectly read 7806 as unambiguously elevating any form of corticosteroid treatment, to include topical corticosteroids, to the level of “systemic therapy.”

The Federal Circuit held that the structure and content of DC 7806 make clear that it contemplates two types of therapy, “systemic therapy” and “topical therapy,” as the operative terms of the diagnostic code, not the exemplary reference – “such as” – to corticosteroids.  The Federal Circuit explained that that the use of “such as” in DC 7806 does not mean that all forms of treatment with “corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive drugs,” no matter how narrowly localized in their impact, count as “systemic therapy.”

The Federal Circuit characterized DC 7806 as drawing a clear distinction between “systemic therapy” and “topical therapy” as the operative terms of the diagnostic code.  DC 7806 provides that a veteran who requires at least some form of “systemic therapy” to receive a rating of 10 percent or higher, but one who requires “no more than topical therapy” to receive a zero percent evaluation.  The Federal Circuit indicated that the Veterans Court analysis of DC 7806 did not take into account this last provision in the code, which governs ratings for treatment with a “topical therapy” as opposed to a “systemic therapy.”

The Federal Circuit noted that the parties agreed that a topical corticosteroid treatment could conceivably be “administered on a large enough scale” to affect the body as a whole, which could fit the definition of systemic therapy.  However, the mere possibility that the use of a topical corticosteroid could amount to systemic therapy in some cases does not mean all applications of topical corticosteroids constitute systemic therapy, particularly if the use of topical corticosteroids affect only the area to which it is applied.  This was not the case here, as the veteran did not challenge the Board’s factual findings that his use of topical corticosteroids affected only the local area to which he applied treatment and did not affect his body as a whole.

The Federal Circuit reversed the Veterans Court holding and remanded for the Veterans Court to reinstate the Board’s otherwise unchallenged factual findings.
