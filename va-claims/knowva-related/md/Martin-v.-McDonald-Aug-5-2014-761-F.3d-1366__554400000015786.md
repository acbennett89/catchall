Decision Assessment Document
 Martin v. McDonald, Aug 5, 2014, 761 F.3d 1366
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that a military discharge for “alcohol rehabilitation failure” does not always constitute willful misconduct regardless of circumstances.  VA must make findings as to whether any conduct constituted misconduct and whether it was engaged in with the state of mind required for willfulness, which led to rehabilitation-failure determination.  Separation from service due to alcohol rehabilitation failure, alone, is not a basis for a finding of willful misconduct.

The Federal Circuit found that it could not uphold the denial of benefits in this case without relying on an incorrect rule of law that alcohol rehabilitation failure always results from willful misconduct.

**Impact on VBA:**

[M21-1, MR, III.v.1.D.16](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014222/) will be revised to indicate that alcohol rehabilitation failure alone may not be considered willful misconduct.  There must be an actual finding of actual willful misconduct that led to an alcohol rehabilitation failure.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served in the Army from January 1988 to April 1990, was honorably discharged  with “alcohol rehabilitation failure” as the reason for separation.  His application for educational assistance benefits under Chpater 30, Title 38, was denied by the regional office in August 2008.  He appealed to the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), arguing that he qualified for benefits under [38 U.S.C. § 3011](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/3011). Note: The statute authorizes educational assistance for veterans in various circumstances.  Section 3011(a)(1)(A)(ii) authorizes benefits for a veteran who was discharged or released from active duty for certain reasons, to include “a physical or mental condition that was not characterized as a disability and did not result from the individual’s own willful misconduct but did interfere with the individual‘s performance of duty.”

The Board denied the application for education benefits in September 2009, citing VA’s regulation that defines willful misconduct, [38 C.F.R. § 3.1(n)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=3165a9d922f4a0154708cc186233f43c&node=se38.1.3_11&rgn=div8), and indicating that “the law generally precludes compensation for primary alcohol and drug abuse disabilities.”  The Board concluded that the record showed that the veteran was discharged for alcohol abuse, which is characterized as willful misconduct.  The Board did not discuss evidence of any specific act that amounted to willful misconduct. Note: The Board erroneously stated that the veteran was discharged due to “alcohol abuse-rehabilitative failure.”  The veteran was discharged for “alcohol rehabilitation failure.”

The Veterans Court affirmed the Board’s decision, agreeing with the Board’s conclusion that the law generally precludes compensation for alcohol-related disabilities.  The Veterans Court also did not discuss any specific acts by the veteran.

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that the Veterans Court committed legal error in affirming the Board's conclusion that his discharge rested on willful misconduct under section 3011(a)(1)(A)(ii), without specific inquiry into or findings about the veteran’s individual conduct relating to his “alcohol rehabilitation failure.”

The Federal Circuit agreed, holding that it was legal error to rule that willful misconduct was present without an inquiry into and findings about the particular conduct leading to alcohol rehabilitation failure.  The Federal Circuit explained that there are three requirements for finding “willful misconduct”:  (1) the Board has to identify *conduct*; (2) the identified conduct must be *misconduct*; and (3) the identified conduct must be *willful*.

The Federal Circuit stated that “alcohol rehabilitation failure” cannot be said to always constitute or result from willful misconduct.  Furthermore, it may not even refer to misconduct, but to a state of mind, which is not the desired result of a rehabilitation program.

The Federal Circuit referenced three disability statutes, [38 U.S.C. §§ 105(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/105), [1110](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1110), and [1131](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1131), that deny compensation for any injury or disease that resulted from the veteran’s “own willful misconduct *or* abuse of alcohol or drugs.”  The Federal Circuit noted that Congress, in November 1990, added the phrase “or abuse of alcohol or drugs” to the pre-existing “willful misconduct” language in the statutes, which the Federal Circuit found to suggest that not all abuse of alcohol always constituted willful misconduct.  The Federal Circuit stated that this contrast underscores that alcohol abuse does not always constitute willful misconduct for purposes of denying basic education-assistance benefits.

The Federal Circuit vacated and remanded the Veterans Court decision.  The Federal Circuit directed that if the Secretary continues to deny benefits, the Board, following appropriate procedures for needed record development should make further factual findings.
