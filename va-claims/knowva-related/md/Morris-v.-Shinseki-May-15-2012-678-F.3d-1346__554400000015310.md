Decision Assessment Document
 Morris v. Shinseki, May 15, 2012, 678 F.3d 1346
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that because, pursuant to [38 C.F.R. § 3.303(c)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/38/3.303), a personality disorder is not an injury or disease within the meaning of [38 U.S.C. § 1110](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1110), the finding of a personality disorder in service does not implicate the presumption of soundness under [38 U.S.C. § 1111](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1111).

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons**:

The veteran served in the Army from July 31, 1964, to October 6, 1964.  His entrance examination showed no psychiatric abnormality.  The veteran reported nervous trouble and other symptoms during a September 1964 separation examination; however, no psychiatric abnormality was shown.  A clinical record from October 6, 1964, contained a diagnosis of chronic, moderate, unchanged passive-aggressive reaction, which was manifested by periods of anxiety and inability to express anger.

In January 1966, the veteran filed for service connection for a psychiatric disorder, claiming he suffered mental and physical abuse from his platoon sergeant while in basic training, which caused him to experience a nervous breakdown.  The regional office (RO), in May 1966, denied the veteran’s claim, finding no evidence of treatment for a nervous condition during service and that the veteran’s condition shown in service was in a chronic stage that had existed prior to service.

The RO denied the veteran’s January 1986 attempt to reopen the claim.  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in February 1988, determined that the veteran had submitted new and material evidence to reopen the claim, but denied the claim on the merits, finding that the evidence did not show that his psychiatric symptoms were due to other than a personality disorder.  The Board noted that under [§ 3.303(c),](http://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/38/3.303) a personality disorder is not a disease for which compensation benefits may be paid under applicable law.

While his appeal was before the Board, the veteran submitted to the RO medical evidence, which was received on May 5, 1987.  After denials by the RO and Board, the Veterans Court remanded the case in March 1992.  Following receipt of additional medical evidence, to include the opinion of an independent medical examiner, the Board, in February 1993, reopened the claim and awarded service connection for schizophrenia.  The RO awarded a 100 percent evaluation and assigned an effective date from May 5, 1987, because that was the date it received additional evidence from the veteran.  The Board, in 1996, denied the veteran’s claim that the 1966 RO decision was clearly and unmistakable erroneous (CUE).

In September 2004, the veteran filed a motion before the Board alleging that the Board’s 1988 decision contained CUE because the Board failed to correctly apply [38 U.S.C. § 105(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/105), 1110, and 1111.  He argued that the Board improperly relied upon the existence in his service medical records of a non-compensable psychiatric condition – personality disorder – as a basis for denying compensation.  Rather, he contended that the Board should have relied upon the presumption of service connection under section 105(a) and the presumption of soundness under section 1111 to award compensation under section 1110 for a psychiatric disorder.

The Board, in September 2008, denied the CUE motion, rejecting the veteran’s contentions that the Board, in 1988, failed to correctly apply the aforementioned laws identified by the veteran.  The Board stated that section 105(a) pertains to line of duty and misconduct considerations, neither of which was before the Board in 1988.  The Board rejected the veteran’s section 1111 argument, noting that the denial of service connection for an acquired psychiatric disability was made on the basis that an acquired psychiatric disability was not incurred in service, not on the grounds that a psychiatric disorder preexisted service and was not aggravated therein.

The Veterans Court, in October 2010, rejected these same arguments, indicating that the veteran had offered no evidence that the 1988 Board incorrectly considered his condition a personality disorder.  The Court also noted that it had previously held that section 1111 does not apply to a personality disorder under § 3.303(c).  *See Winn v. Brown*, 8 Vet.App. 510, 516 (1996).

The Federal Circuit, in summarizing the veteran’s arguments, understood that he was attempting to avoid the bar under § 3.303(c) that a personality disorder is not a disease or injury within the meaning of section 1110, by arguing that the 1988 Board erred by failing to grant him the presumption of soundness.

The Federal Circuit rejected this argument, observing that if, according to § 3.303(c), a condition is not an injury or disease within the meaning of section 1110, then section 1111 and the presumption of soundness does not come into play.  Thus, the Federal Circuit held that the Board did not commit CUE when it denied his claim for service connection without applying section 1111.

The Federal Circuit noted that once a diagnosis is made, a determination of causality – relationship to service – is made, and it is only at this stage that the presumption of soundness is relevant.  However, the Court concluded that it was unnecessary to examine causality in this case, as personality disorders cannot legally be service related.

The Federal Circuit also rejected the veteran’s argument that a personality disorder is a compensable disability unless it is shown to exist prior to service, finding that the language of § 3.303(c) is incompatible with the argument.  The Court also concluded that VA General Counsel Precedent Opinions 67-90 (July 18, 1990) and 82-90 (July 18, 1990) do not help the veteran.  Contrary to his arguments, these opinions do no support the argument that personality disorders are entitled to the presumption of soundness.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court decision.
