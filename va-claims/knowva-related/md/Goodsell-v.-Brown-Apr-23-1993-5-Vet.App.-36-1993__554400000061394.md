Decision Assessment Document
 Goodsell v. Brown, Apr 23, 1993, 5 Vet.App. 36 (1993)
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that even if VA were to conclude that any presumption of service connection was rebutted on the basis of evidence of record adequately developed, that would not compel denial of a claim of service connection for a chronic disease.  A claimant may still establish “direct” service connection on the basis of evidence that the disease was actually incurred or aggravated in active service.

The Court held that VA's conclusion that the  condition's onset was “well after” service or the presumption period appears to place on the appellant the burden of establishing by a preponderance of evidence that the condition did have its onset during such time.  Such a burden is inconsistent with the statutory benefit-of-the-doubt rule.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served in the Army 10/42-11/45 and had no relevant complaints or abnormalities during that time.  Chest x-rays for enlistment in the National Guard in 9/50 revealed active pulmonary tuberculosis for the first time.  The veteran had no subsequent service.  A hospital discharge summary in 1951 indicated that the veteran recalled working in a filling station in 1946 and 1947 with a man who was later found to have had TB.  While the examiner noted that the "patient was more or less exposed during that period," this statement did not articulate a conclusion about TB onset or causation in the Court's opinion.  A comparative review of chest x-rays in 1951 indicated no evidence of TB in 1942, 1943, or 1945, and that moderately advanced reinfection-type TB was first shown in 11/50.  There was again no opinion about causation or date of onset (the 9/50 films were not mentioned).  Service connection was denied in 1952, and the decision was not appealed.  The veteran applied to reopen his claim in 1989, and submitted several medical opinions that TB may have been present in service or the presumptive period even though it was not clinically apparent. The BVA held that there was no evidence of TB prior to 9/50 and that there was evidence of intercurrent exposure.

The Court determined that there was new and material evidence to reopen the claim since competent medical opinions indicated that TB may have been present in service or within the presumptive period.  The BVA had not considered the credibility and probative value of these opinions, and evidence relating to intercurrent exposure did not rebut direct or presumptive service connection (mere "exposure" was not necessarily "an intercurrent injury or disease," and independent medical evidence would be required to show that it constitutes "affirmative evidence" against presumptive service connection).  Equipoise considerations were to be addressed because the medical opinions provided significant evidence of TB in service or within the presumptive period.
