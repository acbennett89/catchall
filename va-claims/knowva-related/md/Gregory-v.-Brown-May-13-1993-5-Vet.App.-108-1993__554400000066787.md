Decision Assessment Document
 Gregory v. Brown, May 13, 1993, 5 Vet.App. 108 (1993)
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that certain conduct subsequent to the time of separation may be relevant in an appropriate case with respect to the question of fault at the time of separation.  Given the plain meaning of 38 U.S.C. 101(3) and the inconsistency of 38 C.F.R. 3.53(a) which eliminates the second part of the two-part test in the statute, the Court held that the first sentence of 3.53(a) is unlawful, as exceeding the authority of the VA.

The Court held that the language of the statute does not indicate that the without-fault requirement is a continuing one.  Fault or the absence of fault is to be determined based on an analysis of conduct at the time of the separation.

**Impact on VBA:**

The guidance from the Court's holding has been incorporated into Manual M21-1, III.iv.5.C.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

On her original application for death benefits, the appellant indicated that she and the veteran had married on 1/14/47, separated on 7/4/51, and that she had been awarded a divorce for mental cruelty on 2/8/52.  The veteran died on 4/27/52.  An interlocutory judgment of divorce was granted on 2/8/52 and no final judgment was entered prior to the veteran's death.  The appellant failed to answer an August 1952 development letter.  When reopening her claim in May 1988, the appellant stated that the marriage had ended by his death and listed three subsequent marriages with the first remarriage in July 1952.  She indicated that she had been receiving Social Security Administration (SSA) benefits since March 1988.  In May 1989, she stated that the veteran suffered from shell shock and had a drinking problem.  Separations were due to his problems.  She obtained the divorce because she was worried that he would try to get custody of their son.  She also submitted two affidavits attesting to the brief separation in 1952.  She testified at a hearing that the veteran's drinking problem had endangered the life of their son on at least one occasion.  The Board of Veterans' Appeals denied the appeal on the basis that the appellant was not completely free of fault in the separation and the continuation thereof.
