Decision Assessment Document
 Massie v. Shinseki, Jul 29, 2013, 724 F.3d 1325
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that [38 C.F.R. § 3.157(b)(1)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=f77b6fe59c4300f8e3ff663c0639111f&node=se38.1.3_1157&rgn=div8) requires a report of a specific, dated examination indicating that a veteran’s disability has worsened to qualify as an informal claim for increased benefits based on a “report of examination.”

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, on April 4, 2001, filed a claim for increased evaluation for his varicose veins, which had been rated 50 percent disabling since 1990.  He submitted medical evidence that included a May 1999 letter from a VA physician who stated that he had treated the veteran for multiple medical problems to include chronic venous insufficiency that had “persisted in spite of prior surgical treatment with vein stripping” and the condition left him with “significant pain when he [was] on his feet for any period of time.”

The regional office, in December 2005, granted a 100 percent evaluation effective April 4, 2001.  The veteran argued that VA failed to consider and apply [38 U.S.C. § 5110(b)(2)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5110) and [38 C.F.R. § 3.400(o)(2)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=f77b6fe59c4300f8e3ff663c0639111f&node=se38.1.3_1400&rgn=div8), which would allow an effective date of April 4, 2000, for the 100 percent evaluation.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in May 2009, found that there was no evidence that the VA physician who authored the May 1999 letter treated the veteran between April 2000 and April 2001.  The Board determined that the April 4, 2001, effective date for the 100 percent evaluation was correct.

The Veterans Court, in affirming the Board’s decision, held that the VA physician’s letter could not possibly qualify as a report of examination under [§ 3.157(b)(1)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=f77b6fe59c4300f8e3ff663c0639111f&node=se38.1.3_1157&rgn=div8) because it was not generated in connection with any particular VA medical examination.  The Veterans Court also held that the regulation requires that a report of examination or hospitalization must demonstrate that the veteran’s service-connected disability worsened since the time it was last evaluated for rating purposes for VA to treat the medical record as an informal claim.

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that the Veterans Court misinterpreted § 3.157(b)(1) to require that a letter from a treating physician expressly relate to specific, identifiable outpatient or hospital examination to qualify as a “report of examination” as a basis for an informal claim for increased benefits.  The veteran contended that such a narrow interpretation of the regulation, when the letter was generated by a VA physician and constructively present in the VA’s records, is contrary to the provisions of [38 C.F.R. § 5107(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5107) and [7104(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/7104), which require the Board to consider all relevant evidence and applicable laws and regulations.

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court did not commit error in interpreting [§ 3.157(b)(1)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=f77b6fe59c4300f8e3ff663c0639111f&node=se38.1.3_1157&rgn=div8) to require that a report of examination must identify a specific, particular examination and indicate that the veteran’s disability has worsened to qualify as an informal claim for increased benefits.  The Federal Circuit elaborated that a report of examination could be based on more than one examination; however, to qualify as an informal claim for increased benefits, a “report of examination” must identify at least one specific examination by date.

The Federal Circuit observed that [38 U.S.C. § 5110(b)(3)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5110) provides that the effective date of an award of increased benefits is the earliest ascertainable date “that an increase in disability had occurred” up to one year before the date of receipt of that informal claim.  Although § 3.157(b)(1) does not expressly require that the report indicate the veteran’s service-connected disability has worsened, the Federal Circuit held that any contrary interpretation would be inconsistent with the statute, as well as nonsensical,  because under such reading any subsequent medical record could trigger an informal claim under § 3.157(b)(1).

The Federal Circuit also rejected the argument that the Veterans Court decision contravened sections [5107(b)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5107) and [7104(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/7104), as it found that the Board did specifically consider the 1999 VA physician’s letter, but did not need to expressly discuss whether that letter was an informal claim for increase benefits because, as the Veterans Court found, it was not reasonably raised in the record.  Thus, the Board did not have to evaluate the letter as an informal claim for increased benefits under § 3.157(b)(1).

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court decision.
