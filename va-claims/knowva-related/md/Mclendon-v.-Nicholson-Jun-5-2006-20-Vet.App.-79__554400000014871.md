Decision Assessment Document
 Mclendon v. Nicholson, Jun 5, 2006, 20 Vet.App. 79
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that medical evidence that suggests a nexus but is too equivocal or lacking in specificity to support a decision on the merits still triggers the duty to assist regarding medical examinations/opinions if it indicates that the veteran’s condition “may be associated” with service.  If the first three elements of [38 U.S.C. § 5103A(d)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) and [38 C.F.R. § 3.159(c)(4)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=1921041e5531a1d397b6f30450a6127d&node=se38.1.3_1159&rgn=div8) are met, a VA examination must be ordered, unless there is sufficient competent medical evidence on file for VA to make a decision without providing an examination.

The Court stated that the third element, which requires that the evidence indicates that there “may” be a nexus between a current disability and an in-service injury, disease, or event, is a **low threshold**.  Examples of a former paratrooper with arthritic knees and a veteran’s credible testimony of ringing in the ears with noise exposure in service would be sufficient to show that the disabilities may be related to service and require VA to provide an examination.  [See 146 CONG. REC. H9912, H9917 (2000)](http://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/CREC-2000-10-17/html/CREC-2000-10-17-pt1-PgH9912.htm) (statement of Rep. Evans); [*Charles v. Principi*, 16 Vet.App. 370 (2002)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014756/).

Regarding these examples, the Court found that the veteran’s testimony concerning continuation of pain since service, if credible, to be sufficient to satisfy the third element that the current disability “may be associated” with his in-service injury.  Third element evidence is not limited to medical evidence that suggests a nexus.  Credible evidence of continuity of symptomatology such as pain or other symptoms are capable of being shown by lay observation.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting Manual revision or regulatory change.  Adjudicators should be aware of this decision and be able to apply the holding in evaluating evidence and fulfilling the duty to assist regarding examinations/opinions under [section 5103A(d)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) and [§ 3.159(c)(4)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=1921041e5531a1d397b6f30450a6127d&node=se38.1.3_1159&rgn=div8).  The holding also indicates that adjudicators cannot arbitrarily determine in-service injuries to be “acute and transitory” without relying on competent medical evidence determining that chronic disability was not demonstrated.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, serving honorably from December 1963 to December 1967, filed a claim for service connection for a low back disability in May 2001.  Although he did not seek medical treatment in service, the veteran stated that he injured his back when falling on a steel lifting ring.  Opinions from two private physicians were submitted, which noted that the disability could have been caused by the in-service injury.  Dr. M indicated, “The process of degeneration may have been initiated by the fall that he had onto his lower back.”  Dr. B, referring to the veteran’s history, suggested that it was within the “realm of medical possibility” that the fall may have produced chronic disability.

The regional office (RO) denied the claim without providing a VA examination.  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in upholding the RO denial, rejected the two private medical opinions as incompetent because they relied on history provided by the veteran and were otherwise speculative and not definitive as to whether the low back disability was related to service.  The Board concluded that a VA examination was unnecessary because the evidence of record was sufficient to decide the claim.

The Court provided an extensive discussion of the four duty to assist elements in regards to determining a need for medical examination.  The elements are:

1. Competent evidence of a current disability or persistent or recurrent symptoms of a disability;
2. In-service event, injury, or disease or disease or symptoms of a disease (listed in certain regulatory provisions) manifesting during an applicable presumptive period;
3. Evidence indicates that a disability, or persistent or recurrent symptoms of disability “may be associated” with service; and
4. Insufficient competent medical evidence on file to make a decision without providing an examination. If these four elements are met, then VA must provide or obtain a medical examination or opinion.

The first element requires no weighing of facts, but only an assessment that the evidence is competent.  The Board’s determination that the veteran suffers from a current low back disability satisfied this element.  Although the Board could have found the veteran’s report of an in-service injury to not be credible, it determined that the evidence of record shows an acute injury in service, satisfying the second element, which involves weighing of facts.

Concerning the third element, the Court found that the Board erred by weighing the evidence and determining that the private medical opinions could not establish a medical nexus, rather than assessing whether the medical opinions, or any other evidence, “indicated” that the in-service injury “may be associated” with the current disability.

Although not establishing a nexus, the private opinions suggested a nexus between the current disability and service.  When such a nexus is “indicated” between a current disability and an in-service event, a medical opinion must be obtained that provides some definitive determination as to the degree of likelihood that a disability was caused by service to constitute sufficient medical evidence on which the Board can render a decision, on the merits, regarding nexus.

The Board also erred by finding that the veteran’s in-service back injury was acute without chronic residual disability.  Since the Board determined that the veteran suffered an in-service back injury, the degree of that injury and whether any disabilities resulted therefrom are medical assessments, which the Board is not competent to render.  *See Colvin v. Derwinski*, 1 Vet.App. 171 (1991).  The Court concluded that there was not sufficient competent medical evidence to make a decision; thus, the Board erred in making this determination.

The Court set aside the Board decision and remanded with instructions that the Board determine whether the third element has been met.  If the Board finds that the third element has been met, the veteran is to be provided with a VA examination because there would be insufficient medical evidence to decide the claim.
