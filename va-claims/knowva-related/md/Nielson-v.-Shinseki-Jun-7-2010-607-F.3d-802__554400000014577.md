Decision Assessment Document
 Nielson v. Shinseki, Jun 7, 2010, 607 F.3d 802
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that a “service trauma” under [38 U.S.C. § 1712(a)(1)(C)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1712) is an injury or wound produced by an external physical force during the performance of military duties, and does not include the intended result of proper medical treatment.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served in the Air Force from 1950 to 1954, had all but three of his teeth removed at a field hospital in Korea without anesthesia.  The remaining teeth were removed upon return to the United States prior to separation.  The regional office (RO), in February 1992, granted service connection for the extraction of his teeth, except his wisdom teeth, and assigned a noncompensable evaluation.  The RO determined that the extractions were not due to “service trauma” or “combat dental injuries.”

The Salt Lake City VA medical center denied the veteran’s application for outpatient dental treatment under section 1712(a)(1)(C) because he did not have a service-connected dental condition or disability determined to be the result of combat wounds or other injuries.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) upheld the denial of outpatient dental treatment on the basis that extraction of the veteran’s teeth by military dentists during service due to periodontal infection did not constitute service trauma.  The Board relied upon a VA Office of General Counsel Opinion that held that “merely to have had dental extractions during service is not tantamount to dental trauma” and the term “service trauma” does not include the intended effects of treatment provided during military service.  See [VA Gen. Coun. Prec. 5-97](http://www.va.gov/ogc/docs/1997/PRC05-97.DOC) (January 22, 1997).

The Veterans Court, finding that the statutory language was unambiguous, held that the plain meaning of “service trauma” is an injury or wound violently produced while the injured or wounded person is in the armed forces.  Only those veterans with a dental disability caused by a particular type of event – combat wounds or other service trauma – are entitled to dental benefits under section 1712(a)(1)(C).

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that the Veterans Court misconstrued the term “service trauma” in section [1712 (a)(1)(C)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1712) and that the extraction of nearly all his teeth in service is a “service trauma.”

The Federal Circuit held that the statute was not so broad as to include the intended results of proper medical treatment provided by the military.  The Federal Circuit acknowledged that the pulling of teeth is an act of force that could fit within the “dental trauma” definition; however, it did not think that Congress intended to use the word “trauma” in that broad of a sense or intend to include proper dental treatment designed to remedy an injury or disease to be within the phrase “service trauma.”

The Federal Circuit explained that Congress’s use of “combat wounds or other service trauma” in the statute, suggested that it intended to include only injuries sustained during the performance of military duties.  The Federal Circuit also held that it was unreasonable to believe that Congress would have considered the intended results of proper medical treatment “of the same kind” as an injury sustained during combat.

The Federal Circuit also rejected the veteran’s argument that “service trauma” can be the psychological stress resulting from pulling of teeth without anesthesia and held that a “trauma” must be a physical injury under the statute.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court decision.
