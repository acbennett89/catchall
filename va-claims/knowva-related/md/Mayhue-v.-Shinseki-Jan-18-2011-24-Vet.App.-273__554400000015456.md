Decision Assessment Document
 Mayhue v. Shinseki, Jan 18, 2011, 24 Vet.App. 273
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that under either the pre-amended or current version of [38 C.F.R. § 3.156(c)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=2a34e6c1771ac2dee7cc1c61d40e28d4&node=se38.1.3_1156&rgn=div8), a claimant whose claim is reconsidered based on newly discovered service department records may be entitled to an effective date as early as the date of the original claim.  The Court also held [38 C.F.R. § 3.156(c)(2)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=2a34e6c1771ac2dee7cc1c61d40e28d4&node=se38.1.3_1156&rgn=div8) was inapplicable, even though the veteran failed to fully cooperate with VA’s requests for information to verify other stressors, because the information used to verify the stressor, such as unit number and dates served in a combat zone, was always a part of the claims file but was not considered initially because of administrative error.

Note: [38 C.F.R. § 3.156(c)(2)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=2a34e6c1771ac2dee7cc1c61d40e28d4&node=se38.1.3_1156&rgn=div8) provides:  “Paragraph (c)(1) of this section does not apply to records that VA could not have obtained when it decided the claim because the records did not exist when VA decided the claim, or because the claimant failed to provide sufficient information for VA to identify and obtain the records from the respective service department, the Joint Services Records Research Center, or from any other official source

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served in the Army from April 1968 to February 1970, to include duty in the Republic of Vietnam.  In November 1994, he filed a claim for service connection for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).  In a May 1995 VA examination, he described his stressors as consisting of (1) a friend killed during an enemy shelling who had used the veteran’s bed while the veteran was on guard duty, (2) being attacked while on “road duty,” and (3) pushing a Vietnamese child laden with explosives from a truck and watching the child disintegrate.  The regional office (RO), in denying the claim in August 1995, indicated that although there was indication of possible stressors in service during the examination report, the information was not capable of verification because specific names, dates, and places were not provided.  The veteran did not appeal the decision.

In September 2000, the veteran filed to reopen his PTSD service connection claim.  After the RO’s initial denial in September 2001, the veteran, in April 2002, submitted a completed stressor questionnaire, which included unit information and description of stressors reported during the 1995 VA examination.  Other stressors were described in later statements.  The RO denied the claim again and the veteran appealed.  The U.S. Armed Services Center for Research of Unit Records (CURR), in March 2005, reported that it was unable to verify his stressors described in the 2002 statements, but was able to verify that the veteran’s unit and base camp were attacked during the veteran’s tour of duty in Vietnam.

The RO, in April 2005, granted service connection for PTSD with a 50 percent evaluation, effective September 5, 2000.  The veteran filed a Notice of Disagreement in response, asserting entitlement to a higher initial evaluation for PTSD; an effective date of November 15, 1994; and a total evaluation based on individual unemployability (TDIU).  The RO eventually granted a 70 percent evaluation.  A TDIU evaluation was granted from April 1, 2005, based on evidence submitted showing that the veteran had not been employed since that date.  The RO denied the earlier effective date claim.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in September 2008, denied the veteran’s appeals for earlier effective dates for the PTSD service connection and the TDIU grants and a higher initial disability rating for PTSD.  The Board, applying [§ 3.156(c)(2)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=2a34e6c1771ac2dee7cc1c61d40e28d4&node=se38.1.3_1156&rgn=div8), determined that he could not seek an earlier effective date of service connection for PTSD under [§ 3.156(c)(1)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=2a34e6c1771ac2dee7cc1c61d40e28d4&node=se38.1.3_1156&rgn=div8) because sufficient information to verify his stressor was not provided until September 5, 2000.  The Board found the veteran’s PTSD most closely satisfied the 70 percent rating criteria and denied an effective date for the TDIU grant prior to April 1, 2005, because the evidence did not show that he was totally unable to secure or maintain gainful employment due to his service-connected disabilities prior to that date.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board misapplied [§ 3.156(c)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=2a34e6c1771ac2dee7cc1c61d40e28d4&node=se38.1.3_1156&rgn=div8) in determining the effective date for the PTSD service connection grant, failed to apply [§ 3.156(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=2a34e6c1771ac2dee7cc1c61d40e28d4&node=se38.1.3_1156&rgn=div8) when it treated his TDIU claim as different from his claim for a higher initial evaluation for PTSD, and failed to apply [38 C.F.R. § 4.3](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=97cd1294b0caf406821c77e2589acc40&node=se38.1.4_13&rgn=div8) in determining the initial evaluation for PTSD.  He also asserted that the Board did not provide an adequate statement of reasons or bases in denying a 100 percent schedular evaluation for PTSD because it failed to address why his Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) scores representing “serious symptoms of social and occupational impairment” did not support a 100 percent evaluation.

The Court, in examining the regulatory history of § 3.156(c) and other related effective date regulations, held that under the current and pre-amended versions of the regulation, a claimant whose claim is reconsidered based on newly discovered service department records may be entitled to an effective date as early as the date of the original claim.

The Court declined to address whether § 3.156(c)(2) may be applied retroactively; however, it determined that the regulation was inapplicable in this case and the Board erred in applying it.  The Court acknowledged that the veteran did not comply with repeated requests to submit additional information to verify his claimed stressors, such as the death of a friend and witnessing civilian casualties; however, the Court observed that the RO ultimately granted service connection for PTSD based upon attacks on the base camp where his unit was stationed in Vietnam, which was verified by CURR, rather than on any of aforementioned stressors.

The Court held that the information that CURR used to verify the veteran’s stressor – i.e., unit number and dates of duty in Vietnam – was always a part of the claims file.  Notwithstanding the fact that the veteran failed to fully cooperate with the RO’s requests for information to verify other claimed stressors, VA’s failure to verify his stressor until March 2005 was the result of an administrative error in locating his unit records, rather than the veteran’s subsequent failure to provide additional information.

The Court held that the Board mischaracterized the veteran’s request for a TDIU evaluation as a claim for an increased evaluation, rather than as part of his initial application for benefits for PTSD, and erred in not applying 3.156(b).  *See* [*Rice v. Shinseki*, 22 Vet.App. 447 (2009)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000015191/) (per curiam).  The Board limited its examination of the evidence of record pertaining to the request for a TDIU evaluation to the one-year period prior to the September 2005 date that the issue of entitlement to a TDIU evaluation was raised.  However, since the veteran’s request for a TDIU evaluation was part of his initial application for benefits for PTSD, the Board was required to consider evidence of unemployability as far back as the date of the underlying initial claim, i.e., as far back as September 2000 when he requested that VA reopen his previously denied claim.

The Court concluded that the Board did not err by not applying [§ 4.3](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=97cd1294b0caf406821c77e2589acc40&node=se38.1.4_13&rgn=div8), because it determined that the evidence was not in equipoise; thus, specific consideration of § 4.3 was unwarranted.  The Court also held that the Board’s statement of reasons or bases were adequate in determining that the veteran was not entitled to an initial evaluation in excess of 70 percent for PTSD.  The Court found that the Board had considered the “serious symptoms of social and occupational impairment” that the veteran’s GAF scores represented in thoroughly discussing the veteran’s disability picture and explaining why his symptoms more nearly approximated a 70 percent, rather than 100 percent, disability rating.

The Court vacated the portion of the Board’s decision that denied entitlement to earlier effective dates for PTSD and a TDIU evaluation and remanded for readjudication consistent with its opinion.  The remainder of the Board’s decision was affirmed.
