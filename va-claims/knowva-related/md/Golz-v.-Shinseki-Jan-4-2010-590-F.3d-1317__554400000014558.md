Decision Assessment Document
 Golz v. Shinseki, Jan 4, 2010, 590 F.3d 1317
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court properly interpreted [38 U.S.C. § 5103A](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) and [38 C.F.R. § 3.159(c)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=8c491e7a585ffa26b0265c43c92cc143&node=se38.1.3_1159&rgn=div8) in holding that VA is not required to obtain disability records from the Social Security Administration (SSA) if VA determines, without review of the actual records, that there is no reasonable possibility that such records are relevant to the veteran’s claim for VA disability compensation.

The Federal Circuit held that relevant records for the purpose of section 5103A are those records that relate to the injury for which the claimant is seeking benefits and have a reasonable possibility of helping to substantiate the veteran’s claim.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact concerning regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

SSA, in 1995, determined the veteran, who served from 1969 to 1972, to be disabled due to low back and leg pain as the result of a 1991 motor vehicle accident.  The SSA decision did not mention the veteran’s mental health or indicate that records or testimony reviewed by SSA discussed any mental health issues.  In 2001, the veteran filed a claim for service connection for post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and indicated that he was in receipt of SSA disability benefits on the application.  The regional office denied the claim on several occasions, finding no evidence of a PTSD diagnosis.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) reopened the claim in 2006, but denied service connection.  The Board reviewed the 1995 SSA decision and, citing [38 U.S.C. § 5103A(c)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) and [38 C.F.R. § 3.159(c)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=8c491e7a585ffa26b0265c43c92cc143&node=se38.1.3_1159&rgn=div8), found that SSA records, which are not in the file, would not be relevant to the appeal as the decision did not mention a psychiatric disorder.  The Veterans Court affirmed the Board’s decision, concluding that the Board did not clearly err in finding that the SSA records relating to its decision were not relevant to the claim.

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that SSA records are always potentially relevant to a disability claim under section 5103A and § 3.159(c) and must be obtained in order to satisfy VA’s duty to assist.  He contended that medical records in general, and SSA disability records in particular, are always potentially relevant because such records may contain medical evidence relevant to the veteran’s claim and it is impossible to determine the content or relevance of medical records without examining them.

In examining section 5103A, the Federal Circuit indicated that while it is clear that Congress intended VA to assist veterans in obtaining records for compensation claims, it is equally clear that Congress only obligated VA to obtain “relevant records.”  The Federal Circuit described the statute as explicitly providing that not all medical records or all SSA disability records must be sought – only those that are relevant to the veteran’s claim.

The Federal Circuit observed that the SSA decision identified testimony, documents, and medical records relating only to the veteran’s back pain and there was no specific allegation from the veteran that any evidence in conjunction with the SSA decision ever delved into mental health.  The Federal Circuit concluded that the Veterans Court had reviewed the Board’s relevance determination under the correct legal standard – whether it related to the veteran’s mental health or there was a reasonable possibility it could help substantiate the veteran’s claim.

The Federal Circuit stated that it is not always required that a record’s relevance be determined without reviewing the record itself.  VA is only required to examine the “information” it has related to medical records and if there exists a reasonable possibility that the records could help the veteran substantiate his claim for benefits, the duty to assist requires VA to obtain the records.  If the SSA decision pertains to a completely unrelated medical condition and the veteran makes no specific allegations that would give rise to a reasonable belief that the medical records may nonetheless pertain to the injury for which the veteran seeks benefits, relevance is not established.

The Federal Circuit advised that in close or uncertain cases, VA should be guided by the principles underlying its uniquely pro-claimant system.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court decision.
