Decision Assessment Document
 King v. Shinseki, Dec 5, 2012, 700 F.3d 1339
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court correctly found that the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) had not erred as a matter of law in determining that lay evidence was insufficient in establishing medical causation between the veteran’s back and hip disabilities and service or the veteran’s service-connected knee disabilities.

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court did not ignore the precedent of [*Jandreau v. Nicholson*, 492 F.3d 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2007)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014836/) or categorically dismiss the lay evidence as incompetent.  The Veterans Court gave due consideration to the lay testimony, rather than finding the evidence incompetent merely because it was provided by laypersons.

Note: The Federal Circuit held in *Jandreau* that lay evidence can be competent and sufficient to establish a diagnosis of a condition when (1) a layperson is competent to identify the medical condition, (2) the layperson is reporting a contemporaneous medical diagnosis, or (3) lay testimony describing symptoms at the time supports a later diagnosis by a medical professional. Also in [*Buchanan v. Nicholson*, 451 F.3d 1331, (Fed. Cir. 2006)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014893/), the Federal Circuit held that competent lay evidence can be sufficient in and of itself and does not lack credibility merely if unaccompanied by contemporaneous medical evidence.  The Federal Circuit, in [*Davidson v. Shinseki*, 581 F.3d 1313 (Fed. Cir. 2009)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000015537/), reversed the Veterans Court determination that a medical opinion is always required under [38 U.S.C. § 1154(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1154) to prove a nexus.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served from February 1973 to June 1974, sought service connection for disabilities of the hips and back, contending that they were related to service or resulted from the falls and movement adjustments due to his service-connected knee disabilities.  His service records showed no treatment for hip or back conditions.  He testified before the Board in 1998 that his knee pain affected his back and hips due to altering his gait in attempting to relieve the knee pain.  His spouse submitted a letter to the Board documenting her observations of the veteran’s physical deterioration due to the knee, hip, and back problems, as well as opining that his back and hip disabilities were caused by the knee conditions.

A VA examination from 2000 diagnosed minimal degenerative joint disease of the hips and lumbosacral spine and associated the conditions with age, rather than service or the service-connected knee disabilities.  The examiner also noted that the veteran had symptoms related to a chronic pain syndrome with possible psychosomatic overlays.  The veteran was examined by the same VA medical examiner in 2003; however, no opinion regarding etiology of the back and hip conditions were given.

A private physician, in a 2000 letter, opined that the veteran’s hip and back disabilities were caused by his knee injuries.  The physician referenced studies concerning the significant effect of weight bearing on the lumbar spine and stated that the veteran’s hips were subjected to abnormal forces that resulted from “off-loading” to ameliorate his knee symptoms.

The VA Chief of Orthopedics, in 2006, reviewed the examinations he conducted in 2000 and 2003 and opined that the veteran’s bilateral hip and back disabilities were unrelated to his service-connected knee conditions.

The Board denied the appeal in 2007; however, following a joint motion for remand before the Veterans Court, additional evidence was entered into the record to include a medical opinion from a VA orthopedist in 2008 that it was unlikely that the veteran’s back or hip disabilities were either directly caused or permanently worsened by the service-connected knee disabilities.  In 2009, the veteran submitted medical treatise evidence that limping could cause back pain or aggravate pre-existing back pain.

The Board, in denying the appeal in 2009, articulated reasons for assigning greater probative value to the VA examinations, rather than the private physician’s letter and medical treatise evidence submitted by the veteran.  The Board also considered the lay testimony, but concluded that the statements of the veteran and his wife were not competent because medical training and expertise were required to render an opinion on the question of medical causation in this case.

The Veterans Court affirmed the Board’s denial of the veteran’s appeal.  While acknowledging that lay evidence may be used to establish medical causation, the Veterans Court concluded that the Board properly considered the veteran’s lay evidence but found that it was outweighed by the competent evidence of record.

The Federal Circuit observed that this case was different from previous decisions it reversed for failure to consider lay evidence on the question of medical causation because neither the Board or the Veterans Court deemed the lay evidence to be categorically incompetent.  The Federal Circuit found that the Veterans Court had correctly evaluated the Board’s credibility assessment, weighing of evidence, and treatment of the lay evidence and did not find clear error.  The Veterans Court also addressed the Board’s reliance on the VA examiner’s opinion over the other medical evidence and determined that the Board did not err in favoring the opinion of one competent medical expert over that of another and found that the Board’s reliance was justified.

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court, in reaching its conclusion, had given due consideration to the lay testimony, and did not deem it incompetent merely because it was provided by laypersons.

The Federal Circuit dismissed the appeal because the veteran’s arguments only challenged the evaluation and weighing of evidence, and thus involved the application of law to fact, which the court lacked jurisdiction to review.  See [38 U.S.C. § 7292(d)(2)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/7292).
