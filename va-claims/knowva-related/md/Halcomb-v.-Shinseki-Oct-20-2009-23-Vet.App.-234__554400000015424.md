Decision Assessment Document
 Halcomb v. Shinseki, Oct 20, 2009, 23 Vet.App. 234
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the regulatory scheme confirms that VA’s standard, generic informed consent form is the best overall approach to accomplishing documentation of the consent process and does not constitute affirmative evidence of negligence per se.  The Court stated that given that VA’s regulations and *VHA* handbook do not require a detailed recitation of all of the information conveyed in securing informed consent, it cannot be presumed that the veteran’s complications were not discussed simply because they were not recorded.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, in April 2002, underwent surgery for removal of a left-eye cataract and lens replacement at a VA medical center (VAMC) and developed endophthalmitis (inflammation of the ocular cavities and adjacent structures).  He eventually developed blindness of the left eye.

Prior to the VAMC surgery, the veteran signed two documents relating to his permission for the procedures.  The first was a form stating that the nature and purpose of the operation or procedure, possible alternative methods of treatment, risks involved, and possibility of complications had been fully explained to him.  The second document was a statement typewritten on a VA progress note that read, in relevant part, as follows:

           “The relevant aspects of the above stated procedure/treatment, the

            indications, risks, benefits, and alternatives have been discussed

            with the patient in understandable language.  The patient was

            given opportunity to ask question concerning the procedure/

            treatment.  Comprehension of the discussion was indicated, and

            the patient freely consented to the procedure/treatment without

            duress or coercion.”

Both documents were co-signed by a physician and witnessed by a nurse.  Neither document contained any itemization, notations, or further description of what risks or alternatives may have been discussed.

The veteran filed a claim for disability compensation under [38 U.S.C. § 1151](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1151), which the regional office (RO) denied.  Following a Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) remand, a VA examiner rendered an opinion that there was no carelessness, negligence, lack of proper skill, error in judgment, or similar fault of VA in furnishing care to the veteran.  The Board, in December 2006, found that the complications were reasonably foreseeable consequences of the veteran’s medical procedures and denied the appeal.  The Board determined that the medical evidence reflected informed consent on the veteran’s behalf under [38 C.F.R. § 17.32](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=833478668a42476c74549b1387b54e82&node=se38.1.17_132&rgn=div8) and that there was no indication that the veteran’s consent was not an informed one.

The veteran argued to the Court that the generic written consent form failed to comply with the VA regulations on informed consent and establishes negligence per se.  He contended that based upon the Board’s findings that endophthalmitis and retinal tears were reasonably foreseeable events meant that under 38 C.F.R. § 17.32(c), VA had a duty to specifically inform him of the possible outcomes and the failure of VA health care providers to inform him of these known risks constituted negligent care under section 1151 and [38 C.F.R§ 3.361(d)(1)(ii)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=a2ba4fce8d9f6af9cabb35e8987c88cd&node=se38.1.3_1361&rgn=div8), thus entitling him to VA benefits.

The Court noted that generally a claimant seeking VA benefits has the burden of presenting evidence in support of the claim, with the statutorily mandated assistance of VA.  The Court concluded that the veteran introduced no evidence that the informed consent discussion was defective in any way.  The Court rejected the veteran’s argument that the fact that the consent form did not refer to specific risks raised the presumption that these reasonably foreseeable complications were not discussed, thus contravening the regulatory requirements for informed consent.

The Court noted that Congress instructed the Secretary to prescribe regulations establishing procedures to ensure that all patient care furnished under title 38 shall be carried out only with the full and informed consent of the patient.  The Court also acknowledged the Secretary’s implementation of a regulation governing the informed consent process and observed that VA’s interpretation of its own regulation is entitled to deference where it “reflect[s] the agency’s fair and considered judgment on the matter in question.”  The Court indicated that the *VHA Handbook* addressing the consent requirements demonstrates that VA has long regarded the use and completion of consent forms such as VA Form OF-522 as appropriate documentation of the consent process.  Given that VA regulations and the VHA handbook do not require a detailed recitation of all information conveyed in securing a patient’s informed consent, it may not be presumed that certain complications were not discussed simply because those complications were note recorded.

The Court, in view of its holding in this case, declined to resolve the question of what evidentiary value a generic consent form might have in a case where the claimant introduced other evidence that VA did not obtain informed consent.

The Court affirmed the Board’s December 2006 decision.
