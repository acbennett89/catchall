Decision Assessment Document
 Colantonio v. Shinseki, Jun 1, 2010, 606 F.3d 1378
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit, in reiterating its recent holding in [*Waters v. Shinseki*, 601 F.3d 1274 (Fed. Cir. 2010)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014562/), held that medically competent evidence is not required in every case to “indicate” that a claimant’s disability “may be associated” with the claimant’s service under [38 U.S.C. § 5103A(d)(2)(B)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A).

The Federal Circuit explained that it was not holding that it would always be possible to establish a nexus through lay evidence, as there may be instances, such as the *Waters* case itself, in which the lay evidence falls short of the standard under section 5103A(d)(2)(B).

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change. However, the Federal Circuit’s holding should be carefully followed in determining whether or not to schedule a veteran for a VA examination under the authority of section [5103A(d)(2)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A).

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served in the Army from 1942 to 1945, filed a claim for service connection for a back injury more than 50 years after separation from service. The regional office and the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) denied the claim. In support of the claim, the veteran had provided statements and sworn testimony concerning in-service and post-service back injuries. The Board, in denying the appeal, found that the only evidence of an in-service back injury was the veteran’s own statements and he lacked competence to give a medical opinion on diagnosis or etiology of a condition.

Although the Veterans Court agreed with the veteran that the Board failed to provide an adequate statement of its reasons for not ordering a medical examination pursuant to section 5103A(d)(2) and [38 C.F.R. § 3.159(c)(4)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=baf1c6cc1b3b15f93f07b583f302ad47&node=se38.1.3_1159&rgn=div8), it found the error to be harmless. The Veterans Court explained that even assuming that the veteran’s lay testimony was sufficient to establish the occurrence of an incident or incidents during service, it could not provide the requisite medical nexus between service and the veteran’s current back disability. As a layperson, the veteran was not competent to opine on matters requiring medical knowledge, such as etiology of a condition or nexus.

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that the Veterans Court erred in its interpretation of section 5103A(d)(2). He contended that Congress, in enacting the statute, only required that the evidence “indicate” that the veteran’s symptoms or injury “may be associated with service,” a low standard that does not require competent medical evidence. Thus, the veteran maintained that his lay testimony regarding service connection for his back injury should have been sufficient to establish the nexus required by section 5103A(d)(2)(B).

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court’s statements could be interpreted as meaning that a veteran’s lay testimony can never be sufficient in itself to satisfy the nexus requirement in section [5103A(d)(2)(B)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A), which would conflict with its recent decision in *Waters*.

The Federal Circuit reiterated its *Waters* holding and indicated that because the Veterans Court did not have the benefit of the *Waters* decision and may have overstated the extent to which competent medical evidence is required to make the minimal showing of nexus required by subparagraph B, it was vacating the Veterans Court decision and remanding the case to the Veterans Court to reconsider its harmless error analysis in light of the proper interpretation of section 5103A(d)(2).
