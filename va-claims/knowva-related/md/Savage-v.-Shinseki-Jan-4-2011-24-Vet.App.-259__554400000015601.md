Decision Assessment Document
 Savage v. Shinseki, Jan 4, 2011, 24 Vet.App. 259
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that VA, pursuant to [38 U.S.C. § 5103A(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A), has a duty to return for clarification unclear or insufficient private examination reports or VA progress notes if it reasonably appears that a request for clarification could provide relevant information necessary to properly decide a claim that is otherwise not in the record and cannot be obtained in some other way.  VA must obtain clarification from the private examiner or VA physician or clearly and adequately explain why such clarification is unreasonable.

The Court, acknowledging that VA cannot compel non-VA medical personnel to comply with its request or force compliance within a reasonable time frame, which could delay a claim’s adjudication, expressed that its holding was limited to those instances in which the missing information is relevant, factual, and objective – not a matter of opinion – and bears greatly on the probative value of the private medical examination report.

The Court, finding that a request for further information from a private examiner would not be burdensome or unreasonable, held that if a private examination report reasonably appears to contain information necessary to properly decide a claim but is “unclear” or “not suitable for rating purposes,” and the information contained in the report otherwise cannot be obtained, VA has a duty to either (1) ask the private examiner to clarify the report, (2) request that the claimant obtain the necessary information to clarify the report, or (3) explain why clarification  is not needed.  Any request for clarification to a private examiner or claimant should clearly indicate what further action needs to be taken to make the insufficient private examination report acceptable for VA consideration.  If no response is received from the private examiner or claimant, VA may continue adjudicating the claim.

**Impact on VBA:**

We will amend [38 C.F.R. § 4.2](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=d92531834bdf12f9f1b4f32faa2729a2&node=se38.1.4_12&rgn=div8) to comply with the Court’s opinion.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, in November 2002, filed a claim for increased evaluation for his service-connected bilateral hearing loss.  Over the course of adjudicating the claim, the veteran had submitted multiple private audiology evaluations, dated November 2001, July 2002, July 2003, December 2004, June 2006, and May 2008.  All private audiological evaluations documented varying degrees of bilateral hearing loss.  VA audiology examinations were conducted in January 2003, September 2007, and March 2009.  VA progress notes from August and September 2009, which included audiology testing, were also considered.  The regional office (RO), in February 2003, assigned a 10 percent evaluation effective November 14, 2002, based on the January 2003 VA examination and, in March 2009, granted a 20 percent evaluation, effective March 18, 2009, also the date of a VA examination.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in October 2009, denied the veteran’s appeal for higher evaluations for hearing loss.  The Board acknowledged that private records included audiograms documenting the severity of hearing loss, but found that these examinations were inadequate for rating purposes as it was unclear whether speech discrimination testing was conducted using the Maryland CNC test.  The Board decided to focus on the VA examinations, determining that they contained the necessary information for evaluating the veteran’s hearing loss.  The Board also rejected the August 2009 VA progress note, as it was also unclear whether word recognition testing was conducted pursuant to the Maryland CNC test.

The veteran argued before the Court that VA had a duty to return the private audiological examinations and VA treatment records that it stated were “unclear” to clarify the type of audiological testing performed before rejecting them as inadequate (as the reports reasonably appear to contain information not otherwise in the record and that may potentially help substantiate his claim).  He also argued that VA’s duty to assist, in conjunction with [38 C.F.R. § 4.2](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=d92531834bdf12f9f1b4f32faa2729a2&node=se38.1.4_12&rgn=div8) and [19.9](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=d92531834bdf12f9f1b4f32faa2729a2&node=se38.2.19_19&rgn=div8), required such action by VA.

In addressing the applicability of §§ 4.2 and 19.9, the Court concluded that the “reasonable efforts” VA is required to make under § 19.9 in assisting a claimant in developing his claim included clarification of unclear “evidence,” which necessarily includes medical examination reports of all kinds.  If VA would opt not to seek such clarification, but rather to develop new evidence, it must thoroughly explain such decision.

The Court determined that the plain language of [§ 4.2](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=d92531834bdf12f9f1b4f32faa2729a2&node=se38.1.4_12&rgn=div8) in no way limits the Secretary’s duty to return insufficient examination reports to only returning examination reports provided by VA physicians.  The Court concluded that nothing in § 4.2, or any other VA regulation, prevents VA from sending a letter to a private examiner or to the claimant explaining that the examination report is unclear or insufficient and why and requesting further information or clarification.  In the absence of a response, VA would simply continue adjudicating the claim.

The Court held that when a private examination report reasonably appears to contain information necessary to properly decide a claim but its “unclear” or “not suitable for rating purposes,” and the information reasonably contained in the report otherwise cannot be obtained, VA has a duty under [section 5103A(a](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A)) to either (1) ask the private examiner to clarify the report, (2) request that the claimant obtain the necessary information to clarify the report, or (3) explain why such clarification is not needed.

The Court expressed that it did not expect that clarification of a private examination report would be necessary in most instances, but its holding was limited to those instances in which the missing information is relevant, factual, and objective – not a matter of opinion – and bears greatly on the probative value of the examination report.  After considering these factors, if VA would determine that seeking clarification would be unreasonable or that the missing information is located elsewhere in the record or may be more easily obtained by some other means, without compromising the favorable character of the private examiner’s opinion, VA must clearly and adequately explain such determination.

In applying its holding to the facts of the case, the Court found the Board’s decision to adjudicate the veteran’s claim without seeking clarification of favorable private medical examination reports and instead relying solely on VA audiological evaluation to be troublesome for many reasons.  First, noting the prolonged adjudication period and the possibility of staged ratings, the Court stated that it was difficult to comprehend that the Board could adequately evaluate the severity of the veteran's hearing disability between November 2002 and March 2009 based solely on the January 2003 and September 2007 VA examinations.  Given the impossibility of developing new evidence that could address the severity of the veteran’s disability during the time, it was especially important for the Board to ensure that the evidence that did exist relevant to that period – VA or private –was developed as thoroughly as possible.

Second, the private examination reports rejected by the Board were detailed reports clearly based on objective testing, rather than general opinions lacking unbiased foundation.  Had the missing information been obtained, the Board would have been able to properly determine the probative value of the private reports.  Third, the single fact missing from the private examinations – a statement of whether the Maryland CNC Word Recognition Test was used – is an objective fact not subject to interpretation or opinion that can easily be obtained by contacting the private examiners with minimal VA burden.  Thus, the Court concluded that the Board erred in not seeking clarification of the private audiological examinations submitted by the veteran or to explain why clarification was not needed.

The Court determined that the error was prejudicial, rejecting the Secretary’s argument that had the Board even assumed – or sought and received clarification – that the Maryland CNC Word Recognition Test was used, the word recognition scores provided in the private examination reports would not change the outcome of the Board’s decision.  The Court stated that to the extent that no puretone threshold averages were given in the private examination reports, the Board should either compute them using the puretone threshold information given in the examination reports, if the Board has such expertise, or seek clarification of that information.

The Court vacated the Board’s October 2009 decision and remanded the matter for the Board to either seek clarification of the private audiological examination reports or explain why such clarification is not needed.
