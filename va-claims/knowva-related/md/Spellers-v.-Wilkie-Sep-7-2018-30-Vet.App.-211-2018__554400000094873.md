Decision Assessment Document
Spellers v. Wilkie, Sep 7, 2018, 30 Vet.App. 211 (2018)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that because of the nature of the criteria in 38 C.F.R. 4.120, and 38 C.F.R. 4.124a, Diagnostic Code (DC) 8520, the schedular rating criteria contemplate the symptoms necessitating the use of assistive devices, as well as the severity of those symptoms, which does not warrant referral for extraschedular consideration under 38 C.F.R. 3.321(b)(1).

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran is service connected for a low back strain, rated 20 percent disabling, and bilateral lower extremity sciatica with separate 10 percent evaluations.  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in November 2016, denied referral of the separate 10 percent evaluations for sciatica for extraschedular consideration.  The Board considered the veteran’s use of a cane and walker and concluded that, although assistive devices are not specifically listed in the rating criteria for evaluating neurologic disabilities, assistive devices are provided to alleviate the presence of symptoms and/or functional limitations.  The Board determined that the veteran’s symptoms that required use of assistive devices are fully contemplated by the rating criteria and associated regulations and the use of such assistive device directly addresses a veteran’s functional limitations.  Therefore, the Board concluded that use of such devices does not create an exceptional disability picture that renders the rating criteria as inadequate.

The Board also considered the veteran’s use of pain medication and associated side effects with its extraschedular analysis, but concluded that that the veteran’s reports were not credible.  The Board based its credibility determination in large measure on the veteran’s failure to report certain side effects during treatment at various times, as purportedly required under a signed patient agreement.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board failed to explain how DC 8520 contemplates the use of assistive devices, such as a cane or walker, for his bilateral sciatica such that extraschedular referral is not warranted.  He alleged that the Board misinterpreted 3.321(b)(1) in finding that assistive devices address functional limitations that are contemplated by the rating criteria.  He also argued that, even if the use of assistive devices was not contemplated as a symptom, the Board was still required to discuss use of assistive devices in the extraschedular analysis, because use of such devices indicates the severity of his condition.

The Court, holding that 4.120 contemplates any impairment of motor or sensory function that would require the use of an assistive device such as a cane or walker, concluded that the Board’s findings fall squarely within 4.120’s language about impairment of motor and sensory function.  The Court indicated that it would be difficult to fathom any use of an assistive device such as a cane or walker that would not be due to symptoms related to impairment of motor or sensory function.

The Court summed up that in considering the first Thun element in the context of evaluating a neurological condition, an assessment must be made as to whether the symptoms are contemplated by the particular DC under which the disability is rated, but also whether they are contemplated in the language of the preface to the neurologic and convulsive disorders diagnostic codes in 4.120.  The Court concluded that the symptoms of sciatica that require use of assistive devices such as a cane or walker are contemplated by the schedular rating criteria and do not satisfy the first part of Thun’s first element.

The Court found that the Board did not address the severity of the veteran’s sciatica in the extraschedular context; however, the error was harmless, as it concluded that, as a matter of law, the severity of the veteran’s sciatica for which he uses a cane or walker is contemplated by the very nature of DC 8520, at least at the level the veteran is rated.

The Court concluded that both the symptoms and severity of the veteran’s sciatica that require him to use a cane or walker are contemplated by the schedular rating criteria, specifically in 4.120 and DC 8520.  The Court held that the Board did not err in its finding that the first element of Thun was not met on the grounds that the veteran’s condition necessitates use of assistive devices.

The Court did agree with the veteran that the Board failed to provide an adequate statement of reasons or bases for its adverse credibility determination.  Specifically, the Court found the Board to have erred in relying on the fact that the veteran had not reported side effects at various treatment appointments; however, those records did not mention side effects.  Thus, it was unclear if the veteran was asked about side effects or that he did not actively deny side effects.

The Board also relied on a July 2009 patient agreement that the veteran signed that obligated him to report “significant side effects.”  However, the Court found that the agreement did not define what constituted “significant side effects,” but listed “potential side effects,” such as nausea, dizziness, upset stomach, and hallucinations.  The list of potential side effects did not include drowsiness or difficulty concentrating, which were the veteran’s reported symptoms.  The Court concluded that it was unclear if the veteran understood that he needed to report symptoms not included in the list of potential side effects or why the Board appeared to believe he had such an understanding of the obligation.

The Court held that judicial review was frustrated and remand was warranted for the Board to provide adequate reasons or bases in assessing the veteran’ credibility regarding his medication side effects and whether those side effects require extraschedular referral.  The Court further held that it was unable to conclude that the Board’s reasons-or-bases error was harmless because the Court could not determine whether extraschedular referral may have been warranted if the veteran’s statements regarding his side effects were properly considered.
