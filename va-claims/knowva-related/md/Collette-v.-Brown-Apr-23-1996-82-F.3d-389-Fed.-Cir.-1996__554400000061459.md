Decision Assessment Document
 Collette v. Brown, Apr 23, 1996, 82 F.3d 389 (Fed. Cir. 1996)
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that 38 U.S.C. 1154(b) does not create a statutory presumption that a combat veteran's alleged disease or injury is service-connected.  A veteran must meet his or her evidentiary burden with respect to service connection.  What section 1154(b) does do is lighten that evidentiary burden with respect to disabilities alleged to be the result of combat service.

The Federal Circuit, interpreting section 1154(b),  held that there is a three-step sequential that, if met, provides a rebuttable presumption of service connection.  Under the first step, “it must be determined whether the veteran has proffered ‘satisfactory lay or other evidence of service incurrence or aggravation of such injury or disease.”  Under the second step, “it must be determined whether the proffered evidence is ‘consistent with the circumstances, conditions, or hardships of such service.”  Finally, under the third step, “it must be determined whether the government has met its burden or rebutting the presumption of service connection by ‘clear and convincing evidence to the contrary.”

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran appealed a decision of the United States Court of Veterans Appeals affirming a decision of the Board of Veterans Appeals (BVA) which denied entitlement to service connection for a hearing loss.  The veteran served in combat in Korea and stated that he was exposed to noise from artillery fire and that incoming artillery rounds had landed close to him, knocking him down from the concussion on several occasions.  Service medical records, including separation examination, showed normal hearing.  A bilateral high frequency hearing loss was first diagnosed in 1979.  His initial claim was denied in 1988, and, after several denials, he appealed to the BVA.  During a hearing, a cousin testified that the veteran's hearing was fine before he went to Korea, but it was necessary to yell at him after he returned.  The veteran also submitted lay statements indicating he suffered a noticeable hearing loss as a result of his service in Korea.  The veteran argued that under the provisions of section 1154(b), the lay statements he submitted were "satisfactory" evidence and should be accepted as sufficient proof of service connection unless rebutted by clear and convincing evidence.  The BVA denied the appeal, finding that the preponderance of the evidence was against the claim.  The Court affirmed the BVA decision and denied reconsideration on the basis that the BVA had found the lay evidence was not credible in view of the separation examination showing normal hearing, and, therefore, was an unsatisfactory predicate for application of section 1154(b).   On appeal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, the veteran argued that the BVA misinterpreted section 1154(b) and that the evaluation of his claim under a preponderance of the evidence standard was contrary to section 1154(b).
