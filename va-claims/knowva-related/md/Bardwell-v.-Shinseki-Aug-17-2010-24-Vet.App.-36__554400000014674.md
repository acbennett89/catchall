Decision Assessment Document
 Bardwell v. Shinseki, Aug 17, 2010, 24 Vet.App. 36
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) did not err in finding that the veteran’s eyes did not undergo an event, injury, or disease in service; therefore, it was not obligated to consider whether the veteran was entitled to a medical examination.

The Court concluded that the Board had properly weighed the veteran’s lay evidence prior to rejecting it and rejected the veteran’s assertion that the Board impermissibly rejected his lay evidence contrary to [*Buchanan v.* *Nicholson*, 451 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2006)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014893/). Note: The Federal Circuit held in Buchanan that the Board can consider and weigh the lack of contemporaneous medical evidence against a veteran’s lay evidence; however, the lack of such records does not, in and of itself, render the lay evidence not credible.

The Court also found that the Board’s statement that it found “incredible that [the veteran] would undergo undocumented chemical or gas testing or other exposure during his less than two[-]month tour of duty” adequately demonstrated that it had considered the veteran’s service and accounted for the places, types, and circumstances of the veteran’s service pursuant to [38 U.S.C. § 1154(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1154).

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served in the Navy from November 1944 to January 1945, submitted a claim in March 2001 for compensation for in-service injuries to his eyes.  He testified during a regional office (RO) hearing that he was exposed to a chemical gas in a training exercise and experienced burning in his eyes in service.  He testified that his eyes deteriorated after the incident and he was no longer able to work by 1978 due to vision problems.  A search by the National Personnel Records Center (NPRC) failed to find any records related to the veteran’s reported exposure to gas or chemicals during service.

In March 2005, the veteran provided a statement from a private ophthalmologist that the veteran was legally blind and displayed nerve dysfunction consistent with toxic retinopathy.  The physician opined that the blindness appeared to be associated with a gaseous chemical exposure.

The Board, in June 2008, denied the veteran’s claim, concluding that there was no competent evidence of an in-service injury to the veteran’s eyes and the preponderance of the evidence was against a finding that his current eye disability was related to military service.  The Board acknowledged the veteran’s current blindness, but found that the veteran’s assertions lacked credibility in view of the lack of documents of an incident in his service records and the NPRC’s inability to verify exposure.  The Board stated that it found “incredible that the veteran would undergo undocumented chemical or gas testing or other exposure during his less than two-month tour of duty and his statements to the contrary are not convincing.”

The veteran argued before the Court that VA failed to fulfill its duty to assist in developing his claim by obtaining a medical examination pursuant to [38 C.F.R. § 3.159(c)(4)(i)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=2f27694d49775ac3d1981f38f4042760&node=se38.1.3_1159&rgn=div8) because he satisfied the regulation’s three criteria for examination.  He asserted that he had a current eye disability; his lay statements about exposure to gases during training are competent and credible evidence that there was an event or injury in service relating to his eyes, and the medical opinion was sufficient to indicate that his current disability may be linked to service.

The Court, distinguishing the veteran’s situation from *Buchanan*, found that the Board had properly weighed and rejected his lay evidence that he suffered an event or injury that harmed his eyes during service.  The Board had also considered the [section 1154(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1154) requirement to account for the “places, types, and circumstances” of service in finding that the veteran’s lay evidence lacked credibility.

The Court affirmed the Board’s June 2008 decision.
