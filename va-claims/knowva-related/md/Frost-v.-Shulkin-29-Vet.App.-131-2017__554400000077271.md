Decision Assessment Document
Frost v. Shulkin, 29 Vet.App. 131 (2017)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that a veteran is not precluded, as a matter of law, from obtaining secondary service connection on a causation basis when the secondary condition was incurred prior to the grant of service connection for, or diagnosis of, the primary condition.  As there is no such requirement in 38 C.F.R. 3.310(a), for a veteran to be service connected on a secondary basis under a causation theory, the primary disability need not be service connected, or even diagnosed, at the time the secondary condition is incurred.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in Manual M21-1, IV.ii.2.B.5.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served from November 1972 to September 1980, was involved in a train accident during service, injuring his shoulder and leg.  Following service, in November 1982, the veteran was involved in an altercation with a store proprietor, which resulted in a gunshot wound (GSW) to his neck.

In February 1983, the veteran filed for non-service-connected (NSC) pension benefits for left extremity paralysis resulting from the 1982 GSW.  In August 1985, the RO, finding that the veteran had become totally disabled in November 1982, awarded NSC pension benefits for the GSW residuals.

In June 2001, the veteran filed for service connection for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), which he claimed resulted from the in-service train accident in February 1980.  A VA examination undertaken in June 2002 yielded a PTSD diagnosis.  The examiner indicated that the veteran’s symptoms had persisted for 20 years, which would place the symptoms starting in June 1982.

The regional office (RO), in July 2002, granted service connection for PTSD with a 30 percent evaluation effective June 2001.  In August 2005, the evaluation was increased to 70 percent from 2001.

The veteran, in December 2005, filed for service connection for GSW residuals as secondary to service-connected PTSD.  The RO, in January 2009, granted a total disability evaluation due to individual unemployability, but denied service connection for the neck GSW with retained missile, finding that the evidence did not show that the condition was related to service-connected PTSD but was the result of a dispute with a store owner.  The veteran appealed the denial, arguing that because the June 2002 VA examiner opined that PTSD symptoms had persisted for 20 years, they were present from at least June 1982, and that PTSD therefore caused him to become involved in the November 1982 dispute that resulted in the GSW.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in April 2015, denied service connection for GSW residuals as secondary to service-connected PTSD.  The Board found that contemporaneous records regarding the November 1982 incident did not suggest or confirm the veteran’s assertions that his behavior resulted from PTSD.  The Board further found that the veteran was first shown to be suffering from PTSD no earlier than June 2002, 20 years after the November 1982 incident.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erroneously failed to consider favorable evidence when it denied service connection for GSW residuals secondary to service-connected PTSD and in failing to obtain a medical examination as to whether GSW residuals are related to the service-connected PTSD.  The Secretary argued that because the Board established June 2001 as the proper effective date for service connection for PTSD, which is almost 20 years after the November 1982 incident that led to the development of GSW residuals, those residuals cannot be due to any service-connected psychiatric symptoms, as there was no service-connected psychiatric disability in November 1982.

The Court indicated that nothing in the text of 3.310(a) specifies or indicates that the primary condition must be service connected, or even diagnosed at the time the secondary condition is incurred.  In view of the absence of such requirement, the Court concluded that the plain meaning of § 3.310(a) is clear and rejected the Secretary’s interpretation that would have barred the veteran’s claim as a matter of law.  The Court stated that it is only required that at the time of any decision establishing entitlement to secondary service connection, there must be a primary service-connected condition.

The Court stated that even if the language was ambiguous on this point, the Court would have reservations concerning the Secretary’s interpretation given the current protracted nature of veterans benefits claims adjudication, as the time from date of filing of a claim for service connection to the date of resolution often exceeds several years and may even exceed a decade.  The Court articulated that since a veteran may file for service connection for a primary condition and not receive a final determination of entitlement until much later renders it unable to countenance an interpretation of 3.310(a) that would require the primary disability to achieve service-connected status before the secondary disability is diagnosed.  Furthermore, the Court observed that a veteran has no obligation to file a compensation claim as soon as he or she becomes aware of disability.

The Court held that 3.310(a) is clear that for a veteran to receive secondary service connection on a causation basis, the primary disability need not be service connected, or even diagnosed, at the time the secondary condition is incurred.

The Court, finding that the Board failed to provide reasons or bases sufficient for the veteran to understand why he was not entitled to a VA examination or opinion, agreed with the veteran that remand was appropriate for the Board to address, in the first instance, whether the veteran was entitled to a VA examination and opinion regarding whether currently diagnosed GSW residuals are likely as not the result of service-connected PTSD that was not diagnosed at the time of the 1982 incident.

In addition, the Court indicated that the Board, in determining that it could not reasonably associate GSW residuals with PTSD, failed to address the June 2002 VA examination, which noted that the veteran’s psychiatric symptoms had persisted for 20 years.  Also, a December 2013 VA examination discussed the veteran’s PTSD symptoms to include impaired impulse control and periods of violence.  The Court found that the Board failed to address this favorable evidence and needed to do so in its McLendon analysis on remand.  The Court further determined that the Board appeared to rely on its own opinion when it determined that that it was unable to reasonably associate GSW residuals with PTSD, as it provided its own medical determination as to when the veteran began suffering from PTSD.  The Board relied on the date of the PTSD diagnosis in 2002, rather than addressing the physician’s statement in June 2002 that the veteran’s symptoms had persisted since June 1982.   The Court reminded that the Board that, on remand, it was to refrain from relying on its own medical opinion.

The Court remanded for the Board to conduct a McLendon analysis and to determine the necessity of a VA examination or opinion regarding whether GSW residuals are as likely as not proximately due to or the result of PTSD, which, although not diagnosed or service connected at the time of the 1982 incident, is now service connected and therefore meets the basic temporal requirement contained in 3.310(a).
