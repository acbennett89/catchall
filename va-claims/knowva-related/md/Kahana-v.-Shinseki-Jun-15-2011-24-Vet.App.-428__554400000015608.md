Decision Assessment Document
 Kahana v. Shinseki, Jun 15, 2011, 24 Vet.App. 428
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) violated *Colvin v. Derwinski*, 1 Vet.App. 171 (1991), by making an inference from the evidentiary record that resulted in a medical determination, because the basis for that inference must be independent and must be cited.  The Board erred in finding the veteran’s lay statements to not be credible based on its determination that a particular type of injury alleged to have incurred in service was the type that should have been documented in the service medical records (SMRs) and was not.

The Court found the Board’s categorical rejection and failure to analyze and weigh the veteran’s lay statements regarding medical nexus on the basis of competency to be legally unsupportable in accordance with established precedent.  Note: The Court has held that “lay testimony is competent … to establish the presence of observable symptomatology and ‘may provide sufficient support for a claim for service connection.’”  *See* [*Barr v.* *Nicholson*, 21 Vet.App. 303, 307 (2007)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014771/).  The Federal Circuit has held that in certain circumstances a layperson may be competent to establish a diagnosis of a condition.  *See* [*Jandreau v. Nicholson*, 492 F.3d 1372, 1377 (Fed. Cir. 2007)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014836/).

Also, the Court found that the VA’s request for a medical opinion infringed upon the basic tenets of fair play in gathering evidence by telling the examiner that there was no right knee injury in service, although the SMRs contain an entry document treatment for a right knee condition.  The request violated the Board’s duty to procure evidence in an “impartial, unbiased, and neutral manner.”  *See Austin v. Brown*, 6 Vet.App. 547, 552 (1994).

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served in the Army from September 1976 to September 1979.  The separation report contains no report of a right knee injury.  The Board found no injury to the right knee in service; however, the Court observed a SMR record from May 1978, which reported “effusion sight right patella” and increased pain on palpation and on leg lift of the medial and lateral meniscus regions and a limited range of motion. The regional office (RO), in March 1980, granted service connection for left knee injuries.

In April 1993, a private orthopedist performed an anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction of the veteran’s right knee.  Other private treatment records show that he injured both knees working as a stuntman in 1995.  The veteran testified before the Board that he injured his knee in service, “snapped” it during a kickboxing competition, but was unsure why the injury did not appear in his SMRs.  The Board, in November 2007, remanded the case for a VA examination to determine the etiology of his right knee condition.

The veteran asserted during a December 2008 VA examination that his right knee snapped during a kickboxing competition in service, he was seen in service, and told that the knee was sprained.  The veteran stated that he continued to have problems after service and was eventually diagnosed with a right ACL tear that required surgical repair in the 1990s.  The examiner concluded that the veteran injured his right knee in service, but did not seek treatment until the 1990s, and that his right knee injury resulted from his habit of putting more weight on his right knee after numerous left knee injuries.  The examiner based her opinion upon review of private medical records and the history reported by the veteran, but did not review SMRs or VA records.

The RO requested that the claims file be returned to the examiner for an addendum opinion and attached instructions indicating that Social Security Administration records show the veteran sustained injuries in 1994 due to work as a stuntman and that there is no right knee injury in service.

The examiner, in a March 2009 report, reversed her previous opinion and espoused that the veteran’s right knee injury was not related to his military service or caused by or related to his left knee condition.  In her rationale, she stated that there is no documentation of a right knee injury in the veteran’s SMRs to support his claim.  The examiner concluded that the right knee ACL ligament sprain resulted from a specific trauma incident and not an overuse type of injury and that medical records reviewed did not support that a right knee injury occurred in service, but was a work-related injury.

The Board, in August 2009, denied service connection for a right knee disability to include as secondary to a service-connected left knee disability.  The Board, noting that no right knee injury was present in the SMRs, stated that given that a right ACL tear is quite a significant injury, there should have been documentation of it in the SMRs or in the separation examination report.  Thus, the Board found that the veteran’s assertion that he tore his right ACL during service was not credible and that, as a layperson, he was not competent to provide an opinion regarding medical nexus.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board made an improper medical finding based on the nature of his claimed injury when it found that, had the veteran sustained such an injury in service, it would have required treatment and thus should have appeared in his SMRs.  He contended that when VA requested a revised medical opinion, it improperly impinged on the examiner’s impartiality by telling the examiner that the veteran had sustained no right knee injury in service.  He also argued that the Board’s reasons or bases were inadequate because it rejected his lay testimony based on a lack of supporting medical records and that the revised medical opinion is inadequate because it was based on an inaccurate factual premise.

The Court held that the Board’s statement that, given the nature of the veteran’s injury, some documentation is expected, violated *Colvin*.  The Court found that the Board made a medical determination as to the relative severity, common symptomatology, and usual treatment of an ACL injury without citing to any independent medical evidence to corroborate its finding.  The Secretary argued that the Board was drawing a permissible inference based on the evidence; however, the Court stated that, under *Colvin*, when an inference results in a medical determination, the basis for that inference must be independent and it must be cited.  Thus, the Court concluded, the Board must reassess the weight and credibility of the veteran’s lay statements and adequately explain any negative credibility determination.

The Court also found the Board’s rejection of the veteran’s lay statements on the basis that, as a layperson, he was not competent to provide a medical nexus between his right knee injury and service and to his service-connected left knee disability to be legally unsupportable, thereby, rendering its statement of reason or bases to be inadequate.

Regarding the May 2009 examination report, the Secretary argued that this case is distinguishable from *Austin* in that VA asked for a new opinion for a number of reasons and not to solicit or suggest a particular result.  The Court rejected this argument because, despite the other reasons given to the examiner for a new opinion, the request was an equivocal statement of fact, which spoke to the crux of the examiner’s opinion, that there was “no right knee injury in service.”  Indeed, the Court stated it was unsure if the examiner revised her opinion based on additional evidence reviewed or because she felt coerced by VA’s proclamation of “no right knee injury in service” against her earlier conclusion that the veteran injured his knee in service.  The Court found that the opinion request violated the Board’s duty to procure evidence in an “impartial, unbiased, and neutral manner.”

The Court vacated and remanded the Board decision.  On remand, the Board was directed to procure an impartial medical opinion on which to base its decision.  The Court advised the Board that it was not precluded from asking the physician (1) whether there is any medical reason to accept or reject the proposition that had the veteran had a right knee injury in service, such injury could have led to his current condition; (2) what types of symptoms would have been caused by the type of ACL injury at issue; and (3) whether a right knee injury as described in the SMRs could have been mistaken for a sprain that was a precursor to the current condition.
