Decision Assessment Document
 Barr v. Nicholson, Jun 15, 2007, 21 Vet.App. 303
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that in determining whether lay evidence is competent to satisfy an evidentiary hurdle in a service connection claim depends on the type of disability claimed.

The Court concluded that a lay person is competent to identify varicose veins that are unnaturally distended or abnormally swollen and tortuous.  The Court explained that because varicose veins may be diagnosed by their unique and readily identifiable features, determining the presence of varicose veins is not “medical in nature” and can be made through lay observation.

The Court also held that if VA provides an examination when developing a service-connection claim, even if not statutorily obligated to do so, the examination must be adequate or the veteran must be notified as to why one will not or cannot be provided.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.  However, in determining whether lay statements satisfy any evidentiary threshold, it must be determined whether the specific type of disability can be demonstrated by lay evidence.  The presence of “unique and readily identifiable features” perceptible to lay observation should be considered. (Note: In Savage v. Gober, 10 Vet.App. 488 (1997), the Court held that continuity of symptomatology may be established by (1) a condition “noted” during service; (2) evidence of post service continuity of the same symptomatology; and (3) medical or, in certain circumstances, lay evidence of a nexus between the present disability and post service symptomatology.  The Court, in Savage, stated that if the condition was one as to which a lay person’s observation is competent, medical evidence of “noting” is not necessarily required.  In Wilson v. Derwinski, 2 Vet.App. 16 (1991), the Court emphasized that “symptoms, not treatment, are the essence of any evidence of continuity of symptomatology.”)

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran rendered honorable service from September 1965 to September 1967.  Aside from a history of leg cramps noted at separation, the service medical records (SMRs) show no abnormalities of the vascular system or lower extremities.  The veteran filed a claim for service connection for varicose veins in 1996, claiming it began during service as a result of running on hard surfaces while wearing boots.  A January 1997 VA examination rendered a diagnosis of venous insufficiency of the legs, more marked on the left.  The examiner indicated that the veteran’s medical records were unavailable for review.  No etiology opinion was given.

After the denial of his claim, the veteran submitted a statement that although pain had just recently developed in the left leg, it has exhibited a horrid appearance since 1966.  He described the veins in his left leg as “erupting” in service and causing unsightly bulges.  He explained that he never sought treatment for the condition in service because as a medical supply clerk he served in a unit with several doctors that had observed his condition and told the veteran that it did not require formal medical treatment.

Following an initial remand, the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) denied the veteran’s claim in 2004.  The Board considered the veteran’s lay testimony, but denied the claim for lack of evidence supporting his assertions.  The Board determined that the SMRs showed no diagnosis of varicose veins, there was no competent evidence linking the varicose veins to service, and the veteran, as a layman, is not competent to offer an opinion as to medical diagnosis or causation.

The veteran argued before the Court that his lay statements constituted a “noting” that his varicose veins started in and have persisted continuously since service; thus, providing competent evidence of continuity of symptomatology entitling him to service connection pursuant to [38 C.F.R. § 3.303(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=09cd81ab0fde3bade1c99c39dc90d0d2&node=se38.1.3_1303&rgn=div8) and *Savage*.  He averred that the Board erred in rejecting his lay statements, as varicose veins are a condition capable of lay observation not requiring supporting medical evidence to show their presence.

The Court found that the Board erred by concluding that the presence of varicose veins is a medical determination that must be supported by competent evidence, as a lay person is competent to identify the condition because of its unique nature.  The Court supported this finding with reference to medical treatises that described varicose veins as “elongated” or “visibly dilated and tortuous.”

In concluding that a lay person is competent to identify veins that are unnaturally distended or abnormally swollen and tortuous, the Court, again referring to a medical treatise, noted that the initial symptoms – tense superficial veins – do not appear to be capable of lay observation.

Although the Court indicated that the Board would have been correct in weighing the veteran’s testimony and making a credibility determination as to whether it supported a finding of service connection, it concluded that the Board, in dismissing the veteran’s lay assertions as incompetent, committed prejudicial error by not adequately considering material evidence favorable to the veteran’s claim. (Note: Competency is a legal concept determining whether testimony may be heard and considered, while credibility is a factual determination going to the probative value of the evidence to be made after it has been admitted.  See Layno v. Brown, 6 Vet.App. 465 (1994).)

The Court also found the January 1997 VA examination inadequate as the examiner did not review the veteran’s claims file and did not indicate whether the veteran’s assertions of continued symptomatology were considered.  The Secretary argued that any inadequacy was nonprejudical because there was no duty to provide the examination in the first instance as there was no medical evidence showing varicose veins in service and the Board rejected the veteran’s claimed history of continuity of symptomatology; however, the Court, in rejecting this argument, held that once VA undertakes the effort to provide an examination in a service-connection claim, even if not legally required to do, it must ensure that such examination is adequate or notify veteran why an examination will not or cannot be provided.

The Court vacated and remanded the Board decision for further adjudication consistent with its opinion.
