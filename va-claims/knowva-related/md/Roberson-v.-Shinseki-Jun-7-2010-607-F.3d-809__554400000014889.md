Decision Assessment Document
 Roberson v. Shinseki, Jun 7, 2010, 607 F.3d 809
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that to establish entitlement to compensation benefits under the former version of [38 U.S.C. § 1151](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1151)[[1]](#f1) for an alleged failure to diagnose or similar act of omission, a claimant must establish that the VA should have diagnosed or acted but failed to do so. Note: This applies to compensation claims under 38 U.S.C. § 1151 filed prior to October 1, 1997.  Congress amended section 1151 in 1996 following the U.S. Supreme Court’s decision in Brown v. Gardner, 513 U.S. 115 (1994) , in which the Supreme Court held that the version of section 1151 extant at the time did not require a claimant to prove fault in order to recover.

The Federal Circuit explained that this is not an element in addition to causation, but serves as the means of establishing the causal connection, or proximate cause, between the injury or the aggravation of the injury and the VA treatment.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, in June 1995, was seen at the Riverside Methodist Hospital (Riverside) for a possible stroke; however, a CT scan was negative.  He was treated by VA the same month and tentatively diagnosed with an adjustment disorder.

Two months later, he was seen at the Riverside emergency room.  This time, a CT scan showed a possible metastatic area in the posterior fossa.  The discharge summary indicated a mass in the right cerebellum with involvement in the liver and several subcutaneous masses.  A medical consultation indicated that the veteran‘s cancer most likely originated in the lung; however, other sources could not be ruled out.  The veteran died in October 1995.  The cause of death was attributed to cardiac arrhythmia due to pneumonia caused by metastatic cancer and status post cerebrovascular accident.

The surviving spouse, in May 1996, filed a claim for DIC benefits, asserting that the failure of VA physicians to diagnose the veteran’s cancer hastened his death.  A private physician stated in January 1998 that the veteran’s cancer was advanced at the time of the August 1995 diagnosis and estimated the cancer’s onset to be four to eight months prior to diagnosis.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in 1998, upheld the regional office’s denial of DIC benefits; however, the Veterans Court vacated the decision and remanded for further development.

In July 2003, two VA physicians reviewed the claims file and stated that there were no symptoms recorded during the episodes of VA treatment suggestive of a medical condition that warranted further investigation.  The physicians placed the onset of the disease at four to six months before the August 1995 diagnosis and indicated that it was impossible to state that the veteran could have been cured if the disease had been detected earlier; however, death from extensive small cell carcinoma with brain involvement usually, but not always, results in death within 10 months.

The Board again denied the appeal in September 2005, determining that there was no evidence that VA treatment, specifically the lack of a diagnosis of the veteran’s small cell carcinoma, had the effect of hastening the veteran’s death.

The Veterans Court, finding that the appellant had not shown that VA should have diagnosed the veteran’s cancer prior to his actual diagnosis, affirmed the Board’s decision.  Relying upon the reasoning of [VA General Counsel (GC) Opinion 5-01 (February 5, 2001)](http://www.va.gov/ogc/docs/2001/prc05-2001.doc), which concluded that a showing of fault is a necessary element of causation in a failure-to-diagnose case, the Veterans Court held that, under former section 1151, the injury, or aggravation of an injury in a failure-to-diagnose case, must necessarily be the failure to diagnosis the condition.  Upon demonstration of the injury or aggravation, a claimant must then prove that the failure to diagnose resulted in additional disability or death.

The appellant argued before the Federal Circuit that the Veterans Court and the GC opinion erroneously reinterpreted the [*Gardner*](http://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/513/115) ruling and reinserted a negligence or fault standard into section 1151 (1996) for one class of cases: acts of omission.  The appellant claimed that *Gardner* is binding precedent and the Veterans Court cannot revisit the previous version of section 1151 and reinsert a fault standard into a statute, the language of which the Supreme Court found to be clear.

The Federal Circuit, in rejecting the appellant’s arguments, concluded that a fault standard is needed in failure-to-diagnose claims under former section 1151 because there would otherwise be no means for eliminating remote consequences or excluding coverage based on an injury’s or a disease’s natural progression.  The Federal Circuit noted that a fault requirement initially appears to be inconsistent with *Gardner* (also recognized by the GC opinion); however, the claimant in *Gardner* sought recovery for aggravation of an injury resulting from the VA’s surgical treatment – an act of commission – while the *Roberson* appellant seeks to recover for VA’s failure to diagnose – an act of omission.  The Federal Circuit observed that this was a significant distinction and that for claims alleging an act of omission, the only way to demonstrate causation is to show that VA should have diagnosed the condition but failed to do so.

The Federal Circuit concluded that the Veterans Court reviewed the Board’s determination under the correct legal standard by requiring that the appellant prove that VA should have diagnosed the veteran’s cancer.  Accordingly, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court decision, as it found that the Veterans Court correctly interpreted the elements required for claimants’ recovery under the former version of section [1151](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1151).
