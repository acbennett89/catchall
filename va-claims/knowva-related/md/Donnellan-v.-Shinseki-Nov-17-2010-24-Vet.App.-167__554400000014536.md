Decision Assessment Document
 Donnellan v. Shinseki, Nov 17, 2010, 24 Vet.App. 167
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the term “aggravated in line of duty” as it is used in [38 U.S.C. § 101(24)(B)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/101)[[1]](#f1)means that in order for a claimant to have active service to qualify as a veteran based upon a period of active duty for training (ADT), the evidence must establish that the claimant experienced a permanent increase in the preexisting disability beyond the natural progress of the disease or injury during the ADT period.  The Court explained that an ADT claimant must establish a causal relationship between the worsening of the preexisting condition and ADT, by showing that the preexisting condition worsened beyond natural progress during the ADT period.

Note: 38 U.S.C. § 101(24)(B) provides that the term “active military, naval, or air service” includes:  “Any period of active duty for training during which the individual concerned was disabled or died from a disease or injury incurred or aggravated in line of duty.”

The Court held that the presumption of aggravation under [38 U.S.C. § 1153](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1153) is unavailable to claimants whose claims are based on ADT because they do not have veteran status.  The Court, concluding that section 101(24)(B) carries the same definition of “aggravated” as in 38 U.S.C. § 1153, held that the ADT claimant bears the burden of demonstrating that the pre-existing disability permanently worsened during ADT and that the worsening was not the result of natural progress of the disease or injury.

The Court also held that VA must apply the “benefit of the doubt” standard when determining veteran status.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The appellant, who served in the Army National Guard from October 1969 through February 2000, had a portion of his sigmoid colon surgically removed in 1996 after having been diagnosed with colon cancer.  In March 1998, after multiple polyps were found in the veteran’s colon and rectum, he underwent surgical removal of the entire large colon and portion of the small intestine that was attached directly to the rectum.  The following month he was hospitalized for five days for treatment of an “intra-abdominal abscess.”

The appellant had ADT from May 30, 1998, through June 5, 1998.  He was hospitalized on June 3, 1998, with fever, chills, and severe abdominal pain.  Emergency surgery was conducted, which resulted in removal of 48 inches of small intestine and drainage of the abdominal abscess.  A small bowel enterocutaneous fistula was also discovered and conservatively treated.  A diagnosis of “perforated small bowel secondary to intra-abdominal abscess, and resolving small bowel fistula” was given.

In October 2001, the appellant filed a compensation claim for perforated small intestine.  The regional office (RO) denied the claim; however, the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in December 2004, remanded the claim with instructions that a medical opinion be obtained as to whether the status post colectomy underwent a permanent increase in severity beyond its natural progression during the May-June 1998 ADT.

The Board, upon return of the case from the RO, determined that the opinion obtained by the RO was inadequate and requested an independent medical opinion to answer the question from the remand.  In December 2006, a private physician, characterizing the questions as “somewhat confusing,” indicated that the period of ADT did not cause the claimant’s fistulous disease, but he could have had a setback during ADT that could have reopened the preservice fistula.  However, the physician did not state whether the appellant’s preexisting condition underwent a permanent increase in severity and whether any such increase was beyond the natural progression of the condition.

The Board, in March 2007, denied the appeal, concluding that the preexisting colon cancer and complications of treatment were not aggravated during ADT.  The Board determined that the appellant was entitled to the presumption of aggravation, but that there was clear and unmistakable evidence that the ADT did not aggravate the preexisting condition.

The appellant argued before the Court that the Board’s decision should be reversed because VA had not shown by clear and unmistakable evidence that his preexisting condition was not aggravated in service.  He maintained that a claimant with only ADT may establish aggravation only by showing that his preexisting condition “increased in disability” during service.  He argued that the presumption of aggravation would apply and the government would have the burden of showing a lack of aggravation by clear and unmistakable evidence that the increase in disability was due to the natural progress of the disease.  Alternatively, he also argued for remand of the Board’s decision because of noncompliance with the remand’s instructions.  *See Stegall v. West*, 11 Vet.App. 268 (1998).

The Court rejected the appellant’s arguments concerning what was required for establishment of “aggravation” of preexisting disability during a period of ADT.  The Court held that section [101(24)(B)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/101) carried the same definition of “aggravated” as in section [1153](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1153); thus, both elements of aggravation in section 1153 (aggravation and beyond natural progression) were included in section 101(24)(B).

The Court further held, contrary to the Board’s decision but consistent with the Secretary’s position, that the presumption of aggravation was inapplicable to an ADT claimant, as such individual has no veteran status, and that such claimant bears the burden of demonstrating both that a preexisting disability worsened during a period of ADT and that such worsening was not due to natural progression of the disease or injury.  The Court explained that placing the burden of both elements of aggravation on an ADT claimant is consistent with the clear distinction that section 101(24) makes between an ADT claimant and an active duty claimant.

Although agreeing with the Secretary’s arguments regarding aggravation under section 101(24)(B), the Court found that the 2006 independent medical evaluation was inadequate as it failed to comply with the Board’s original remand instructions from December 2004.  The Court advised that the private physician’s response that she was confused by the questions should have signaled to the Board that her response was inadequate to address the questions posed to her.

The Court remanded the Board’s March 2007 decision with instructions that the Board ensure compliance with the original remand order and obtain a medical opinion that addresses the Board’s 2004 remand order.
