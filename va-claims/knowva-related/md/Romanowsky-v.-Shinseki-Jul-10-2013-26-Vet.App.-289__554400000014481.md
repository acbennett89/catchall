Decision Assessment Document
 Romanowsky v. Shinseki, Jul 10, 2013, 26 Vet.App. 289
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) erred by refusing to consider an in-service May 2008 diagnosis of the veteran’s adjustment disorder in determining that the veteran did not suffer from a current disability at the time he filed his November 2008 claim for VA disability compensation benefits.  The Court held that the Board inaccurately interpreted [*McClain v. Nicholson*, 21 Vet.App. 319 (2007)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014804/), in excluding the May 2008 examination from consideration, as *McClain* does not prohibit evidence predating the filing of a claim from establishing a current disability for purposes of establishing service connection.

Note: The Court held in **McClain** that that the requirement that a veteran have a current disability before service connection may be awarded is satisfied when the veteran has a disability at the time a claim for VA disability compensation is filed or during the pendency of that claim even though the disability resolves prior to the claim’s adjudication.

The Court held that when the record contains a recent diagnosis of disability prior to a veteran filing a claim for benefits based on that disability, the report of diagnosis is relevant evidence that VA must address in determining whether a current disability existed at the time the claim was filed or during its pendency.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served in the Navy from May 1995 to May 1999 and in the Air Force from November 2002 to October 2008.  In May 2008, his commanding officer, citing the veteran’s two counsellings, a reprimand, and having a pending administrative action, requested that he be seen for a formal mental health evaluation.  A psychologist diagnosed the veteran with, inter alia, an adjustment disorder of mixed disturbance of emotions and conduct and indicated that the disorder was so severe that it significantly impaired his ability to function effectively in the military environment, making him unsuitable for military service.  The veteran was subsequently administratively discharged.

In November 2008, the veteran filed a service connection claim for an adjustment disorder and underwent a VA examination the following month.  The examiner concluded that the veteran did not have an adjustment disorder, finding that his decreased motivation and problems trusting people were “normal issues that individuals go through” and that he showed no significant impairment in social and/or occupational functioning.  The examiner noted the in-service diagnosis of adjustment disorder, but did not further address the previous diagnosis.  The regional office (RO) denied service connection for an adjustment disorder on the basis that the medical evidence of record failed to show a clinical diagnosis of that disorder.

The Board, in July 2011, denied the appeal, finding that the veteran did not have a current diagnosis of a mental disorder.  The Board, citing **McClain** determined that since the May 2008 diagnosis fell outside of the claim period, there was no current disability for service connection purposes.

The Court held that the Board erred in finding no evidence of a current diagnosis of a mental disorder, finding that the Board had mistakenly suggested that *McClain* held that where evidence of a diagnosis falls outside of the claim period, such evidence cannot establish a current disability as a matter of law.  The Court stated that such a misunderstanding of *McClain* would permit VA to deny the existence of a disability at the time the veteran filed his claim when there was a recent doctor’s diagnosis of that disability, to the extent of incorrectly establishing that a report diagnosing a veteran with a disability one day before his filing of a claim would be irrelevant to whether a current disability existed on the date of filing.

The Court also held that the Board erred in relying on the December 2008 examination alone as adequate evidence upon which to make a decision, as the examination report did not reflect that the examiner effectively considered the near-contemporaneous diagnosis of an adjustment disorder or provided a sufficient explanation for his conclusion that the veteran did not suffer from an adjustment disorder, in light of the May 2008 diagnosis.

The Court explained that without an indication, positive or negative, that the recently diagnosed psychological disorder resolved itself or was incorrectly diagnosed, the VA examination is inadequate as a basis for the Board’s decision.  Also, the Court, noting that the VA rating schedule provides ratings for a chronic, but not an acute, adjustment disorder, found that the examiner had erred in not offering an opinion as to whether the prior in-service diagnosis was of an acute, rather than chronic, adjustment disorder.  Accordingly, the Court concluded that the Board improperly made such determination on its own, without the benefit of a medical opinion and erred in relying upon the December 2008 VA examination.

The Court vacated and remanded the Board’s decision with instructions that the Board readjudicate the veteran’s claim and provide a medical opinion resolving the discrepancies between the existing medical examinations.  The Board was directed to specifically account for the evidence that the veteran was diagnosed with an adjustment disorder prior to his discharge from service for that disability.
