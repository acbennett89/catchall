**Decision Assessment Document**

***Morse v. McDonough*, 994 F.3rd 1371 (April 19, 2021)**

**United States Court of Appeals, Federal Circuit**

**Case No: 2020-1838**

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit Court of Appeals (Federal Circuit) found that the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (CAVC) did not commit legal error in sustaining the finding of the Board of Veterans Appeals (Board) that internal Joint Services Records Research Center (JSRCC) memorandum did not constitute official service department record for purposes of 38 C.F.R. § 3.156(c). The relevant memorandum was produced by a VA employee, not a service department, it merely summarized information that was already of record, and it did not contain new information from official service department records.

**Impact on VBA**:

The Adjudication Procedures Manual M21-1 may be amended to reference the Federal Circuit’s decision.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The Veteran served in the Navy between March 1970 and October 1972. Between September 1971 and March 1972, he was stationed at the Naval Support Facility in Da Nang, Vietnam. In 1999, he filed a claim for “compensation or pension” in which he listed several disabilities he had suffered from since February 1996, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). In 2000, he filed a claim for service connection for PTSD. The Regional Office (RO) granted his claim for a nonservice connected pension in 2001. However, in a 2002 rating decision, the RO denied service connection for PTSD. The rating decision noted that hospital records between 1999 and 2001 showed occasional diagnoses of PTSD but no ongoing treatment for that condition. The rating decision also stated that “[a]lthough we have evidence of a diagnosis of [PTSD] there has been no credible evidence of verification of the claimed stressors” and therefore found “no basis on which to establish service connection for PTSD.” The Veteran did not appeal that rating decision and it became final.

In 2004, the Veteran attempted to reopen his PTSD claim. Medical records relating to a period of hospitalization during that year included a diagnosis of PTSD, but in November 2004, the RO denied the request for reopening, finding that the evidence submitted was not new and material. The Veteran filed a notice of disagreement with that rating decision, and in response the RO reversed course and reopened his claim. The RO also indicated that it had made a request for additional service department records, which were received on July 11, 2005. Those records showed that in June 1972 the Veteran saw a psychiatrist who reported that the Veteran was “very tense and nervous,” appeared “moderately depressed,” and was anxious about personal problems, including finances and his wife’s behavior while he was gone. Another medical report from May 1971 assessed the Veteran as experiencing “anxiety-situational.” The service department records also included a report of a Vietnamese sailor being killed in Da Nang when a cement truck went out of control and struck him. In his testimony before a RO hearing officer on July 27, 2005, the Veteran identified that incident as a stressor for his PTSD.

In October 2006, the Veteran underwent a VA examination, which included a medical examination and review of the materials in his claims file. In the C&P examination report, the examiner concluded that the Veteran’s case was “challenging with respect to definitive diagnosis.” Although the examiner noted that the medical record “is replete with diagnoses of PTSD” based on the symptoms reported by the Veteran, he found that the Veteran was “unable, at least spontaneously, to provide symptoms that convincingly relate to his reported military exposure.” In light of all the circumstances, the examiner concluded that diagnosis of military-related PTSD was “impossible at the present time.” Consequently, the RO denied service connection for PTSD for lack of a confirmed diagnosis.

The Board of Veterans Appeals (Board) affirmed that decision in 2008, noting that the record showed several diagnoses and hospitalizations for PTSD dating back to 1999, but that a two-day evaluation in 2000 had not supported a confirmed diagnosis of PTSD. Like the RO, the Board relied heavily on the 2006 VA examination, in which the examiner found that the Veteran did not have PTSD. The Veteran did not appeal the Board’s 2008 decision and it became final.

The Veteran subsequently sought to have the denial of benefits overturned on the ground of clear and unmistakable error. That claim was denied by the Board in 2014, was not further pursued, and became final.

In 2009, the Veteran again sought to reopen his claim. A VA examiner conducted another PTSD examination and diagnosed PTSD with moderate symptoms. In addition, in January 2010 a Joint Services Records Research Center (JSRRC) coordinator prepared a memorandum summarizing the traumatic events that the Veteran reported experiencing in Da Nang. The memorandum noted that it is “well documented that the Da Nang area of Vietnam often received rocket, mortar, and small arms attacks” and added that the events that took place, “as reported by the veteran, are confirmed and are otherwise consistent with the circumstances, conditions, and/or hardships of such service even though we were unable to locate official records of the specific occurrence.” In 2010, the RO reviewed the evidence of record including the verified stressor information and acknowledged service-connection for the Veteran’s PTSD effective date of August 5, 2009, the date of his most recent request to reopen his claim.

The Veteran appealed the effective date of his benefits award, arguing that the effective date should have been made retroactive to 1999, the date he first filed a claim referencing PTSD. However, in 2016 the Board upheld the RO’s decision.

The Veteran then appealed to CAVC, which referred the matter back to the Board pursuant to the parties’ joint motion for remand. The Board issued another decision in 2018, once again upholding the RO’s decision assigning an effective date of August 5, 2009 (the date of the most recent request for reopening) for the Veteran’s PTSD. The Board explained that the service department records received in July 2005 did not justify an earlier effective date, noting that those records “were specifically considered as part of a merits reconsideration of the Veteran’s claim in the unappealed January 2008 Board decision.” The 2008 Board’s consideration of those records in that proceeding “exhausted VA’s duty to reconsider the claim under 38 C.F.R. § 3.156(c)(1).” Consequently, the Board ruled, “an earlier effective date based on those same service department records is not available under 38 C.F.R. § 3.156(c).”

Once again, the Veteran appealed that decision to the CAVC, which affirmed the Board’s decision. The CAVC agreed that the 2008 Board’s ruling became final when it was not appealed and that VA’s obligation to reconsider the PTSD claim upon receipt of service department records received in July 2005 was exhausted in 2008. The CAVC found that the 2008 Board decision conducted a “holistic reweighing of all record evidence and adjudication of the claim on the merits,” which “satisfies the duty to reconsider under § 3.156(c).” The CAVC also ruled that the 2010 memorandum by JSRRC coordinator did not constitute a service department record that triggered a renewed obligation to reconsider the Veteran’s PTSD claim under § 3.156(c). The CAVC concluded that the 2008 Board decision had reviewed the PTSD claim in light of the evidence of record at the time service connection was originally sought, along with the service department records obtained in 2005 and other evidence developed later. The CAVC held that the Board, effectively “reconsidered” the PTSD claim in 2008 and was not required to do so again.

In his appeal to the Federal Circuit, the Veteran reiterated his position that: (1) the 2008 Board did not properly “reconsider” his claim as required under 38 C.F.R. § 3.156(c); and (2) the 2010 JSRCC memorandum is a service department record and thus the 2018 Board should have reconsidered the original 1999 PTSD claim pursuant to § 3.156(c).

The Federal Circuit began its analysis by acknowledging that 38 C.F.R. § 3.156(a) provides that previously adjudicated claims can be reopened upon the presentation of “new and material evidence.” If benefits are granted after the claim is reopened, the effective date from which those benefits are awarded is the date on which the request for reopening was filed. The Federal Circuit also noted that 38 C.F.R § 3.156(c) creates an exception to that rule. That exception applies when a veteran seeks to reopen a claim and official service department records are obtained that were not previously part of the claims file. In that situation, VA is required to “reconsider” the veteran’s claim, taking into account all the evidence of record, including the newly obtained service department records. If the veteran is then granted benefits based at least in part on the service department records, the award of benefits is made retroactive to the date of the veteran’s original claim, or the date on which the veteran became eligible for benefits, whichever is later.

The Federal Circuit determined the Veteran’s challenge to the 2008 Board’s ruling fails because he did not appeal the decision of the 2008 Board, which then became final. Citing controlling case law, the Federal Circuit reiterate that a final decision of VA on a benefit claim is not subject to challenge other than on grounds of clear and unmistakable error or upon the submission of new and material evidence. The Veteran argued that § 3.156(c) renders the 2008 Board’s decision non-final because of the service department records VA received in 2005. The Federal Circuit noted that that argument was problematic because § 3.156(c) provides an exception to the finality of decisions made before previously unavailable service department records are obtained and considered by VA. In this case, the Board’s 2008 decision was made after those records were obtained and considered. Although the Veteran believed the 2008 Board’s consideration of the service department records obtained in 2005 was flawed, that argument was foreclosed because he did not appeal the 2008 Board’s decision. Hence, as of 2009, the Veteran’s only avenue for relief on his PTSD claim was to seek reopening of his claim based upon new and material evidence under § 3.156(a). He pursued that avenue of relief and succeeded. As a result, he received benefits retroactive to August 5, 2009, the date he filed that request to reopen.

The Veteran’s second contention was that the 2010 memorandum from the regional office’s JSRRC coordinator qualified as an official service department record and therefore when VA acknowledged service connection for his PTSD in 2010, it was required to make that award of benefits retroactive to the date of his initial claim. The Federal Circuit noted the CAVC rejected that argument, concluding that the 2010 memorandum was as a matter of law merely an internal DVA communication and not an official service department record within the meaning of § 3.156(c).

The Federal Circuit acknowledged that the JSRRC coordinator’s 2010 memorandum was produced by a VA employee, not a service department, and that it merely summarized information that was already of record. In particular, the memorandum recited that “the events that took place as reported by the Veteran are confirmed and are otherwise consistent with the circumstances, conditions, and/or hardships of such service even though official records of the specific occurrence were unable to be located.” In sum, the record in this case showed that the memorandum did not contain any new information from official service department records, and thus did not constitute “an official service department record” for purposes of § 3.156(c). The CAVC sustained the Board’s findings and its conclusion on that issue and in view of the Board’s findings, the Federal Circuit determined the CAVC committed no legal error.
