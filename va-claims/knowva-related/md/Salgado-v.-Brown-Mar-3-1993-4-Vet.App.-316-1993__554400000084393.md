Decision Assessment Document
Salgado v. Brown, Mar 3, 1993, 4 Vet.App. 316 (1993)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the protection afforded by 38 U.S.C. 110 applies to ratings for compensation purposes whether or not a veteran elects to receive a monetary award.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court's holding has been incorporated into the guidance contained in Manual M21-1, III.iv.8.c.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran was awarded a 50% service-connected disability evaluation in 1961.  He never elected to receive compensation (in lieu of military retired pay?).  Between 1963 and 1986, the 50% rating was affirmed on eight occasions.  The decisions in 1983, 1985, and 1986 noted that the rating was protected.  In 1989, the RO determined that the rating was not protected and reduced the evaluation.  In doing so, the RO relied on a precedential opinion of the General Counsel (O.G.C. Prec. 31-90, initially issued as O.G.C. Prec. 8-79, Oct. 2, 1978) which held that protection arises only if a rating has been the basis of an award for the required period.  The Board of Veterans' Appeals (Board) concurred.

The Court noted that section 110 contains the phrase "for compensation purposes" as a necessary requirement for protection to accrue.  This is reiterated in the implementing regulation, 38 C.F.R. 3.951.  The sole issue before the Court was the meaning of the phrase.

In determining the meaning of a statute, the Court is required to examine the specific language at issue and the overall structure of the statute.  The Board argued that for a rating to be "for compensation purposes", the rating must be the foundation for a concurrent award of monetary benefits.  However, the Court stated that both parties agreed that, under a literal reading, the statute does not require a monetary award.  Nevertheless, the Secretary argued that a literal interpretation of the phrase would be contrary to the congressional intent and lead to ludicrous results.  The Court stated that the Secretary "does not provide an illustration of the absurd result of a literal interpretation, nor is it obvious to the Court."  To the contrary, the Court noted that it was not unlikely that Congress could give a blanket protection to a rating in effect for two decades, with VA continuing to find the condition warranted payment of monetary benefits, had the veteran chosen to accept such compensation.

In examining the legislative history, the Court found no clear evidence of legislative intent contrary to protection of a disability rating without an actual concurrent monetary disbursement.  "It is plausible that Congress intended to protect a rating which had been in place for 20 years regardless of any monetary award."  The Secretary's argument that congressional intent was to guarantee solely monetary benefits to which the veteran has grown accustomed was determined to be unpersuasive.  The legislative history speaks to "benefits" derived from a rating for "compensation, pension, or insurance purposes."  There is no indication of a limitation to monetary benefits only.  As both parties pointed out, there are non-pecuniary benefits derived from a 50% rating.

The Secretary argued that the Board is bound by the VA General Counsel's interpretation and, consequently, has committed no legal error in following that interpretation.  However, the Court noted that the Secretary failed to recognize that "although courts frequently grant deference to an administrative agency's interpretation of an applicable statute under certain circumstances, this is true only when the statute is unclear."

Therefore, the decision of the Board was reversed and the matter remanded for restoration of the 50% protected rating.
