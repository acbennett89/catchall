Decision Assessment Document
 Buczynski v. Shinseki, Jan 6, 2011, 24 Vet.App. 221
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that in evaluating a skin disorder under [38 C.F.R. § 4.118](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=97cd1294b0caf406821c77e2589acc40&node=se38.1.4_1118&rgn=div8), Diagnostic Code 7806 (1995), a determination of exceptional repugnance is a subjective assessment based on how a lay observer would respond to the condition.  This determination is made by the adjudicator on the basis of a review of the medical evidence.  It is not required that the medical examiner opine as to whether the skin disorder is “exceptionally repugnant” or not “exceptionally repugnant” for the adjudicator to make such determination.

Note: The rating schedule for the skin was revised effective August 2002.  The evaluation for a skin disorder under DC 7806 is currently based upon percentage of entire body or exposed areas affected or the type of medication regimen prescribed for treatment of the skin disorder.

The Court explained that when words are objective and have a precise medical meaning, then an examiner may be required to use those words to provide an adequate opinion.  However, if the words are subjective, such as “exceptionally repugnant,” and do not have a precise medical meaning, then a medical opinion is adequate when the examiner provides sufficient detail so that the rating specialist can interpret the report and make a subjective determination as to whether the condition meets the rating criteria.

The Court advised that the percentage of visible areas of the body affected should not be the only factor in determining exceptional repugnance.  The Court provided the example of a skin condition confined to the feet and ankles that may still be considered “exceptionally repugnant” if the claimant was wearing shorts or sandals, or was barefoot, or had extensive oozing seeping through the trousers, or if an odor emitted from the condition that was repugnant despite the skin condition not being visible.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, in March 2004, was granted service connection for lichen simplex chronicus with hyperkeratosis of the ankles and feet.  A 10 percent evaluation was assigned from January 1995.  In December 2006, the evaluation was increased to 30 percent from January 1995 and to 60 percent effective August 30, 2002 (pursuant to a change in the rating criteria for DC 7806).

A VA examination from July 1997 showed the skin of both feet to be normal and without discoloration, inflammation, ulceration or soft tissue swelling.  A VA examination from September 1998 revealed lichenified eczematous eruptions over the ankles and extending down into the instep with oozing, bleeding, excoriatory marks, and scaling.  The examiner noted that the veteran had used a cream for decades to relieve symptoms and would immediately become symptomatic if not using the cream.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in its August 2008 decision, denied the veteran’s appeal for higher evaluations for his skin disorder.  The Board discussed the findings of the VA examinations and observed that none of the treatment records or examiners characterized the skin condition as repugnant.  The Board also determined that since the disability was essentially confined to the skin of the feet and ankles and it was not extensive or exceptionally repugnant, the criteria for an increased rating under the old criteria were not met.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred by solely relying on an absence of evidence as negative evidence in denying his appeal and that the Board failed to provide adequate reasons or bases for its decision.

The Court described the first prong of the rating criteria for a 50 percent evaluation under DC 7806 in effect when the veteran filed his claim, “ulceration or extensive exudation or crusting, and systemic or nervous manifestations,” as an objective assessment based on the severity of the medical symptoms.  The second prong, whether the condition is “exceptionally repugnant,” is a subjective assessment based on how others respond to the condition.  The Court noted that neither the regulations nor its case law provides any guidance as to how the second prong of the test is to be interpreted or applied.

The Court, referencing precedents holding that the Board may not consider the absence of evidence as substantive negative evidence, found that there was no medical reason, in this case, as why a doctor would be expected to comment on the repugnance of a condition.  Thus, this was not a situation where the silence in regard to the condition could be taken as proof that a doctor did not observe the symptom.  The Court concluded that the Board had determined that the veteran’s skin condition was not exceptionally repugnant because neither the treatment records nor the medical examiners had characterized the condition in those words.  Thus, the Court concluded that the Board erred by treating the absence of evidence as negative evidence that the veteran’s skin condition was not exceptionally repugnant, because this is not situation where silence in the records tends to disprove the fact.

The Court concluded that the July 1997 and September 1998 VA medical examiners described the veteran’s skin condition in sufficient detail so that the Board should have been able to provide an informed determination as to whether the condition was “exceptionally repugnant” between 1995 and 2002, under DC 7806 (1995).

The Secretary argued that because DC 7800 (1995) was limited to disfigurement of the head, face, or neck, and because DC 7806 was to be interpreted in uniformity with DC 7800, DC 7806 must also be limited to disfigurement of the head, face, or neck.  The Court rejected this argument, finding no indication in the language of DC 7806 limiting it to the head, face, or neck, whereas there is specific language in DC 7800 that limits it to the head, face, and neck.  The Court stated that if the Secretary had intended to limit DC 7806 in a similar manner, he would have included such language in the regulation.

The Court vacated the Board’s decision in regard to the appropriate disability rating from January 1994 to August 2002 and remanded the matter to the Board for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.
