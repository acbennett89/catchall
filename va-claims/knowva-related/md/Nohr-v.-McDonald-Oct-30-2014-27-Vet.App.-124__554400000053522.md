Decision Assessment Document
 Nohr v. McDonald, Oct 30, 2014, 27 Vet.App. 124
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the veteran’s submission of “interrogatories,” which requested documents and clarifications from a VA medical examiner, reasonably raised issues concerning (1) the competence of the VA examiner, (2) the adequacy of the opinion, and (3) VA’s duty to assist.

The Court further held that the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) erred in failing to address whether the reasonably raised issues required further development or to provide a statement of reasons or bases why such development was unnecessary.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Manual M21-1 has been revised at III.iv.3.D.2.o to provide guidance in accordance with the Court’s holding.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served from July 1971 to February 1975, filed a claim for service connection for dysthymic disorder in 2003.  The regional office denied the claim and the veteran appealed the decision.  The Board, in August 2010, denied the appeal, finding that the dysthymic disorder clearly and unmistakably preexisted and was not aggravated by service.  The Court granted the parties’ joint motion for remand in which it was agreed that an October 2009 VA medical opinion was equivocal as to aggravation and insufficient to constitute clear and unmistakable evidence that the dysthymic disorder was not aggravated during service.

The Board, on remand, obtained a VHA medical expert opinion, pursuant to 38 C.F.R. § 20.901, to address whether there is clear and unmistakable evidence that the veteran’s preexisting dysthymic disorder was not aggravated by service.  A VHA psychiatrist, in July 2011, reviewed the claims file.  The psychiatrist noted that the veteran had “not endorsed any traumatic event other than his ordinary military duty” and opined that “there is obvious and manifest evidence that the preexisting dysthymic disorder was not aggravated by service.”

The Board denied the claim in October 2011; however, the Court granted another JMR, in which the parties agreed that the VHA expert’s opinion was not supported by an adequate rationale.  In July 2012, the VHA expert reiterated that the veteran “did not endorse any traumatic event other than his ordinary military duty” and the in-service complaints were typical of dysthymic disorder “running its own course.”  She reaffirmed her opinion that there was obvious and manifest evidence that that the veteran’s preexisting dysthymic disorder was not aggravated by service and commented that the veteran’s “alcohol and substance use was certainly the contributor to his depressive mood condition during service.”  The VHA expert concluded:  “Respectfully, while I recognize my personal limitation, the Board should seek for the next expert opinion if this examiner’s report still is not satisfied by the Board review.”

The Board provided the veteran and his representative with a copy of the opinion and informed them that the veteran had 60 days in which to review the medical opinion and respond with additional evidence or argument.  The veteran’s counsel submitted eleven questions and request for documents, which he referred to as “interrogatories,” for the VHA expert to answer.  In the alternative, counsel requested the Board to subpoena the VHA expert to appear at a personal hearing. The written request contained questions and requests for documents to include the VHA expert’s current curriculum vitae, copy of the Board’s request letter for a medical opinion, written correspondence between the VHA and Board concerning the veteran, transcript of VHA expert’s interview with the veteran, all handwritten notes made during the interview, explanation of the phrase “personal limitation” in the medical opinion, explanation of the etiology for the use of the terms “ obvious and manifest,” and elaborate on what a “typical dysthymic disorder is” and what she considered to be “ordinary military duty.”

The Board, in April 2013, denied service connection for a dysthymic disorder.  The Board rejected the request for the VHA expert to answer interrogatories on the basis that there is no regulatory authority for interrogatories and stressed that the benefits system is non-adversarial.  The Board also denied the request to subpoena the VHA expert, finding that the request to be outside of the scope of the situations contemplated by 38 C.FR. § 20.711, as the regulation does not contemplate issuing subpoenas to allow witnesses to be questioned outside the context of a personal hearing.  The Board determined that the duty to assist had been fulfilled, as the veteran had not referred to outstanding records that he wanted VA to obtain, and the Board had obtained an adequate VHA expert opinion, which was based on a review of the claims file, including the veteran’s statements, and supported by an extensive rationale.  The Board afforded significant probative value to and relied upon the VHA expert’s opinion to conclude that there was clear and unmistakable evidence that the veteran’s preexisting dysthymic disorder did not increase in severity beyond its natural progression as a result of military service.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board violated his Fifth Amendment due process rights when it denied him a meaningful opportunity to participate in the development of his claim by refusing to allow him to question the VHA expert and preventing him from obtaining information necessary to effectively respond to the unfavorable medical opinion.

The Court held that the veteran’s questions for the VHA expert and request for documentary evidence reasonably raised questions as to the VHA expert’s competence, adequacy of the medical opinion, and VA’s duty to assist.

The Court found that the VHA expert’s statement of some unspecified “personal limitation” in providing the requested opinion arguably suggested that there may have been some irregularity in the process of selecting the expert to provide an opinion.  Thus, the Court concluded that the veteran’s request for the VHA expert’s curriculum vitae reasonably sought information necessary to overcome the presumption of competence generally afforded to VA-selected physicians. VA is presumed to choose a person who is qualified to provide a medical opinion in a particular case.  See Parks v. Shinseki, 716 F.3d 581, 585 (Fed. Cir. 2013).

The Court held that the VHA expert’s statement reasonably raised an issue concerning the adequacy of her opinion, and the veteran’s request for an explanation of the phrase “personal limitation” should have signaled to the Board that clarification may have been necessary or, at a minimum, a statement of reasons or bases why the veteran was not entitled to answers to his questions and why clarification was unnecessary.  The Court held that the Board’s failure to return the medical opinion report for clarification or provide a statement of reasons or bases why such action is unnecessary frustrated judicial review.  The Court also found inadequacy in the Board’s reasons or bases by failing to address whether the duty to assist obligated VA to attempt to obtain the requested records.

Having found error, the Court concluded that the Board’s reason or bases error was prejudicial because the Board determined that the VHA expert’s opinion was adequate and supported by an extensive rationale without addressing the veteran’s questions that reasonably implicated the VHA expert’s competency and adequacy of her opinion.  The Court noted that the veteran had argued that had he received the documentary evidence and answers to his questions, he may have been able to diminish the probative value of the medical opinion below the level of “clear and unmistakable evidence” so that the Board would not have had a sufficient basis to find that the presumption of soundness had been rebutted.

The Court deemed it unnecessary to address the veteran’s constitutional argument because VA’s statutory and regulatory duty to assist the veteran in the development of his claim and the Board’s requirement to provide a written statement of reasons or bases on all material issues of act and law provide an adequate remedy.

The Court vacated and remanded the Board’s decision for further proceedings and readjudication consistent with its opinion.
