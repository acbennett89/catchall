Decision Assessment Document
 Wilson v. Mansfield, Oct 15, 2007, 506 F.3d 1055
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that [38 U.S.C. § 5103(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103) requires only that VA give a claimant notice at the outset of the claims process of the information and evidence necessary to substantiate the claim, before the initial RO decision, and in sufficient time to enable the claimant to submit relevant evidence.

The notice may be generic in the sense that it need not identify evidence specific to the individual claimant’s case (though it necessarily must be tailored to the specific nature of the veteran’s claim).  It need not describe the VA’s evaluation of the veteran’s particular claim.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual revision.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran’s counsel wrote to the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) in January 2005 and included the following request:  “If you determine there is significant negative evidence on a material issue in this claimant’s record, please let my client know what this evidence is and what types of evidence would aid in rebutting this negative evidence and thus substantiate these claims.”  Without responding to the request, the Board affirmed the denial of service connection for a psychiatric disability the following month.

The Veterans Court, in affirming the Board’s decision, stated that VA was not required to analyze the evidence gathered and inform the appellant of the inadequacy of his submissions.  The Veterans Court further explained that the duty to notify deals with evidence gathering, not the analysis of already gathered evidence, and concluded that the veteran was not entitled to an advisory opinion from the Board. Note: The Veterans Court had previously rejected that section 5103(a) requires “predecisional adjudication” in Locklear v. Nicholson, 20 Vet.App. 410 (2006).

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that the duty imposed by section 5103(a) is not limited to providing notice after the initial filing of a claim but continues throughout the claims process and that the statute requires a specific notice of what evidence is missing – that the VA must, at least upon request, share its predecisional assessment of the evidence.

The Federal Circuit, holding that [section 5103(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103)'s notice obligation was intended to be limited to the outset of the claims process, stated that the plain language of the statute does not suggest that the notice required extends beyond the filing of a “complete or substantially complete application.”  The Federal Circuit cited legislative history indicating that Congress intended that the purpose of the VCAA was “to reinstate VA’s traditional practice of assisting veterans at the *beginning* of the claims process.”  The Federal Circuit also noted that the existence of other statutes, such as [38 U.S.C. §§ 5104(b](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5104)) and [7105(d)(1)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/7105), each requiring specific notice at other points in the claims adjudication process, strongly suggests that section 5103(a) was not intended to sweep as broadly as the veteran contended.

The Federal Circuit also held that section 5103(a) is satisfied by generic notice and that specific notice of the missing evidence with respect to a particular claim is not required as there was no indication that Congress intended to require an analysis of the individual claim in each case.  The notice must simply identify the information and evidence necessary to substantiate the particular type of claim asserted by the veteran (referred to as a “generic notice”).

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court decision.
