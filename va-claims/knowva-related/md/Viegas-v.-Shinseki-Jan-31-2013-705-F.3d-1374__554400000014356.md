Decision Assessment Document
 Viegas v. Shinseki, Jan 31, 2013, 705 F.3d 1374
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that the first causation element under [38 U.S.C. § 1151](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1151) – requiring that a veteran’s additional disability be caused by hospital care or medical treatment received from VA – is satisfied not only when a disability is directly caused by hospital care or medical treatment provided by VA employees, but also when a disability is caused by VA’s installation and maintenance of the equipment necessary for such treatment to occur.

**Impact on VBA:**

[M21-1MR.IV.ii.2.G.33](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014567/) and 34 will be revised to comply with the Federal Circuit’s holding.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who was discharged from service in 1967, was injured in a diving accident following service, resulting in incomplete quadriplegia. In May 2004, after participating in a prescribed aquatic therapy session at a VA medical center (VAMC), he stopped to use a restroom within the VAMC. While in the restroom, the grab bar that he was using to lift himself into his wheelchair came loose from the wall and he fell to the ground. He sustained injuries to both his upper and lower extremities. His condition deteriorated after his fall as he was now limited to only being able to stand with assistance. Prior to the fall, he could sometimes walk with a walker.

In July 2004, the veteran filed a section [1151](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1151) claim, arguing that the injuries to his shoulder and neck resulted in loss of use of his lower extremities and impairment of his upper extremities. The regional office denied the claim because the veteran was “not in direct VA care” at the time of his fall. The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) affirmed the decision, concluding that section 1151 benefits are available only if a veteran’s “additional disability [is] the result of injury that was part of the natural sequence of cause and effect flowing directly from the actual provision of hospital care, medical or surgical treatment, or examination furnished by [the] VA and … such additional disability was directly caused by that VA activity.”

The Veterans Court upheld the Board decision, concluding that although the veteran’s injury occurred in a VA facility, it was not caused directly by “hospital care, medical or surgical treatment, or examination furnished by [the] VA.”

At the outset, the Federal Circuit observed that section 1151 has two causal elements: (1) the veteran’s additional disability must be caused by VA hospital care, medical care or surgical treatment, or examination; and (2) it must be proximately caused by some element of fault on the part of VA or an unforeseeable event. The Federal Circuit concluded that the veteran unquestionably met the second causation element since his injury was proximately caused by the VA’s failure to properly install and maintain the grab bar in the VAMC’s restroom. Thus, the Court concluded that the sole issue on appeal was whether the injury was “caused by” the medical treatment or hospital care he received from the VA.

The Government argued that section 1151 only applies to disabilities that are directly caused by actual care or treatment, rather than injuries that coincidentally occur in VA facilities. The veteran contended that his injury was covered under section 1151 because the statute’s causation requirement is satisfied whenever a veteran comes to a VAMC for medical treatment and, as a result of VA’s negligence, sustains an injury while on VA premises.

The Federal Circuit did not wholly agree with either position. The Court explained that nothing in section [1151](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1151) requires that the veteran’s injury must be “directly” caused by the “actual” medical care provided by VA personnel. However, section 1151 does not extend to the “remote consequences” of VA medical treatment, and a veteran is not entitled to section 1151 benefits simply because he was in a VAMC at the time he sustained an injury. The Federal Circuit concluded that recovery under the statute is not restricted to injuries caused by the actual care provided by VA medical personnel, but also includes treatment-related incidents occurring in the physical premises controlled and maintained by VA.

The Federal Circuit held that the veteran’s injury was caused by medical treatment furnished by VA because it was caused by VA’s installation and maintenance of the equipment necessary to provide him with medical treatment. The Court reasoned that VA cannot reasonably furnish hospital care or medical treatment without also providing access to handicapped-accessible restrooms. The Court further reasoned that restroom grab bars and other equipment specifically designed to assist the disabled are necessary components of VA’s health care services because without such equipment many veterans would be unable to obtain treatment.

The Federal Circuit rejected the Government’s assertion that the veteran’s injury was merely “coincident” with his prescribed physical therapy. The Federal Circuit indicated that the fact that the VA medical treatment normally involves interaction with VA personnel does not mean that such treatment *only* encompasses the actions of VA employees.

The Federal Circuit additionally made a point of noting that nothing in the legislative history of section 1151 indicates that Congress intended to preclude compensation for veterans’ injuries that stem from VA’s failure to properly install and maintain the equipment necessary to provide hospital care and medical treatment.

The Federal Circuit clarified, that, although section 1151 recognizes that a veteran should be compensated when VA inflicts loss while bestowing a benefit, the statute does not extend to remote consequences. For example, the Court indicated that the veteran would not have been covered under section 1151 if he were engaged at recreational activities at the VAMC hours later after reporting for an examination, as it may have been deemed a remote consequence of VA treatment. However, in this case, the Federal Circuit found that the injury was not a remote consequence of VA treatment; instead it occurred because the VA failed to properly install and maintain the equipment necessary for the provision of his medical care.

The Federal Circuit reversed the Veterans Court decision and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.
