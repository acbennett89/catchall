Decision Assessment Document
Cousin v. Wilkie, Sep 28, 2018, 905 F. 3d 1316 (Fed. Cir. 2018)
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit, holding that a defect is properly construed as excluding degenerative conditions, concluded that a 1954 regional office (RO) decision contained clear and unmistakable error (CUE) in denying service connection for spondylosis.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served in the Army from September 1951 to September 1953, showed no abnormalities or complaints related to his back upon service entrance.  Shortly after entering service he injured his back lifting and a month later, in May 1952, he was treated for back pain that was diagnosed as a mild back strain.  The veteran was placed on restricted duty and medically cleared to return to full duty the following month.  However, in March 1953 he was again treated and assessed with “back pain prior to service.”  An X-ray showed “pedicle defect L5” and “spondylosis.”  He was placed on a permanent profile for “weak back,” which determined him to be unfit for lifting, bending, prolonged marching, or carrying a pack.  The discharge examination noted that his back had been taped in 1952; however, it was asymptomatic.

The RO denied the veteran’s claim for service connection for his back condition in December 1954, finding that his mild back strain in service to be acute, without residuals.  Between 1979 and 2009, the veteran filed three applications to reopen his back claim, which were denied.

In January 2012, the veteran again filed to reopen his back claim and was granted service connection for a back disability by the RO in February 2013 with an effective date of January 19, 2012.  The veteran appealed the effective date, contending that there was CUE in prior denials.

The Board of Veteran’s Appeals (Board) denied the appeal, determining that the 1954 decision was final and did not contain CUE.  The Board acknowledged that the RO decision contained no reference to his permanent physical profile for the back in March 1953; however, the ROs at that time were not required to give a detailed explanation for its findings.  The Board found that the veteran was diagnosed with spondylosis; however, it indicated that because the veteran’s 1953 X-ray revealed evidence of a defect, the RO might have concluded that the condition was a congenital defect.

The Veterans Court affirmed the Board decision, finding that it had offered a plausible explanation for why the RO may have discounted the 1953 records.  The Veterans Court also noted that the RO was not required to fully explain its finding and it was not clear from the 1954 decision that the RO failed to consider certain presumptions in the veteran’s favor.

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that the in-service medical records established a diagnosis of spondylosis, which is a form of degenerative change.  The government conceded to this assertion as a fact that was not in dispute.

The Federal Circuit concluded that since the Board expressly found that the veteran was diagnosed with spondylosis, and the government conceded that spondylosis is a degenerative condition, the only way the 1954 RO could have found against the veteran was if it concluded that the legal definition of “defect” under the 1946 VA Rating Schedule included degenerative conditions, which would be an error as a matter of law.

The Federal Circuit noted that VA General Counsel Precedent Opinion 82-90 addressed the term “defect” under 38 C.F.R. 3.303(c) and expressed that “unlike a disease, a defect is incapable of any improvement or deterioration.”  The Federal Circuit afforded deference to this interpretation of the regulation, and held it to be reasonable.

The Federal Circuit stated that it was not retroactively applying this law, but was interpreting the 1946 regulation in effect at the time.  The Federal Circuit concluded that “defect” is properly construed as excluding degenerative conditions, and did not find the government to contend otherwise.

The Federal Circuit stated that given the proper legal interpretation of defect in the regulation in effect in 1946, and the concessions made by the government regarding the facts of the case, the RO could not, without error, have determined that spondylosis was a “defect.”  Therefore, the Federal Circuit concluded that as a matter of law that the RO’s 1954 decision contained CUE and the veteran is entitled to an earlier effective date for his benefits.

The Federal Circuit reversed the Veterans Court decision and remanded for entry of judgment consistent with its opinion.
