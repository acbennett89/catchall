Decision Assessment Document
O' Brien v. Wilkie, May 4, 2018, 30 Vet.App. 21
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the meaning of dependents under 38 U.S.C. 1115 includes children only as defined under 38 U.S.C. 101(4)(A) and does not encompass any child who lives with and is actually dependent on the veteran.  Appointment as guardian over a minor grandchild would not qualify as a dependent under section 1115.

Section 101(4)(A) defines a “child” for VA purposes as “a person who is unmarried and (1) under the age of 18 years; (2) became permanently incapable of self-support before attaining the age of 18 years; or (3) after attaining the age of 18 years (but not after the age of 23 years) is pursuing a course of instruction at an approved educational institution; and who is a legitimate child, a legal adopted child, or a stepchild who is a member of a veteran’s household or was a member at the time of the veteran’s death.”

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran is presently rated 100 percent disabling for compensation purposes and has been in receipt of at least a 70 percent evaluation at all times during the appeal.

In September 2012, a state court appointed the veteran and his wife legal guardians of their then-minor grandson.  The appointment authorized the veteran and his wife to have custody of the grandson and to “do all acts required of the guardian by law.”

In November 2012, the veteran submitted VA Form 21-686c, Declaration of Status of Dependents, listing his grandson as his stepchild.  The regional office (RO), in January 2014, denied additional compensation for the veteran’s grandson as a dependent.  The RO explained that it could not pay the additional compensation for a grandchild that has not been legally adopted, as VA only recognizes biological children, stepchildren, or adopted children.  The veteran appealed the decision.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in May 2016, denied the appeal, finding that, as a matter of law, the veteran was not entitled to a dependency allowance because the grandson is not a dependent child for VA purposes.  The Board found that, because there was no dispute that the veteran had not adopted the grandson, it was bound by law and without authority to grant benefits.

The veteran argued before the Court that VA has not defined “dependents” and therefore the Court should rely on the plain meaning of the word.  He further asserted that the plain meaning includes his grandson who has been “actually dependent” on him and his wife since he was an infant and became their legal charge in September 2012.  The veteran also argued that the definition of “dependents” relied upon by the Board requiring that a particular family unit be in place before benefits may be awarded violates his and his grandson’s First Amendment “right of intimate association.”  Specifically, he contended that a government benefit may not be conditioned in a way that penalizes the exercise of a fundamental right, such as the right of family association.

The Court observed that although “dependents” was neither specifically defined in section 1115 or its implementing regulation (38 C.F.R. § 3.4(b)(2)), nor included in the list of statutory definitions that govern VA (generally found under 38 U.S.C. § 101), the structure of section 1115 makes clear that Congress specifically and unambiguously answered the question, as it plainly limited the term to spouses, children, and dependent parents.  Thus, the Court concluded that since the statute expressly identified who is a “dependent” for its purposes, the Court may not accept the veteran’s invitation to adopt the ordinary dictionary meaning of that term.

The Court, turning to the relevant issue on appeal, found that Congress had limited the term “child” to a minor “who is a legitimate child, a legally adopted child, or a stepchild who is a member of a veteran’s household or was a member at the time of veteran’s death” under section 101(4)(A).  The Court noted that the Board had found, and the parties did not dispute, that the veteran was appointed the legal guardian of his grandson, but did not adopt him.  Thus, the Court concluded that the Board’s determination that the veteran was not entitled to a dependency allowance for his grandson as a matter of law was correct.

The Court, in addressing the First Amendment issue raised by the veteran, characterized his argument as requesting the Court to apply the doctrine of “constitutional avoidance,” which provides that where a statute is susceptible of two constructions, by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise and by the other of which such questions are avoid, a court’s duty is to avoid the former and adopt the latter.

The Court rejected the argument, as it determined that the plain language of section 1115 was unambiguous; therefore, it need not apply the doctrine of constitutional avoidance.  The Court did not further address the matter in view of the absence of an express constitutional challenge to section 1115.

The Court affirmed the Board’s decision.
