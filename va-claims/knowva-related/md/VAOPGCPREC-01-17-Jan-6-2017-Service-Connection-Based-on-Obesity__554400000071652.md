Decision Assessment Document
VAOPGCPREC 01-17, Jan 6, 2017, Service Connection Based on Obesity
Office of General Counsel Precedent Opinion

**What the opinion is about:**

General Counsel held that obesity is not a disease or injury for the purposes of 38 U.S.C. 1110 and 1131; therefore, it may not be service connected on a direct basis.  Since it is not a disease under sections 1110 and 1131, VA is not required to consider whether it resulted from willful misconduct for the purpose of line-of-duty determinations under 38 U.S.C. 105(a).

Obesity cannot qualify as an in-service event that can cause a disability because it occurs over time and is based on various external and internal factors, as opposed to being a discrete incident or occurrence, or a series of discrete incidents or occurrences.

General Counsel held that obesity per se is not a “disability” for purposes of 38 C.F.R. 3.310.  However, if, in a particular case, obesity resulting from a service-connected disease or injury is found to produce impairment beyond that contemplated by the VA rating schedule, an extra-schedular rating under 38 C.F.R. 3.321(b)(1) for the service-connected disability may be considered.  Obesity would not receive a separate extra-schedular rating.

Although General Counsel held that obesity is not a “disability” under 3.310, it may be an “intermediate step” between a service-connected disability and a current disability that may satisfy the “proximate cause requirement” under 38 C.F.R. 3.310(a) to service connect the current disability on a secondary basis.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Manual M21-1, IV.ii.2.B, will be revised in accordance with the precedent opinion.

**Summary of the facts and findings:**

General Counsel addressed this issue at the request of the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) following a joint motion for remand approved by the Veterans Court that required the Board to address whether obesity is a disability that is compensable as secondary to a service-connected condition under 3.310(a) and whether it is properly considered as a disease or defect.

 General Counsel acknowledged that several organizations, including the American Medical Association (AMA) and Federal agencies have stated that obesity is a disease.  However, such findings do not compel VA to make the same finding and General Counsel observed that these statements were made for a variety of purposes other than disability compensation, such as to advance obesity treatment and prevention.  General Counsel also noted that AMA’s determination was contrary to the recommendation of councils within the organization.

 General Counsel indicated that the question before VA of whether obesity should be considered a disease involves exercise of VA’s gap-filling authority under 38 U.S.C. 501, which may consider factual and policy concerns, such as whether there is general consensus that obesity is a disease or continued divergence of opinion.

General Counsel acknowledged that while VA regulations recognize that the rating schedule is not exhaustive,[1] it interpreted the omission of obesity in the VA rating schedule to reflect VA’s considered judgment that the condition is not a disease or injury for the purposes of sections 1110 and 1131.  Since obesity is a well-known and wide-spread condition, General Counsel determined that if VA had intended to consider obesity as a disease, it would almost have certainly included provision in its rating schedule.  It was also noted that the analysis of the AMA councils showed substantial bases for concluding that the obesity does not satisfy the elements for definition of a disease under medical authorities.

Accordingly, General Counsel concluded that VA’s longstanding policy that obesity per se is not a disease for purposes of establishing service connection under sections 1110 and 1131 to be consistent with applicable statutes and General Counsel Precedent opinions, and to be supported by numerous scientific authorities.  General Counsel applied this conclusion in holding that obesity cannot be service connected on a secondary basis under 3.310(a) and (b), as it is not a disease or injury within the meaning of those regulations.

 Although not a disease or injury, General Counsel concluded that it does not preclude a finding, in a particular case, that obesity resulting from a service-connected disease or injury contributes to disability and should be considered in assigning a disability evaluation for the service-connected disability.  If appropriate, entitlement to an extra-schedular evaluation under 38 C.F.R. 3.321(b)(1) for the service-connected disability may be considered.

In determining that obesity cannot qualify as an in-service event that can cause a disability, General Counsel relied upon VA’s definition of an event under 38 C.F.R. 3.159(a)(4) as “one or more incidents associated with places, types, and circumstances of service giving rise to disability.”  The definition was derived from 38 U.S.C. 1154(a).[2]  General Counsel concluded that obesity did not qualify as in-service event, as contemplated in the aforementioned regulation and statute, because it is not a discrete incident or occurrence associated with the places, types, and circumstances of service.  Rather, it occurs over time and is based on various external and internal factors and processes, many of which cannot be considered a discrete event.

Lastly, General Counsel held that obesity could qualify as an “intermediate step” between a service-connected disability and a current disability on the basis of being the “proximate cause” under 3.310(a).

General Counsel, to illustrate the application, provided the example of whether a veteran could establish service connection for hypertension if a service-connected back disability causes obesity due to lack of exercise, which leads to hypertension.  In such a scenario, adjudicators would have to resolve the following issues:  (1) whether the service-connected back disability caused the veteran to become obese; (2) if so, whether the obesity as a result of the service-connected back disability was a substantial factor in causing hypertension; and (3) whether the hypertension would not have occurred but for obesity caused by the service-connected back disability.  General Counsel instructed that if all of the aforementioned issues are answered affirmatively, service connection could be established for hypertension on a secondary basis.
