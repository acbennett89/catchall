Decision Assessment Document
Hansen-Sorensen v. Wilkie, Dec 11, 2018, 909 F.3d 1379
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that the C-123 Rule (38 C.F.R. 3.307(a)(6)(v)) and Camp Lejeune Rule (38 C.F.R. 3.307(a)(7)) did not modify the controlling interpretation of the amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) Rule, 38 C.F.R. 3.318(a), under Bowers v. Shinseki that active duty for training (ADT) does not qualify as “active duty” for the purpose of establishing entitlement to service connection for ALS on a presumptive basis under 3.318.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The appellant’s husband, Curtis Hansen, served in the National Guard for six years, which included a period of 182 days of active duty for training at inception of his military service.  He died from ALS in 1998.  In May 2009, his widow applied for dependency and indemnity compensation (DIC) benefits.

The regional office denied the claim and the Veterans Court agreed with the Board of Veterans’ Appeals that Mr. Hansen’s ADT service did not qualify as active service and denied the appeal.

VA, in September 2008, amended its regulations under 3.318(a) to provide that the “development of [ALS] manifested at any time after discharge or release from service from active military, naval, or air service is sufficient to establish service connection for that disease.”

The Federal Circuit held in Bowers in 2014 that the presumption of service connection for ALS under 3.318 only applied to individuals that attained veteran status by performing “active military, naval, or air service” as defined under 38 U.S.C. 101(24).  The Federal Circuit held that “active duty for training” does not come within “active duty,” to include individuals who only served in the National Guard, under section 101(24), and would not be entitled to the 3.318 presumption, as they had not performed “active military, naval, or air service.”

The appellant argued before the Federal Circuit that Bowers had been superseded by the Secretary’s adoption of the C-123 and Camp Lejeune Rules.  The C-123 Rule provides presumptive service connection for Agent Orange-related diseases to a certain class of covered individuals that includes, under specified circumstances, reservists who had worked with a C-123 aircraft that had been used to spray herbicides.  The Camp Lejeune Rule provides for presumptive service connection for certain diseases for individuals, to include reservists or National Guardsman, who served on Camp Lejeune during the nearly 35 years in which its water was contaminated.  VA had designated the Agent Orange and toxic water exposures as injuries under 38 U.S.C. 101(24)(B) and (C) for the purpose of establishing that the individual served on “active military, naval, or air service.”

The Federal Circuit rejected the appellant’s contention that the C-123 and Camp Lejeune Rules modified the Bowers statutory interpretation of the phrase “active military, naval, or air service” or the application of the phrase in ALS regulation under 3.318.  The Federal Circuit explained that these two rules establish that the “incurred or aggravated in line of duty” and “during which the individual concerned was disabled” requirements of sections 101(24)(B) and (C) are met in specified circumstances.  Those circumstances involve individuals who come down with specified diseases and had specified experiences (with C-123 aircraft or at Camp Lejeune) representing risks of harm from specified chemical agents (herbicides or water contaminants).  The Federal Circuit noted that the Secretary made empirical judgments about those circumstances, which included a National Academy of Science Institute of Medicine study and other studies.

The Federal Circuit concluded that it was undisputed that the present case does not come within the circumstances covered by the C-123 or Camp Lejeune Rules.  For the situation covered by the ALS Rule, at issue in this case, the Secretary did not make the same kind of empirical judgment or adopted the same kind of shortcut to make the showing required by section 101(24)(B) for a person with “active duty for training” but not “active duty.”  Therefore, the two post-Bowers rules did not involve a new legal interpretation, or a factual or policy determination that applies to the present cases and removes from the controlling authority of Bowers.

The Federal Circuit also rejected the appellant’s argument that the Secretary’s different regulatory treatment of the ALS situation apart from the Camp Lejeune and C-123 situations is arbitrary and capricious.  The Federal Circuit concluded that the exposures underlying the C-123 and Camp Lejeune Rules do not underlie the ALS rule, which relies on different empirical studies that the Secretary did not treat as tying ALS to any specific harm-causing chemical agent, use of specific equipment, or period of time at a specific location.  The Federal Circuit found no basis for concluding that the Secretary cannot reasonably distinguish the ALS situation from the C-123 and Camp Lejeune situations in making judgments about whether to declare categorically or to presume that specific diseases or injuries were “incurred or aggravated in line of duty,” or that certain individuals became disabled during specified periods, under sections 101(24)(B) and (C).

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Veterans Court decision.
