Decision Assessment Document
 Comer v. Peake, Jan 16, 2009, 552 F.3d 1362
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that a claim for a total disability evaluation due to individual unemployability (TDIU) is implicitly raised whenever a pro se (unrepresented) veteran, who presents cogent evidence of unemployability, seeks to obtain a higher disability rating, regardless of whether the veteran specifically states that he is seeking TDIU benefits.

The Federal Circuit also held that the VA duty to read an appeal submission sympathetically to ascertain all potential claims it raises is antecedent to its duty to ensure that all issues have been properly raised on appeal.

The Federal Circuit also held that the limited assistance the veteran received from a veterans’ service organization (VSO) in appealing a determination of the VA to the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) was insufficient to disqualify the veteran as a pro se claimant, and therefore the veteran still had the right to have his claims read sympathetically by the Board.  The assistance provided by the VSO was not equivalent to legal representation.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact requiring regulatory revision or Manual change.  VBA policy is to consider *all* claims sympathetically, irrespective of whether the veteran is unrepresented veterans or represented by a veterans’ service organization (VSO) or private attorney.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The regional office (RO) denied the veteran’s claim for service connection for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in 1988.  He filed to reopen in February 1999.  Following a remand by the Board, the RO granted service connection for PTSD with a 30 percent evaluation effective February 26, 1999.

The veteran filed a Notice of Disagreement (NOD) concerning the evaluation and argued for an earlier effective date to 1988.  The RO granted a 50 percent evaluation for PTSD; however, it did not revise the effective date.  He filed his appeal to the Board in June 2003.  At this time, he was represented by a VSO, which submitted a statement on his behalf (this was the initial appearance of a representative on the veteran’s behalf).

While the appeal was pending, the veteran underwent a VA examination on May 5, 2004.  The examiner diagnosed PTSD and noted that the veteran had “no stable job” since his last examination.  The RO subsequently increased the evaluation for PTSD to 70 percent and granted TDIU, both effective May 5, 2004.

The Board denied the veteran’s appeal for an increased evaluation from February 1999 to May 2004 and an earlier effective date for service connection prior to February 1999.  The Board stated that it could not award benefits retroactive to the initial claim for benefits because the veteran had not specifically alleged CUE in the 1988 rating decision.

The Veterans Court held that it had no jurisdiction to consider whether TDIU benefits should have been granted prior to May 2004, because the veteran had not specifically raised the issue in his NOD.  The Veterans Court also rejected the veteran’s argument that VA failed to comply with its notice obligations under [38 U.S.C. § 5103(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103), concluding that VA had provided notice of what evidence VA was responsible for obtaining and what the veteran could do to assist in the process.

The veteran argued before the Federal Circuit that the Veterans Court misconstrued [*Roberson v. Principi*, 251 F.3d 1378 (Fed. Cir. 2001)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/251/251.F3d.1378.00-7009.html) when it held that it had no jurisdiction to consider the issue of an earlier effective date for TDIU benefits because he had not explicitly raised the issue before the Board.  The Federal Circuit agreed, holding that *Roberson* requires the Board to consider whether a TDIU award is warranted whenever a pro se clamant seeks a higher disability rating and submits cogent evidence of unemployability, regardless of whether he states specifically that he is seeking TDIU benefits.

The Federal Circuit stated that although the veteran did not explicitly assert the right to a TDIU award for the period from February 1999 to May 2004, his pro se appeal for an earlier effective date and a higher disability rating for PTSD benefits was sufficient, when coupled with the evidence showing his unemployability, to raise the issue of entitlement to an earlier effective date for his TDIU award.  The Federal Circuit added that although *Roberson* involved an initial claim submitted to an RO, there is no reason that it should not apply with equal force to a NOD submitted after an RO’s decision.

The Federal Circuit observed that while a veteran is obligated to raise an issue in a NOD if he wishes to preserve the issue on appeal, determining whether an issue has been properly raised must be made with due regard for the VA’s duty to read a veteran’s submissions sympathetically.  This duty is necessary because a pro se veteran may lack a complete understanding of the various forms of VA disability benefits and VA’s occasional use of arcane terminology.  The Federal Circuit noted that the need for such assistance is particularly acute where, as in the present case, a veteran is afflicted with a significant psychological disability at the time he files an appeal.

The Federal Circuit rejected the government’s argument that the Board had no duty to construe the veteran’s appeal sympathetically because had assistance from an aide from a VSO.  The Federal Circuit held that the assistance provided by the VSO aide was not the equivalent of legal representation.  Even if the veteran had received more significant assistance from the VSO, representation from an organizational aide is not equivalent to representation by a licensed attorney.

The veteran also argued that VA had an obligation to inform him that he was required to file a CUE motion if he wanted to obtain benefits retroactive to the date of his initial claim.  Although indicating that it was troubling that VA never informed the veteran of the need to file such a motion, the Federal Circuit declined to resolve the issue of whether VA had a duty, under section 5103(a), to notify the veteran that he could only obtain retroactive benefits by filing a CUE motion.  The Federal Circuit explained that even if it did conclude that VA had such a duty, failure to fulfill it does not rise to a reversible error, as the veteran maintains the right to file a CUE motion, and if successful, could still obtain disability benefits retroactive to the date of the initial claim in 1988.

The Federal Circuit reversed the Veterans Court decision and remanded the case for the Veterans Court to determine whether the veteran has raised a valid CUE claim before the Veterans Court.
