**Decision Assessment Document**

***Labruzza and McBride v. McDonough*, 37 Vet.App. 111 (202****4)**

**Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)**

**What this case is about:** The Court held that, for purposes of total disability based on individual unemployability (TDIU) benefits, employment in a protected environment means employment in a lower-income position that, due to the Veteran's service-connected disability or disabilities, is shielded in some respect from competition in the employment market

**Impact on VBA:** The M21-1 Adjudication Procedures Manual will be updated at VIII.iv.3.A.2. to incorporate the holding of this case.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:** In separate decisions that the Court consolidated, the Board of Veterans Appeals (Board) denied Veterans LaBruzza and McBride entitlement to TDIU for periods when they were employed, finding that their respective occupations were substantially gainful. Although the Board concluded that neither Veteran was engaged in “employment in a protected environment,” the Board defined that phrase differently in each case.

Mr. LaBruzza served on active duty in the U.S. Navy from August 1967 to July 1971 and is service connected for PTSD, residuals of prostate cancer, tinnitus, inactive chronic duodenal ulcer, erectile dysfunction, and hearing loss, and was receiving TDIU effective July 1, 2018, the first day of the month after he retired, Prior to retiring LaBruzza was employed in a municipal building inspector's office from August 1999 to June 2018 and during his last full year of employment in 2017, he earned $198,147. LaBruzza stated that his employer gave him “special treatment” because he was a Veteran and that others had told him that, in a typical work environment, his behavior would have cost him his job. A private psychologist in February 2018 and a private vocational counselor in April 2020 both opined that he had been incapable of performing nonsheltered, substantially gainful employment for many years. In the March 2021 decision on appeal, the Board denied LaBruzza entitlement to TDIU prior to July 1, 2018, because it found that he was engaged in substantially gainful employment at that time.

Mr. McBride served on active duty in the U.S. Navy from March 1984 to May 1986, is service connected for anxiety disorder and hydrocephalus, and is receiving TDIU effective November 1, 2013. He was unable to find full-time employment since his previous employer closed in November 2013. When employed, his service-connected disabilities caused considerable occupational impairment. His employer overlooked mistakes and assigned others to finish his jobs. In September 2020 the Board issued a decision denying entitlement to TDIU prior to November 1, 2013. The Board found, in relevant part, that McBride's prior employment was not protected because he was retained and provided accommodations based on his value to the company rather than for altruistic or personal reasons, his disabilities did not result in lost income, and legally required accommodations permitted him to maintain substantially gainful employment.

The Board denied LaBruzza entitlement to TDIU prior to July 1, 2018, because it found that he was engaged in substantially gainful employment at that time. As relevant here, the Board determined that the LaBruzza’s employment with the building inspector's office from August 1999 to June 2018 was not “in a protected environment” because that employment did not result in lost income, legally required accommodations allowed him to remain employed for many years, and there was no evidence that he was hired for a charitable, rehabilitative, or therapeutic purpose.

The Board denied McBride’s entitlement to TDIU prior to November 1, 2013, because it found, in relevant part, that his prior employment was not protected because he was retained and provided accommodations based on his value to the company rather than for altruistic or personal reasons, his disabilities did not result in lost income, and legally required accommodations permitted him to maintain substantially gainful employment. The Board dismissed “[t]he fact that [McBride]'s employer may have made additional accommodations above and beyond what they are required to do by law” because, per McBride's testimony, “these extra accommodations were granted due to his value as an employee.”

LaBruzza and McBride timely appealed their respective Board decisions, and their appeals were consolidated and referred to a panel of the Court to address the meaning of the phrase “employment in a protected environment” in 38 C.F.R. [§ 4.16(a)](https://www.westlaw.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=L&pubNum=1000547&cite=38CFRS4.16&originatingDoc=I3f1be2c0bb1a11eeaece92e459f12ff5&refType=RB&originationContext=document&vr=3.0&rs=cblt1.0&transitionType=DocumentItem&contextData=(sc.History*oc.UserEnteredCitation)#co_pp_8b3b0000958a4). In its analysis the Court first noted that although it had previously determined that the phrase was ambiguous (in *Cantrell v. Shulkin*, 28 Vet.App. 382, 2017), an intervening Supreme Court decision clarified the framework that courts should use when interpreting undefined regulatory terms. The Court therefore concluded that to conform with controlling Supreme Court precedent it needed to revisit the ambiguity question.

The Court then analyzed the text, structure and history of the regulation and noted that [§ 4.16(a)](https://www.westlaw.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=L&pubNum=1000547&cite=38CFRS4.16&originatingDoc=I3f1be2c0bb1a11eeaece92e459f12ff5&refType=RB&originationContext=document&vr=3.0&rs=cblt1.0&transitionType=DocumentItem&contextData=(sc.History*oc.UserEnteredCitation)#co_pp_8b3b0000958a4) contains two examples (family businesses and sheltered workshops) of protected-environment employment that assist with discerning the phrase's meaning. The examples share two common characteristics that bring “employment in a protected environment” into focus. The first common characteristic of the examples is that family businesses and sheltered workshops offer employment that is insulated, to a degree, from the open labor market. A second common characteristic is that employment in a family business or sheltered workshop is based on something other than the strict qualifications of the employee. The Court found that together these unifying characteristics point toward employment that is excepted, in some respect, from the competitive labor market.

By examining the text, structure, history, and purpose of [§ 4.16(a)](https://www.westlaw.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=L&pubNum=1000547&cite=38CFRS4.16&originatingDoc=I3f1be2c0bb1a11eeaece92e459f12ff5&refType=RB&originationContext=document&vr=3.0&rs=cblt1.0&transitionType=DocumentItem&contextData=(sc.History*oc.UserEnteredCitation)#co_pp_8b3b0000958a4), the Court found that the meaning of “employment in a protected environment” becomes clear. The Court specified the phrase unambiguously means employment in a lower-income position that, due to a Veteran's service-connected disability or disabilities, is shielded in some respect from competition in the employment market.

The Court noted that, as a general matter, receipt of an Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) accommodation is not by itself evidence that a Veteran is working in a protected environment, but a Veteran receiving accommodations beyond those legally required by the ADA is more likely to be able to demonstrate that he or she is employed in a protected environment. Conversely, a working Veteran who does not require any ADA-mandated accommodation to work is less likely to be able to show that his or her employment is in a protected environment. Given the fact-specific inquiry required to determine whether a Veteran's employment is in a protected environment the Court reiterated that these generalities are not categorical or dispositive. While they will guide most cases, consideration must be given to any individualized factor or evidence that tends to clarify whether a specific position was shielded in some respect from competition in the employment market.

The income that a Veteran earns from employment must be relatively low to qualify as “in a protected environment” because [§ 4.16(a)](https://www.westlaw.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=L&pubNum=1000547&cite=38CFRS4.16&originatingDoc=I3f1be2c0bb1a11eeaece92e459f12ff5&refType=RB&originationContext=document&vr=3.0&rs=cblt1.0&transitionType=DocumentItem&contextData=(sc.History*oc.UserEnteredCitation)#co_pp_8b3b0000958a4) designates earned annual income as the primary touchstone for determining whether employment is marginal, and caselaw has long highlighted the importance of a Veteran's income in distinguishing between substantially gainful and marginal employment. Given the central role that earned annual income plays in the marginal employment assessment, and in the TDIU determination itself, a Veteran's earned annual income must factor into the protected-environment inquiry. Otherwise, if income played no role, the protected-environment exception could swallow the general rule. Hence, for the purposes of ascertaining the meaning of employment in a protected environment, the Court concluded that the phrase limits such employment to lower-income positions that produce earned annual income that exceeds the poverty threshold. The extent to which a Veteran's earned annual income exceeds the poverty threshold is clearly a key regulatory consideration.

A Veteran's unique circumstances should be considered in determining “employment in a protected environment.” Context is critical, and no fact or factor should be dispositive. Employment is not “in a protected environment” simply because a Veteran receives workplace accommodations for service-connected disabilities or remains employed despite being occupationally impaired. A further showing is required to establish that the Veteran's position is in fact shielded from competition in the employment market. And entitlement to protected-environment TDIU, like all other types of TDIU, ultimately depends on whether a Veteran's service-connected disabilities render the Veteran incapable of performing the mental and physical acts required for substantially gainful employment.

Ultimately, the Court held that the phrase “in a protected environment” in [§ 4.16(a)](https://www.westlaw.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=L&pubNum=1000547&cite=38CFRS4.16&originatingDoc=I3f1be2c0bb1a11eeaece92e459f12ff5&refType=RB&originationContext=document&vr=3.0&rs=cblt1.0&transitionType=DocumentItem&contextData=(sc.History*oc.UserEnteredCitation)#co_pp_8b3b0000958a4) is unambiguous and means a lower-income position that, due to the Veteran's service-connected disability or disabilities, is shielded in some respect from competition in the employment market. Because the Board did not apply this definition to either Labruzza’s or McBride’s case or make the necessary factual findings to do so, the Court set aside the appealed portions of their Board decisions and remanded the matters for further development and readjudication consistent with its decision.
