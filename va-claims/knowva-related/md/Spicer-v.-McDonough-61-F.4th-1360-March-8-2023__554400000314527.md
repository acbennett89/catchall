**Decision Assessment Document**

***Spicer v. McDonough*, 61 F.4th 1360 (March 8, 2023)**

**U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (Federal Circuit)**

**What the case is about:** The Veteran, Mr. Spicer, sought service connection for a bilateral knee disability secondary to service-connected leukemia. He based his claim on the theory that his leukemia treatment prevented him from undergoing knee surgery to treat his arthritis. The VA Regional Office (RO) denied the claim and the Board of Veterans Appeals (Board) upheld the denial on appeal. The Veterans Court of Appeals (Court) affirmed the Board’s decision.

On appeal, the Federal Circuit held that 38 U.S.C § 1110 requires a “but-for” causation standard. Therefore, the statute provides for compensation for worsening of functionality, whether through an inability to treat or a more direct, etiological cause. The Federal Circuit also determined that to the extent VA applied 38 C.F.R. § 3.310(b) to reject Mr. Spicer’s theory of compensation, the regulation is unlawful as inconsistent with 38 U.S.C. § 1110.

**Impact on VBA:** The M21-1 will be updated and implementing guidance will be issued to field offices.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:** Prior to 2017, Mr. Spicer was granted service-connection for chronic myeloid leukemia. Separately, he developed arthritis in both knees and was scheduled for knee replacement surgery. The surgery was cancelled because his leukemia treatment reduced his red blood cell level to a level that precluded surgery.

In February of 2017, Mr. Spicer claimed secondary service connection for his bilateral knee condition. The RO denied the claim, finding no link between the knee disability and his service-connected leukemia. Mr. Spicer appealed to the Board, and in August of 2018, the Board affirmed the RO’s denial. Next, he appealed to the Court, which affirmed the Board’s decision. Mr. Spicer then appealed to the Federal Circuit.

The issue at the Federal Circuit was whether the but-for causation requirement is limited “to bringing something about or the onset or etiological link”, or whether the but-for causation requirement includes “situations where the service-connected disease or injury impedes treatment of a disability.”

Section 1110 provides compensation to a Veteran “[f]or disability resulting from personal injury suffered or disease contracted in the line of duty.”

The Federal Circuit noted that “resulting from” in § 1110 requires but-for causation; therefore, § 1110 “plainly requires compensation when a service-connected disease or injury is a but-for cause of a present-day disability.” The Federal Circuit further stated that the language of § 1110 applies “to the natural progression of a condition not caused by a service-connected injury or disease, but that nonetheless would have been less severe were it not for the service-connected disability.” In other words, “§ 1110 provides for compensation for a worsening of functionality— whether through an inability to treat or a more direct, etiological cause.”

Furthermore, the Federal Circuit explained the but-for causation standard is not limited to a single cause and effect. It can include multi-causal links. The Federal Circuit provided examples of but-for causation, such as the failure to shovel snow can be a but-for cause of someone falling, or failure to signal a turn at an intersection can be a but-for cause of a collision.

VA argued that it could not properly compensate Mr. Spicer’s knee condition. According to VA, it would be too speculative to assess the difference between his current condition and what it would have been post-surgery. The Federal Circuit was not persuaded by VA’s position. In part, the Federal Circuit reasoned “some speculation is naturally baked into but-for-causation.”

The Federal Circuit noted that its holding was based on 38 U.S.C. § 1110 alone. The Federal Circuit stated that to the extent VA applied 38 C.F.R. § 3.310(b) to reject Mr. Spicer’s theory of compensation, the regulation is unlawful as inconsistent with 38 U.S.C. § 1110.

The Federal Circuit vacated the Court’s decision and remanded the case for further proceedings consistent its opinion.
