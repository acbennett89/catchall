Decision Assessment Document

Hatfield v. McDonough, March 8, 2021, 33 Vet.App. 327

United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held the rule articulated in McNair v. Shinseki, 25 Vet. App. 98 (2011) (deviations from the informed consent requirements of 38 C.F.R. § 17.32 are minor and immaterial if a reasonable person in similar circumstances would have proceeded with the medical treatment even if informed of the foreseeable risk) applies only when VA has attempted to obtain informed consent but the consent is in some way defective.

The Court determined that McNair is not applicable where VA does not obtain consent at all. The Court reversed the Board's decision denying appellant entitlement to benefits under 38 U.S.C. § 1151 and remanded the matter for the assignment of an effective date for those benefits and for any other actions necessary to effectuate the award of benefits.

**Impact on VBA:**

M21-1, Part IV, Subpart ii, Chapter 2, Section G will be updated to reference the holding of this case.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons**:

The Veteran served in the Army during World War II and developed Hodgkin's disease after service. In January 1979, he died from pulmonary complications of radiation therapy he underwent at a VA medical center that was treating his Hodgkin's lymphoma. His widow appealed a Board of Veterans' Appeals (Board) decision that denied entitlement to dependency and indemnity compensation (DIC) benefits for the Veteran's death under 38 U.S.C. § 1151. While it was argued that no reasonable person in the Veteran's position would have foregone radiation therapy to treat Hodgkin's disease, in this case the record contained no evidence documenting that the Veteran provided informed consent for radiation therapy.

The case had a long procedural history including numerous Board of Veterans Appeals Decisions and two Court remands prior to the instant Court decision. In this appearance before the Court, the Veteran’s widow raised several arguments asserting error in the Board decision on appeal. As relevant here she argued the Board erred in determining that informed consent was obtained because it misapplied 38 C.F.R. §§ 17.32 and 3.361, as well as the Court’s decision in McNair. The Veteran’s widow asserted that VA obtained no informed consent and as a matter of law such lack of consent satisfies the proximate causation requirement of § 3.361(d)(1)(ii). She contended the Board erred when it used McNair to establish consent instead of to cure consent that was otherwise defective. The Board had determined that informed consent could be established if a reasonable person in circumstances similar to those of the Veteran (i.e., the patient) would not have refused the treatment even if informed of the risk of the complication the Veteran experienced.

The Court addressed the widow’s claim for compensation under section 1151 based on VA's failure to obtain the Veteran's informed consent concerning the risks associated with the radiation treatment used to treat his Hodgkin's disease. The Court explained why the Board erred in denying entitlement to 1151 benefits by misapplying the rule articulated in McNair. Once that legal error was removed from the equation, the Court explained why the Board's factual findings led to reversal and an order to award the benefits the widow sought.

Considering §§ 3.361(d)(1)(ii) and 17.32 in conjunction with the ruling in McNair, the Court held that the minor-deviation exception provided in § 3.361(d)(1)(ii) applies only when there has been a predicate finding of informed consent that is in substantial compliance with § 17.32. The McNair rule does not apply to situations where no informed consent was obtained or attempted.

In this case there was no dispute that the Veteran developed pneumonitis as an additional disability resulting from VA's radiation treatment (and not from his own willful misconduct). Hence, the Court found the first element (a Veteran must experience a qualifying additional disability or death that was not the result of the Veteran's willful misconduct) for establishing entitlement to benefits under §1151 was satisfied. Likewise, there was no dispute that the Veteran's pneumonitis and resulting death were actually caused by radiation treatment administered by VA, so the second element (the additional disability or death must have been caused by VA medical treatment, care, or examination) for establishing entitlement to benefits under §1151 was established as well.

The disputed issue in this case was proximate causation. The Court engaged in statutory and regulatory interpretation and construction and determined that understanding the structure and content of § 3.361 was critical to resolving the appeal. The Court noted that §3.361(d) sets forth the three scenarios in which Veterans may establish proximate causation with regard to entitlement to 1151 benefits. The Court found the first paragraph of § 3.361(d)(1) essentially mimics 38 U.S.C. § 1151(a)(1)(A), and provides that proximate causation can be established with a showing of “carelessness, negligence, lack of proper skill, error in judgment, or similar instance of fault on VA's part in furnishing hospital care, medical or surgical treatment, or examination caused for the additional disability or death.”

Regarding the concept of informed consent, the Court found its decision Halcomb v. Shinseki, 23 Vet. App. 234 (2009), instructive. In Halcomb the Court noted that under § 3.361(d)(1), “[t]o establish that the proximate cause of a disability was ... carelessness, negligence, lack of proper skill, error in judgment, or similar instance of fault” on the part of VA, the claimant must show either (1) VA failed to exercise the degree of care that would be expected of a reasonable health care provider; or (2) VA furnished the care, treatment, or examination without the veteran's informed consent. Halcomb recognized this regulatory subsection describes two scenarios that count as a “similar instance of fault” not explicitly set forth in § 1151. The plain language of subparagraphs 3.361(d)(1)(i) and (ii) show that proximate causation is established when VA fails to exercise the expected standard of care of a reasonable health care provider or, as relevant in this case, when VA fails to obtain the Veteran's informed consent based on the requirements of § 17.32. The Court recognized that minor deviations from the requirements of § 17.32 that are immaterial under the circumstances of a case will not defeat a finding of informed consent, and that consent may be express (i.e., given orally or in writing) or implied under the circumstances specified § 17.32(b), as in emergency situations.

In this case the Board made a factual finding that no informed consent was provided in connection with the Veteran's radiation treatment. The Court therefore found that there is no way to read the plain language of § 3.361(d)(1)(ii) to mean that the “minor deviation” exception applies when there is no finding of informed consent in the first instance. In other words, in order to consider whether there was a minor deviation from the requirements of § 17.32, there must first be a finding of informed consent to which to compare any alleged minor deviation. Where there is no finding of informed consent, any minor-deviation inquiry is inapplicable.

Ultimately, the Court reversed the Board’s determination that informed consent was obtained in this case. The Court noted that the Board unquestionably found that the first and second requirements for compensation (additional disability or death caused by VA treatment) were met. The Court also concluded the Board found that there was no attempt to obtain consent (as opposed to a situation such as that in McNair, one with defective consent) in this case, and that the lack of documentation of informed consent here underscored VA's failure to even attempt to comply with the requirements of § 17.32. Since the record did not show even a generic informed consent form that could potentially satisfy the basic documentation requirement under the regulations, the Board’s reliance on McNair to find consent was erroneous.

In sum, the Court held that that the minor-deviation exception provided in § 3.361(d)(1)(ii) applies only when there has been a predicate finding of informed consent (as directed by the first sentence in that subparagraph) that is in substantial compliance with § 17.32's requirements. Furthermore, the Court held that the McNair rule does not apply to situations where no informed consent was obtained or attempted.
