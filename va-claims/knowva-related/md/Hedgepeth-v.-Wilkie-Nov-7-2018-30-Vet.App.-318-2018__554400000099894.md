Decision Assessment Document
Hedgepeth v. Wilkie, Nov 7, 2018, 30 Vet.App. 318 (2018)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) erred in reducing a 70 percent evaluation for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) to zero percent on the basis of a change in diagnosis to personality disorder by failing to initiate and conduct severance of service connection proceedings under 38 C.F.R. 3.105(d) before eliminating service-connected VA benefits.

The Court found that the Board had, without going through the proper channels of severance, performed a de facto severance by attempting to apply the regulations governing rating reductions while also finding that the “reduction” is based upon a change in diagnosis rather than material improvement in symptoms.

The Court distinguished its holding from Read v. Shinseki, 651 F. 3d 1296 (Fed. Cir. 2011) in which the Federal Circuit held that the diagnostic change of a gunshot wound disability (GSW) affecting Muscle Group (MG) XIII to MG XV did not constitute a severance of service connection because the diagnosis change did not alter the service-connected status of the veteran’s GSW disability.  In the present case, the diagnosis change from PTSD to personality disorder involved the “service connection” element since the change had the effect of altering the cause of the veteran’s symptoms from PTSD, a disability for which service connection is permitted, to a personality disorder, a disability that cannot be service-connected as a matter of law.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in the M21-1 under III.iv.4.O.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served in the Navy from April 1981 to March 1983, was awarded service connection in March 2010 for PTSD with major depressive disorder with an evaluation of 30 percent, effective April 8, 2005.  The veteran’s stressor was having seen women, children, and babies killed from a warship while off the coast of Lebanon.

The Board, in January 2015, awarded a 70 percent initial rating for PTSD and major depressive disorder and remanded the case to schedule an examination to determine the effects of the veteran’s PTSD and major depressive disorder on his ability to maintain employment.

A June 2015 VA examination diagnosed a personality disorder and attributed the veteran’s anxiety and depression to the personality disorder.  The examiner concluded that the diagnostic criteria for a separate anxiety or depressive disorder were not met.  The regional office (RO) issued a proposal to decrease the 70 percent evaluation to zero percent and scheduled the veteran for another examination.  A different VA examiner from June 2015 diagnosed the veteran with “other specified personality disorder, mixed personality features” and concluded that the veteran did not describe symptoms that met the PTSD diagnostic criteria.  The examiner opined that the veteran experienced occupational and social impairment with deficiencies in most areas and noted that his occupational functioning was seriously impaired by personality disorder traits.  The RO, in January 2016, reduced the veteran’s rating to zero percent, effective April 1, 2016.

The Board, in April 2016, issued a decision awarding total disability evaluation due to individual unemployability (TDIU) from April 2005 to April 2016 and remanding the issue for a determination of whether the veteran met the requirements for extra-schedular TDIU for the period beginning after April 1, 2016.  Compensation Service, in November 2016, denied entitlement to an extra-schedular TDIU evaluation.  The Board, in February 2017, determined that reduction to zero percent for PTSD was proper and denied entitlement to a TDIU evaluation after April 1, 2016.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board failed to properly apply 38 C.F.R. 3.343 and 3.344 when reducing his “protected” disability rating for PTSD from 70 percent to zero percent and discontinuing his TDIU effective from April 1, 2016.  He also contended that the Board erroneously determined that a rating reduction was proper because the reduction amounts to a de facto severance of service connection for PTSD without the procedural protections for severance under 38 U.S.C. § 1159 and 3.105(d).

The Court agreed with the veteran that the Board made its decision without observance of the law and contains clear error.  The Court found that the Board failed to properly apply 3.343 and 3.3444, which, respectively, require VA to find clear and convincing evidence of employability before discontinuing TDIU and “material improvement” of symptoms before reducing a disability rating.  The Board also failed to apply the statutes, regulations, and caselaw governing severance of service connection to the veteran’s case.

The Court rejected the Secretary’s arguments that the applicable laws and regulations provide for an avenue of “reattribution” of symptoms from a service-connected condition to a non-service connected condition in order to reduce or eliminate VA benefits.

The Court noted that the Board discussed the requirements of 3.344 for reducing the disability rating for PTSD from 70 to zero percent; however, the Court found that all the medical evidence the Board cited as supporting the “improvement” of the psychological symptoms clearly showed that the very opposite was true – that the veteran’s symptoms and their frequency and severity had remained unchanged since the 70 percent rating was assigned.  The Court instructed that the Board should have discussed the provisions of 3.344(a) that require it to consider whether a “change in diagnosis represented no more than a progression of an earlier diagnosis, an error in prior diagnosis or possibly a disease entity independent of the service-connected disability” and whether the “new diagnosis” or a personality disorder represented “only temporary remission of a super-imposed psychiatric disease.”

Concerning TDIU, the Court found that the Board failed entirely to consider or apply 3.343, which governs TDIU awards.  The Board also failed to properly frame the issue as “discontinuance of TDIU effective from April 1, 2016,” at it had erroneously listed the issue on appeal as “entitlement” to non-schedular TDIU after April 1, 2016.  The Court described the Board’s framing of the issue as “entitlement to TDIU” rather than “discontinuance of benefits” to wrongly shift the burden of proof from VA to the veteran.  Thus, the Board committed error when it failed to discontinue the TDIU award under the requirements of 3.343.

The Court, in addressing the Board’s error in failing to apply the relevant law governing severance of service connection, concluded that the Board, in finding that the diagnosis accounting for the veteran’s psychological symptoms had changed from PTSD to a personality disorder, had, in effect, determined that it was no longer correct to say that the veteran’s disability had a nexus to his military service because the diagnosis change necessarily indicated that the etiology of the veteran’s symptoms was a personality disorder, which cannot be service connected as a matter of law.

The Court concluded that the Board clearly based the elimination of VA benefits in this case on medical evidence showing a change of diagnosis form PTSD to personality disorder.  Under these circumstances, the applicable law and regulations require VA to initiate and conduct severance of service connection proceedings before eliminating service-connected VA benefits.  The Court held that the Board may not, without going through the proper channels of severance, perform a de facto severance by attempting to apply the regulations governing rating reductions while also finding that the “reduction” is based upon a change of diagnosis rather than material improvement of symptoms.

The Court found that the Board’s determinations reducing the rating for PTSD from 70 to zero percent and discontinuing the TDIU award effective from April 1, 2016, are void ab initio because the Board failed to observe the applicable regulations and improperly shifted the burden of proof to the veteran.  The Court reversed the Board’s decision and remanded the case with instructions to the Board to reinstate the 70 percent rating for PTSD and to reverse the discontinuance of TDIU effective from April 1, 2016.
