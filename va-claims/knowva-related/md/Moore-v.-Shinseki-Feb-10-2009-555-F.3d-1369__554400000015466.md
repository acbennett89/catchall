Decision Assessment Document
 Moore v. Shinseki, Feb 10, 2009, 555 F.3d 1369
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case is about:**

The Federal Circuit held that the Veterans Court erred when it determined that service medical records (SMRs) are not relevant if they pre-date the period for which a veteran seeks disability compensation.

The Federal Circuit held that an integral part of the “duty to assist” under [38 U.S.C. § 5103A](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) is VA’s obligation to obtain all of a veteran’s relevant SMRs before adjudicating a claim for disability compensation.

**Impact on VBA:**

Procedures in the M21-1MR concerning identifying and obtaining in-service mental health treatment records that are not normally associated with the SMRs will be revised to comply with the Federal Circuit’s decision.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran was hospitalized in the psychiatric ward of Tripler Army Medical Center (Tripler) from December 29, 1990, to January 3, 1991, after having made superficial lacerations to his wrists.  He was diagnosed with a severe personality disorder and discharged from service in February 1991.

In September 1992, he filed an initial claim for service connection for a psychiatric disorder and was denied by the regional office (RO).  In 1999, service connection was granted for a psychiatric disorder.  A 10 percent evaluation, effective September 16, 1992, was assigned.  In its decision, the RO noted that the veteran “was reported to have gone ‘berserk’ and to have made superficial lacerations on his wrists.”  However, the RO concluded that a disability rating higher than 10 percent was not warranted because a November 1996 VA examination revealed that the in-service event was a single episode that was now resolved.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in August 2004, increased the evaluation to 30 percent for the period from January 27, 1997, to August 7, 2002, and to 50 percent for the period thereafter; however, the 10 percent rating was maintained for the period from September 16, 1992, to January 26, 1997, as it was concluded that only “mild social and industrial impairment” was shown during that period.

The veteran argued before the Veterans Court that the VA had an affirmative obligation under section 5103A to obtain the Tripler hospitalization medical records prior to making any rating determinations as such records would have given VA a “more complete picture” of the extent of his psychiatric disability.

The Veterans Court rejected this argument, holding that VA was not obligated to obtain the Tripler medical records because even if such records were obtained they would not help his claim.  The Veterans Court noted that the record contained a description of the veteran’s in-service symptoms that was prepared eleven days after he was discharged from Tripler.  Although acknowledging that it did not know the precise content of the Tripler hospitalization records, the Veterans Court concluded that the veteran had failed to establish how the Tripler hospitalization records would be meaningfully different from the records already in VA’s possession.

On appeal, the Federal Circuit agreed with the veteran that the Veterans Court misinterpreted [section 5103A](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) in holding that SMRs are irrelevant if they pre-date the period for which disability compensation is sought.  The Federal Circuit noted that the VA, by regulation, is specifically required to assess a disability “in relation to its history” when making ratings determinations.  See [38 C.F.R. § 4.1](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=bee66191c7a1b57181cbb20027af616e&node=se38.1.4_11&rgn=div8).

The Federal Circuit stated that it was particularly important to evaluate psychiatric disorders in light of its history, as VA regulations specifically provide that a rating for a psychiatric disorder must be “based on all the evidence of record that bears on occupational and social impairment rather than solely on the examiners’ assessment of the level of disability at the moment of the examination.”  See [38 C.F.R. § 4.126(a)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=bee66191c7a1b57181cbb20027af616e&node=se38.1.4_1126&rgn=div8).  The Federal Circuit noted that the veteran sought disability compensation for the very same disability that led to his in-service hospitalization and separation, which occurred less than two years before the period for which he sought disability compensation.

The Federal Circuit rejected the government’s arguments that failure to obtain the Tripler records was harmless error because VA considered other documentation that summarized the Tripler hospitalization and that the veteran would not have obtained a higher disability rating even if VA had obtained and evaluated the Tripler records.

The Federal Circuit reversed the Veterans Court decision and remanded the case for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.
