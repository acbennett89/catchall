Decision Assessment Document
 Trafter v. Shinseki, Apr 29, 2013, 26 Vet.App. 267
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that [38 U.S.C. § 5103A(d)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A), rather than [38 U.S.C. § 5103A(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A), is the correct standard for VA to apply in determining whether to schedule an examination in conjunction with a section 1151 disability compensation claim.

The Court held that the “may be associated” language of section [5103A(d)(2)(B)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) requires that the evidence of record indicate a causal connection between the current disability and VA treatment before VA is required to provide a medical opinion. Note: [38 U.S.C. § 5103A(d)(2)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) provides:  “The Secretary shall treat an examination or opinion as being necessary to make a decision on a claim for purposes of paragraph (1) if the evidence of record before the secretary, taking into consideration all information and lay or medical evidence (including statements of the claimant) – (A) contains competent evidence that claimant has a current disability, or persistent or recurrent symptoms of disability; and (B) indicates that the disability or symptoms may be associated with the claimant’s active military, naval, or air service; but (C) does not contain sufficient medical evidence for the Secretary to make a decision on the claim.”

The Court further held that in keeping with [*McLendon v. Nicholson*, 20 Vet.App. 79 (2006)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014871/) the threshold for providing a VA examination must be low.  VA, when applying section 5103A(d)(2)(B), may not require that the evidence of record prove a nexus between the asserted injury or event and the current disability or competent medical evidence of fault before providing the veteran with a VA examination or opinion.

**Impact on VBA:**

Manual [M21-1MR, Part IV.ii.1.a.3](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014321/) will be amended consistent with the Court’s holding.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran argued two theories under section 1151 before the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board):  (1) VA failed to timely diagnose and treat his breast cancer, which led to development or aggravation of his mental disability; or (2) VA misdiagnosed him as having bipolar disorder, which caused or aggravated his mental disability.

The Board, in its October 2010 decision, cited section [5103A(d)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) and *McLendon* in recognizing that “assistance shall include providing a medical examination or obtaining a medical opinion when such an examination or opinion is necessary to make a decision on the claim.”  However, the Board found that VA was not required to provide an examination or opinion in the case because the record contained no “competent medical evidence that additional disability resulted as a consequence of negligence, careless[ness], or other fault on the part of VA, or an event not reasonably foreseeable,” and that the veteran’s lay testimony did not “satisfy the criteria for obtaining a VA opinion or examination.”

The veteran argued before the Court that VA should have provided him with a medical opinion under section 5103A(d) because both the competent medical evidence and his lay testimony were sufficient to indicate that an additional disability, depression, resulted from VA medical treatment.  Alternatively, he argued that section [5103A(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) should have been applied to his claim because a section 1151 claim does not seek “disability compensation,” as required for the application of section 5103A(d).  Note: [38 U.S.C. § 5103A(a)(1)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) provides:  “The Secretary shall make reasonable efforts to assist a claimant in obtaining evidence necessary to substantiate the claimant’s claim for a benefit under a law administrated by the Secretary.  He alleged that under section 5103A(a), VA should have provided him with a medical opinion because it was necessary to decide his claim as it had a reasonable possibility of substantiating his claim.  He also contended that the Board improperly substituted its own medical judgment in lieu of obtaining a medical opinion, provided an inadequate statement of reasons or bases, and failed to obtain relevant private medical records.

The Court, in reviewing the language and purpose of section 1151, other statutes, and case law concerning section 1151, and noting the placement of section 1151 in a chapter in title 38, U.S. Code, designated “Compensation for Service-Connected Disability or Death,” concluded that section 1151 is to be considered as disability compensation.  Accordingly, the Court rejected the veteran’s appeal to [*Wood v. Peake*, 520 F. 3d 1345, 1349 n. 3 (Fed. Cir. 2008)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014842/) and [*DeLaRosa v. Peake*, 515 F. 3d 1319, 1321-22 (Fed. Cir. 2008)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014835/), as the Court observed that the Federal Circuit discussed only Dependency and Indemnity Compensation (DIC) under Chapter 13 of title 38, rather than section 1151 in those cases.  Note: The Federal Circuit held in *Wood* and *DeLaRosa* that DIC is not a disability compensation claim and that section [5103A(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) applies to “any benefit under a law administered by the Secretary” and encompasses DIC claims, while section [5103A(d)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) only applies to disability compensation claims, which does not include DIC claims.  The Court agreed with the Secretary that a section 1151 claim, as opposed to a DIC claim, is considered a claim for disability compensation.  The Court, in holding that a section 1151 claim is a disability compensation claim, further held that section [5103A(d)(1)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) applies to a section 1151 disability compensation claim, rather than section [5103A(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A).

The Court held that because section 5103A(d)(1) applies to a section 1151 claim and section 5103A(d)(1) cannot be read in isolation, it would interpret the three factors under section [5103A(d)(2)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) broadly to determine when a medical examination or opinion must be provided in a section 1151 compensation claim.  Factor “A” under section 5103A(d)(2) is satisfied if the evidence of record “contains competent evidence that the claimant has a current disability, or persistent or recurrent symptoms of disability.”  [38 U.S.C. § 5103A(d)(2)(A)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A).  The Court found that the “A” factor under section 5103A(d)(2) is to be applied to a section 1151 disability claim in a manner similar to other disability compensation claims.  Thus, if there is no competent evidence of a current disability or persistent or recurrent symptoms of a disability, then a medical examination or opinion is not required.  Similarly, the Court found that factor “C”, which is satisfied when the record before the Secretary “does not contain sufficient medical evidence for the Secretary to make a decision on the claim,” is to be applied no differently than it is applied to a basic disability compensation claim.

Factor “B” of section 5103A(d)(2) is satisfied if the evidence in the record before the Secretary “indicates that the disability or symptoms may be associated with the claimant’s active military, naval, or air service.”  38 U.S.C. § 5103A(d)(2)(B).  In addressing the “B” factor under section 5103A(d)(2), the Court held that the “may be associated language” requires that the record indicate a causal connection between the current disability and VA treatment before VA is required to provide a medical examination or opinion.  The Court also held that, in adherence to *McLendon,* the threshold for the evidence under factor “B” must be equally low in a section 1151 disability compensation claim as it is for a basic disability compensation claim.  Thus, when applying factor B under section 5103A(d)(2) to a section 1151 compensation claim, the Secretary cannot require that the evidence of record be sufficient to *prove* a nexus between the asserted injury or event and the current disability.  Instead, when adjudicating a section 1151 compensation claim, a medical examination or opinion is required when the evidence of record *indicates* that the current disability or symptoms *may* be associated with the hospital care, medical or surgical treatment, or examination furnished the claimant under the law by the Secretary.

In applying its holding to the facts of the case, the Court found that the Board failed to discuss the section 5103A(d)(2) factors with any specificity and erroneously implied that a medical opinion did not need to be provided because the “record is absent for competent medical evidence that additional disability resulted as a consequence of negligence, careless[ness], or other fault on the part of VA, or an event not reasonably foreseeable.”  The Court held that this finding was legally untenable because under the “B” factor, the evidence must only indicate that there “may” be an association between a current disability and VA treatment.

The Court also found that the Board’s determination that the there was “nothing in the medical records from VA to suggest that the diagnosis of breast cancer was not made in a timely fashion” was in error as the Board is precluded from making unsubstantiated medical determinations and considering the absence of evidence as substantive negative evidence when deciding whether VA has complied with the duty to assist provisions.  The Court held that the Board not only misinterpreted factor “B” as requiring competent medical evidence of VA fault, but provided inadequate reasons or bases for determining that a medical opinion was not warranted as to whether VA failed to timely diagnose the veteran’s breast cancer.

The Court found that the undisputed factual findings, taken as a whole, unequivocally “indicate” that there “may be an association” between the veteran’s current mental disability and his VAMC treatment, which included an approximately 11-month gap between repeated requests for a breast cancer check-up and a diagnosis of recurrent breast cancer.  The Court, in concluding that the Board clearly erred in finding that VA substantially complied with its assistance requirements, directed that the Board provide the veteran with a medical opinion pursuant to section [5103A(d)(2)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) on remand.

As to the veteran’s second theory of injury under section 1151 – VA’s misdiagnosis of bipolar disorder caused or aggravated his mental disability – the Court also found that the Board provided inadequate reasons or bases as it failed to discuss whether the evidence of record indicated that there may be association between the veteran’s current disability and VA treatment.  However, it did not find that the evidence of record unequivocally showed an indication between the veteran’s current disability and VA treatment.  The Court directed the Board to provide a more detailed analysis of its application of section 5103A(d)(2) to the facts surrounding the veteran’s current mental disability and diagnosis on remand as to this theory.

The Court also found the record unclear as to what records VA may or may not have in its possession that are relevant to the claim.  The Court directed that, on remand, VA provide the veteran with an updated release of information (ROI), as the prior ROI appeared to be no longer viable, and attempt to obtain VAMC records pertaining to his mental disability and cancer treatment.

The Court vacated the Board’s decision and remanded the case for adjudication consistent with its opinion.
