Decision Assessment Document
Stewart v. Wilkie, Dec 20, 2018, 30 Vet.App. 383 (2018)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that under the first sentence of 38 C.F.R. 3.317(a)(2)(ii), a medically unexplained chronic multisymptom illness (MUCMI) is established if the diagnosed illness has either an inconclusive etiology or pathophysiology.  Demonstration of both an inconclusive etiology and pathophysiology is not required to establish a MUCMI.  An illness is a MUCMI where either the etiology or pathophysiology of the illness is inconclusive.

In addressing the second sentence of 3.317(a)(2)(ii), the Court held that a multisymptom illness is not a MUCMI if both the etiology and pathophysiology of the illness are partially understood.  If only the etiology or the pathophysiology of the multisymptom illness is partially understood, the condition is still recognized as a MUCMI.

The Court also held that the determination of whether an illness is medically unexplained must be based on an individual veteran’s unique circumstances that is particular to the claimant in each case.  Thus, even if a veteran’s multisymptom illness has a partially understood etiology or pathophysiology as it generally affects the public, it may still qualify as a MUCMI if the etiology or pathophysiology of the veteran’s individual multisymptom illness is inconclusive.

The Court noted that it has held in other cases that generic information in a medical journal or treatise that certain factors could cause a medical condition does not, as a general matter, establish nexus absent additional evidence that those factors did cause a veteran’s condition.  However, the Court indicated that if definitional or treatise evidence were sufficient to conclusively identify the cause of a claimed illness, i.e., with the requisite degree of certainty, the illness would not be medically unexplained.  Any lesser degree of certainty would require evaluation of the unique facts of the veteran’s situation to determine if his or her illness is medically unexplained.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court’s holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in the Manual under IV.ii.2.D.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran claimed that he was surrounded by burn pits and exposed to smoking from burning garbage and to sand and dust during service in Southwest Asia from February 2004 to January 2005.  He was separated from service in February 2005.  The veteran was treated for and diagnosed with asthma in December 2005.  Clinical testing conducted the following month showed chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

The veteran filed a claim for service connection for asthma in October 2008.  In August 2011, the veteran’s private nurse wrote that the veteran had a combined obstructive and restrictive pulmonary disease with no known etiology.  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) denied the veteran’s claim in December 2013.

The Court, in September 2014, granted a motion to remand the case with the parties agreeing that the Board failed to consider whether the veteran’s asthma or combined obstructive and pulmonary disease constituted a MUCMI.  The Board remanded the case for an examination.

A VA examination undertaken in February 2015 found that the veteran’s pulmonary function tests were consistent with asthma.  The examiner diagnosed asthma and stated that the veteran did not have multiple respiratory conditions.  The VA examiner, relying on medical literature, discussed the nature of asthma and explained that people with asthma have inflamed airways that react strongly to certain inhaled substances.  The examiner reported that asthma attacks may be triggered by exposure to substances including allergens such as dust, animal fur, mold, pollen, trees, air pollution, certain medicines, and chemicals.  The examiner opined that the veteran had no chronic respiratory illness caused by or as the result of service and less likely than not had a MUCMI.

The Board, in October 2015, concluding that asthma was not a MUCMI, denied the veteran’s claim.  The Board reasoned that because the etiology of asthma is partially understood, it could not be considered a MUCMI.

The veteran argued before the Court that a MUCMI is a diagnosed illness that lacks either a conclusive pathophysiology or a conclusive etiology.  He also argued that a chronic multisymptom illness is not considered a MUCMI when the illness has both a partially understood etiology and pathophysiology.  The veteran also contended that if the etiology of his individual asthma is unknown, it may still qualify as a MUCMI, even though asthma generally affects the public has a partially understood etiology or pathophysiology.

The Court, at the outset, found 38 U.S.C. 1117 to be clear and plain that a MUCMI is a medically unexplained chronic illness; however, the statute did not define what it means for an illness to be “medically unexplained.”  Instead, Congress delegated authority to the VA Secretary to prescribe regulations to carry out the statute.  VA implemented 3.317(a)(2)(ii) to carry out section 1117.

The Court, agreeing with the veteran, held that a multisymptom illness is a MUCMI if either the etiology or pathophysiology of the illness is inconclusive and would not be a MUCMI only if both the etiology and pathophysiology are partly understood.  The Court, rejecting the Secretary’s argument that both an inconclusive etiology and pathophysiology are required to establish a MUCMI, found the Secretary’s construction to be strained and clearly at odds with the plain reading of the regulation.

The Court also agreed with the veteran’s argument that the determination of whether an illness is medically unexplained is made on an individual basis that is particular to the claimant in each case.  The Court indicated that reliance on definitional or treatise evidence would only be sufficient to find an illness as medically unexplained if it conclusively identified the cause of the claimed illness to the requisite degree of certainty.  In the absence of such conclusive findings through medical literature evidence, an evaluation of the unique facts of the veteran’s situation to determine if the illness is medically unexplained is required.

The Court found that the 2015 VA examiner’s opinion that the veteran’s asthma was not a MUCMI to be inadequate.  The Court acknowledged that the VA examiner discussed in great detail the symptoms of asthma and identified the events or substances that may trigger asthma attacks; however, the Court found the examination to be inadequate because the examiner did not address the etiology or pathophysiology of asthma, either partial or conclusive.

The Court vacated the Board decision and remanded for the Board to obtain a medical opinion that addresses the etiology or pathophysiology of the veteran’s asthma.
