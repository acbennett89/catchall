Decision Assessment Document
 Stefl v. Nicholson, Mar 27, 2007, 21 Vet.App. 120
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court vacated and remanded an October 8, 2004 Board decision, finding VA’s medical examination inadequate and holding:

1. a medical nexus opinion finding a condition is not related to service because a condition is not entitled to presumptive service connection, without clearly considering direct service connection, is inadequate on its face; and

2. a medical examiner’s opinion that is not supported with an analysis that could be considered and weighed against contrary opinions is inadequate; without a medical opinion that clearly addresses the relevant facts and medical science, the Board is left to rely on its own opinion (citing *Colvin v. Derwinski,* 1 Vet.App. 171, 175 (1991) (holding that the Board may only consider independent medical evidence and may not substitute its own medical opinion)).

The Court provided examples of relevant points that can be discussed in a medical examiner’s analysis; however, the Court clearly stated that its opinion does not dictate how medical opinions should be formulated.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.  However, the Court’s holding should be applied when evaluating the adequacy of VA medical opinion evidence in applicable cases.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran’s military service from 1967 to 1969 included duty in Vietnam.  Service medical records contain no complaint of or treatment for a nasal or sinus condition.  In March 1998 he filed a claim for service connection for atypical squamous metaplasia (nasal sinus disease) based on exposure to herbicide agents, or tobacco use that began during service.  The RO’s denial was based upon the claimed condition’s absence from VA’s presumptive herbicide exposure list.  The RO later denied service connection for the nasal sinus disease as secondary to tobacco use as well.  The veteran submitted a statement from a private physician in January 2000 that espoused a relationship between the nasal sinus disease and herbicide exposure in Vietnam.

The Board remanded the case in 2001 for development to include a VA medical examination, which was conducted in March 2003.  The examiner reviewed the claims file to include a Statement of the Case listing diseases associated with herbicide exposure.  The examiner, noting that nasal sinus disease was not on the list and does not constitute an obvious malignancy of the respiratory tract, stated:  “It is therefore my opinion that the veteran’s nasal and sinus polyp disease is not related to service or exposure to Agent Orange.”  The Board upheld the RO denial in October 2004.

The veteran argued before the Court that the VA examination was inadequate because it was based on a misunderstanding of law, as the physician wrongly assumed that the nasal sinus disease could not be related to service since it was not included in the list of presumptive service connected diseases.

The Court agreed with the veteran that the examination was inadequate.  Referencing prior precedents, the Court stated that an opinion was adequate when based upon consideration of the veteran’s prior medical history and examinations and also described the disability, if any, in sufficient detail so that the evaluation of the claimed disability was a fully informed one. See Ardison v. Brown, 6 Vet.App. 405, 407 (1994) and Green v. Derwinski, 1 Vet.App. 121, 124 (1991).

The Court found that the VA medical examiner discussed presumptive service connection but did not, as directed by the 2001 Board remand, discuss whether exposure to herbicide agents, or smoking, directly caused the veteran’s condition.  Thus, when the examiner failed to consider direct service connection, or reported in a manner that was not clear enough to be understood, the report failed in its purpose.  Without a medical opinion that clearly addressed the relevant facts and medical science, the Board was left to rely on its own lay opinion, which is forbidden.

The Court held that a medical opinion that found a condition unrelated to service because it was not entitled to presumptive service connection must also consider direct service connection.  The Court provided a list of “relevant points” that can be discussed in the examination report such as why the examiner finds cited studies persuasive or unpersuasive, whether the veteran has other risk factors for developing the claimed condition, and whether the claimed condition has manifested itself in an unusual manner (the Court emphasized that it does not dictate how a medical opinion should be formulated, the list of examples given is not exclusive, and it does not hold that any of these items would be necessary to render a valid opinion).  The Court found no such analysis in the March 2003 VA examination report, which rendered it inadequate even if it addressed direct service connection.

The Board’s October 2004 decision was vacated and remanded by the Court for further action consistent with its decision.
