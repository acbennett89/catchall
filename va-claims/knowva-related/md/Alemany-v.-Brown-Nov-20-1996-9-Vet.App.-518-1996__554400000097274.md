Decision Assessment Document
Alemany v. Brown, Nov 20, 1996, 9 Vet.App. 518 (1996)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that to deny a claim on its merits, the evidence must preponderate against the claim, but to favorably determine entitlement to the benefit sought, the evidence must only be in equipoise.  The veteran is not required to prove his service connection claim by a preponderance of evidence.  Whenever evidence is in equipoise, the decision must be in the claimant’s favor.  Also, in light of the benefit of doubt provisions of 38 U.S.C. 5107(b), an accurate determination of etiology is not a condition precedent to granting service connection; nor is “definite etiology” or “obvious etiology.”

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court's holding has been incorporated into guidance contained in the Manual M21-1 under III.iv.5.A.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

While in service between June 1969 and March 1971, the veteran, who had acknowledged having had severe or frequent headaches on his preinduction physical, was seen with complaints of increasingly frequent headaches accompanied by visual blackouts.  His service separation examination was negative for any disability or disease.  In 1985, he was hospitalized after a seizure and was diagnosed with a convulsive disorder.  In June 1989, a claim for service connection for epilepsy was filed.  During a hearing, he stated that following service discharge he had several seizures with loss of consciousness until 1985.  A physician reviewed the service medical records and opined that the symptoms during service may have been the result of an "epileptic spark in the brain," and that the stress of the war may have played a roll in unleashing a process that had been dormant and latent.  The claim was denied, and on appeal, the Board of Veterans' Appeals (Board) remanded for a VA examination.  The examiner stated that there was not enough information to determine the definite etiology of the seizure disorder.  The Board subsequently affirmed the denial stating that it might be impossible to determine the etiology of the seizure disorder, but that was not necessary because it only had to determine if the evidence in favor of the claim outweighed the negative evidence.

The Court determined that a well-grounded claim had been presented because there was competent evidence of a current seizure disorder, episodes of headaches in service, and a "possible" link between the seizure disorder and the headaches.  The Court identified two errors in the Board decision.  First, the Board impermissably relied on its own unsubstantiated medical conclusion to rebut the medical evidence favorable to the claim.  The physician's statement provided a possible link between the onset of the disorder and service, and the opinion of the VA examiners did not exclude that possibility.  Second,  the Board erroneously required the appellant to prove the claim by a preponderance of the evidence.  To deny a claim on the merits, the evidence must preponderate against the claim, but to favorably determine entitlement to the benefit sought, the evidence must only be in equipoise.  Because the Board erred in placing an erroneous burden of proof on the appellant, the decision could not be affirmed.  Because the Board believed that "it may be impossible to accurately determine the etiology of the appellant's seizure disorder," the Court felt it was difficult to see any benefit to further protracting the seven-year course of adjudications by seeking further medical opinions.  Should the Board undertake further development, however, "it must do so with the utmost expedition."
