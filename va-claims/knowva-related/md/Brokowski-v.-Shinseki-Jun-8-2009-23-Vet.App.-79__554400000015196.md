Decision Assessment Document
 Brokowski v. Shinseki, Jun 8, 2009, 23 Vet.App. 79
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that a reference to “all disabilities of record” was insufficient to satisfy the requirement for informal claims under [38 C.F.R. § 3.155(a)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=cfbd91934eb2006a365b8d9dd2525420&node=se38.1.3_1155&rgn=div8) that the claim must “identify the benefit sought.” The Court explained that in identifying the benefit sought, the veteran must describe the nature of the disability for which he is seeking benefits, which may be satisfied by referring to the body part or system that is disabled or by describing symptoms of the disability.

The Court explained that it did not hold that the inclusion of the term "all disabilities of record" in an application for VA benefits coupled with the submission of particular medical records can be ignored in determining whether the claimant has sufficiently identified the benefit sought. For example, if that term is used and selected records are submitted to support the claim that clearly discuss disabilities or *specific* symptoms other than those listed on the application, it may be inferred that those records were submitted because the claimant intended to apply for benefits for those conditions or conditions that are suggested by the specific symptoms.

However, the Court cautioned that such language or the indiscriminate inclusion of materials with an application for benefits cannot be used to require VA to conduct an unguided safari through the record to identify all conditions for which the veteran may possibly be able to assert entitlement to a claim for disability compensation as such a requirement would nullify the specificity required by § 3.155(a).

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran submitted a claim for service connection for a psychiatric disorder in June 1978. In his statement, he also indicated: “This is also a claim for service connection for all disabilities of record.” No other documents were attached to the statement. The regional office (RO), in July 1978, denied service connection for anxiety and depression.

In February 1994, the veteran’s counsel sent a letter to the RO to clarify that the veteran was also seeking service connection for peripheral neuropathy (in addition to the previous claim for compensation for peripheral vascular disease first filed in 1989). Although the RO initially denied the claim in January 1995, service connection was ultimately granted for peripheral neuropathy of the lower extremities in November 2002 with an effective date of December 1994. The RO, in November 2004, awarded a total unemployability evaluation and amended the effective date for service connection for peripheral neuropathy to February 1994.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in January 2007, denied an earlier effective date of service connection for peripheral neuropathy. The Board noted that the veteran first submitted a claim for service connection for peripheral neuropathy on February 15, 1994, and the medical evidence submitted at that time was the first evidence of record of the disorder. The Board concluded that earlier claims and medical evidence did not reflect an intent to apply for service connection for peripheral neuropathy and did not establish the veteran as having peripheral neuropathy.

The veteran argued before the Court that because the medical record contained evidence of peripheral neuropathy neuropathy existing prior to February 15, 1994, he was entitled to an earlier effective date for the commencement of his disability compensation award based on peripheral neuropathy. He contended that he filed an informal claim for benefits based on this disability when he filed his 1978 application seeking service connection for depression, anxiety, and “all disabilities of record.” He also put forth that his October 1989 application for VA benefits for peripheral vascular disease included a claim for peripheral neuropathy because the evidence of record disclosed peripheral neuropathy.

The Court, in rejecting the veteran’s argument that by including the term “all disabilities of record” in his 1978 application made his intention clear he was seeking benefits for peripheral neuropathy, stated that an informal claim for VA benefits must identify the benefit. The Court criticized the argument as implying that the phrase indicates an intention to apply for every benefit that might be implied by some entry in the record then before VA. In this case, the term was insufficient to “identify the benefit sought” as required by [§ 3.155(a)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=cfbd91934eb2006a365b8d9dd2525420&node=se38.1.3_1155&rgn=div8). The Court held that the Board’s finding that the veteran did not file an informal claim for compensation for peripheral neuropathy in 1978 was supported by the record because the veteran did not identify peripheral neuropathy as a benefit for which he was seeking disability compensation.

The 1978 application contained no reference to peripheral neuropathy and provided no description of the symptoms he was experiencing. The Court noted that the medical records showed that when the veteran filed his 1978 claim, his treating physicians had concluded that the disability involving his lower extremities was attributed solely to a peripheral vascular disorder and lumbar back disorder.

The Court indicated that the requirement to “sympathetically read” a claim does not obligate the Board to conduct an exercise in prognostication, but only requires that it consider all claims reasonably raised by the evidence. The Court also held that a VA adjudicator is not required to anticipate a claim for benefits for disability that have not been identified by medical professionals or by competent lay evidence at that time that claimant files a claim or during the claims’ development.

The Court concluded that the February 1994 correspondence was the earliest document in the record that satisfied all three requirements – communication was written, indicated an intent to file for benefits, and identified the benefit sought (peripheral neuropathy) – for an informal claim for disability compensation for peripheral neuropathy.

The Court affirmed the Board’s January 2007 decision.
