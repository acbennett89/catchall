Decision Assessment Document
 Velez v. Shinseki, Oct 9, 2009, 23 Vet.App. 199
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that in determining whether new and material evidence is required to reopen a claim, the focus of the analysis must be on whether the evidence presented actually amounts to a new claim “based upon distinctly diagnosed diseases or injuries” or whether it is evidence tending to substantiate an element of a previously adjudicated matter.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran served from January 1968 to December 1969. In July 1970, he established service connection for a nose condition.  In July 1984, he claimed service connection for a back disorder and a stress disorder.  In support of his stress disorder claim, he reported that he had been nervous and depressed and experiencing nightmares and flashbacks since service.  He submitted a clinical report showing a diagnosis of adjustment disorder with depressed mood related to marital problems.  During a hearing before the regional office (RO), he indicated that he had a “nervous condition.”  The RO, in August 1986, denied service connection for post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and an adjustment disorder with depressed mood.  No appellate action was taken.

The RO, in denying a claim to reopen in April 1991, determined that service connection for PTSD was unwarranted as there was no evidence of a nervous disorder in service or of a present diagnosis of PTSD.  The veteran appealed.  Following a 1992 Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) remand, the veteran underwent two VA examinations, which concluded that he had “no gross psychiatric disorder.”  The Board again remanded the case, noting that the competent medical evidence of record revealed that the veteran did not have PTSD or any other current psychiatric disorder.  The RO, in April 2000, again declined to reopen the PTSD claim.  The same month, the veteran claimed service connection for cervical disc disease as secondary to his nose fracture.  The RO denied the claim in March 2001.

In July 2002, the veteran submitted a statement requesting service connection for a cervical spine condition as secondary to the accidents suffered on his face.  A VA primary care follow-up note from August 2002 indicated that the veteran’s chronic pain of the upper back and neck were related to an injury in service.

The RO, in December 2002, determined that new and material evidence had not been submitted with regard to his claims for service connection for a neck disorder or for a psychiatric condition.  The veteran appealed and underwent a VA examination in November 2005, which did not find evidence for PTSD, but yielded a diagnosis of depressive disorder NOS (not otherwise specified) and cannabis dependence.  The Board, in May 2007, determined that new and material evidence had not been submitted to reopen the previously and finally disallowed claims for service connection for an acquired psychiatric disorder, to include PTSD, and for a cervical spine disability.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred in requiring him to submit new and material evidence for his claim for a nervous disorder.  He asserted that this claim is a separate and distinct claim from his previously denied claim for PTSD and the Board should have adjudicated it on the merits.  He cited [*Ephraim v. Brown*, 82 F.3d 399 (Fed. Cir. 1996)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/082/82.F3d.399.94-7035.html) and [*Boggs v. Peake,* 520 F.3d 1330 (Fed. Cir. 2008)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014850/) to support his argument that there are no previous final denials of his claims for a nervous disorder and cervical spine disorder.

Note: The Federal Circuit held in Boggs that claims based upon separate and distinctly diagnosed conditions must be considered separate and distinct claims for purposes of VA benefits.  In Ephraim, the Federal Circuit held that a newly diagnosed disorder, whether or not medically related to a previously diagnosed disorder, cannot be the same claim when it has not been previously considered.

The Court noted that it had recently interpreted *Boggs* and *Ephraim* in [*Clemons v. Shinseki*, 23 Vet.App. 1 (2009)](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000015190/) in which it held that an initial claim for a particular mental condition submitted by a pro se claimant cannot be a claim limited only to that diagnosis, but must rather be considered a claim for any mental disability that may reasonably be encompassed by several factors including: the claimant’s description of the claim; the symptoms the claimant describes; and the information the claimant submits or that the Secretary obtains in support of the claim.

The Court, in holding that the Board must determine whether the evidence presented truly amounts to a new claim or tends to substantiate a previously adjudicated claim, stated that to reflexively conclude that the appearance of a new diagnosis is always evidence amounting to a new claim could have the unfortunate side effect of limiting the benefits awarded in some claims that would otherwise relate back to prior proceedings.

In the present case, the Court observed that the evidentiary record revealed that the veteran’s claimed psychiatric condition has been described by himself and construed by VA according to multiple manifestations involving overlapping symptomatology.  The veteran has constantly asserted since filing his claim in 1984 that he suffered from nervousness.

The Court found that the RO’s 1986 decision considered the veteran’s entire mental health picture in denying service connection for PTSD and an adjustment disorder and also had explicitly considered whether the veteran had any diagnosed psychiatric condition that could be related to service.

The Court distinguished the present case from *Ephraim* and *Boggs*, as the veteran’s claim for a stress disorder was denied on the basis that the evidence failed to establish a diagnosis of any psychiatric condition that could be related to service and it was clear that “any” psychiatric condition includes a nervous condition.  Therefore, the veteran’s submission of further evidence relating to a nervous condition did not introduce a new claim based on a distinctly diagnosed condition from the claim for a stress disorder previously denied by the RO.  The RO’s decision to deny service connection for a psychiatric condition in 1986 is final as the veteran did not appeal.  Thus, the Court held that the Board did not err in requiring him to submit new and material evidence to reopen his claim for a psychiatric disability.

Regarding the cervical spine disability claim, the Court determined that regardless of whether the RO in March 2001 adjudicated the cervical spine disorder claim on both direct and secondary bases, or whether the veteran even raised the issue of direct service connection to the RO, the veteran had failed to timely appeal the RO’s denial of service connection for a cervical spine disability and he is now foreclosed from having the matter adjudicated on a direct basis without either submitting new and material evidence or seeking revision on the grounds of clear and unmistakable error. However, the Secretary conceded that remand on this issue was required because the Board failed to adequately address the August 2002 VA treatment record that indicated a relationship between the cervical spine disability and military service.

Note: The Federal Circuit held in Bingham v. Nicholson, 421 F.3d 1346 (Fed. Cir. 2005) that separate theories in support of a claim for a particular benefit are not equivalent to separate claims and that a final denial on one theory is final denial on all theories.

The Court affirmed the Board’s decision determining that new and material evidence had not been submitted to reopen the claim for service connection for an acquired psychiatric disorder, to include PTSD, and vacated and remanded the cervical spine disability issue for further development and readjudication consistent with its opinion.
