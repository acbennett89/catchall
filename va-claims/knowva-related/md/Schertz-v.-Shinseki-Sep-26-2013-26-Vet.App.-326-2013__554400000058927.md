Decision Assessment Document
 Schertz v. Shinseki, Sep 26, 2013, 26 Vet.App. 326 (2013)
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that, in deciding entitlement to compensation for a disability under 38 U.S.C. 1151, the determination under 38 C.F.R. 3.361(d)(2) as to whether an event is not reasonably foreseeable is based on an objective standard of “what a reasonable health care provider would have foreseen.”  This means that the determination of the actual foreseeability of an event by a treating physician, an expert, or any other health care provider, is not dispositive.  The Court held that actual foreseeability or possible foreseeability is not the standard, as merely because a treating physician, expert, or other health care provider actually foresaw certain risks does not mean that a reasonable health care provider with primary care for the patient would have disclosed these risks.

The Court held that the correct standard is what a “reasonable health care provider” would have considered to be an ordinary risk of treatment that would be disclosed in connection with the informed consent procedures of 38 C.F.R. 17.32, which requires the primary health care provider to explain the reasonably foreseeable risks associated with the surgery or treatment being provided.

**Impact on VBA:**

Manual M2-1, IV.ii.2.G was revised to comply with the Court's holding.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, in October 2006, underwent aortic bifemoral bypass graft surgery at the Iowa City VA medical center (VAMC).  Prior to the procedure, he signed an informed consent form acknowledging that he understood the known risks which included “No guarantee of return of normal blood supply … Inadequate blood supply to legs and/or pelvic structures requiring removal or amputation.  No guarantee of return of normal function.  Nerve of spinal cord damage from poor blood supply, possibly permanent … death.”  The veteran’s wife testified before VA that she and her husband were verbally informed of the risk of paralysis.

Six days after the procedure, the veteran was able to walk without difficulty and was discharged from the VAMC.  Ten days later, on October 29, 2006, the veteran suddenly lost the ability to move his legs and was diagnosed with a spinal cord infarction and partial paralysis.

The veteran, in December 2006, filed a claim for compensation under section 1151 for spinal cord impairment and paralysis caused by the VA surgery.  The veteran provided a March 2008 letter from one of his treating physicians who stated that the loss of blood flow to the spinal cord, which resulted in cord damage and paraplegia, was a common sequelae, but not a wholly predictable outcome, of abdominal vascular disease or surgical correction.  She also stated that the spinal cord impairment was not a routinely anticipated consequence of the vascular disease process and advised that the veteran should be considered for service-connected benefits.

Following a Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) remand, a VA physician opined in a May 2010 report that paraplegia-paralysis after aortobifemoral graft is an infrequent complication of less than one percent and is not emphasized prior to the operative procedure.  The VA physician further commented, “The complication could have been foreseen; however, it would not normally be discussed in the preoperative discussion because of its low frequency.”

The Board, in May 2011, denied the claim for compensation under section 1151 for spinal cord impairment and leg paralysis.  The Board, while acknowledging that additional disability resulted from the VA surgery, found that VA physicians did not commit negligence or similar instance of fault and that the spinal cord impairment and paralysis were reasonably foreseeable complications of the surgery.  In support of its latter finding, the Board referenced physicians’ statements that loss of blood flow is a common sequelae of surgical correction for abdominal vascular disease and paralysis was an infrequent complication, but could have been foreseen, and that the veteran was informed that spinal cord injury and paralysis were known risks of the surgery.

The veteran argued before the Court that whether an event is reasonably foreseeable under § 3.361(d)(2) is an entirely objective inquiry that rests on what a “reasonable health care provider would have foreseen.”  He maintained that it is irrelevant whether his disability was actually foreseen by his care providers and also irrelevant that he had actual knowledge that his disability could result from surgery.  He also contended that the evidence discussed by the Board does not demonstrate that a “reasonable health care provider” would have foreseen the spinal cord impairment and paralysis resulting from the surgery or discussed the risk of complication while obtaining informed consent for the veteran’s procedure.

The Court, finding that the term “not reasonably foreseeable” under section 1151 may be interpreted in many ways and Congress had not given VA any guidance to assist in sifting through competing interpretations, held that VA’s interpretation of Section 1151’s “event not reasonably foreseeable” contained in § 3.361(d)(2) to be well within the scope of the language and a permissible construction of the statute.

The Court held that whether an event is not reasonably foreseeable is based on an objective standard of “what a reasonable health care provider would have foreseen” and the determination of the actual foreseeability of an event by a treating physician, an expert, or any other health care provider, is not dispositive.  The Court provided an example of an extremely cautious treating physician who may take the “kitchen sink approach,” informing a patient of numerous risks that still may be considered “not reasonably foreseeable” under the law if a reasonable health care provider primarily responsible for the patient would not have foreseen such risks or determined them subject to disclosure under § 17.32.

The Court clarified that what actually was foreseen by the treating physician is not wholly irrelevant of what a reasonable health care provider primarily responsible for the patient would have considered reasonably foreseeable and would have disclosed to the patient.  If VA determines, and provides explanation, that the treating physician primarily responsible for the patient acted in a manner consistent with how a reasonable health care provider confronted by the patient’s case would have acted, then the scope of the informed consent actually provided by the treating physician is evidence to be weighed under § 3.361(d)(2).

The Court found the Board’s reasons and bases to be inadequate, as it failed to render a determination that a reasonable health provider seeking to obtain informed consent would have disclosed the possibility of spinal cord impairment and paralysis as a reasonably foreseeable risk.  The Board never addressed whether the May 2008 letter from the veteran’s treating physician adequately conveyed whether a reasonable health care provider with primary responsibility for the veteran’s surgery would have foreseen the spinal cord injury and resulting paralysis.  The Board also failed to address the portion of the physician’s statement that paralysis “would not normally be discussed in the preoperative discussion.”

The Court set aside the Board decision and remanded the case for readjudication consistent with its decision.
