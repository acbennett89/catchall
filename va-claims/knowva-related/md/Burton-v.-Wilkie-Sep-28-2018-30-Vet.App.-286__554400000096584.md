Decision Assessment Document
Burton v. Wilkie, Sep 28, 2018, 30 Vet.App. 286
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the factual circumstances (topical therapy could be systemic if applied on a large enough scale) discussed in Johnson v. Shulkin, 862 F.3d 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2017) are not limited to situations involving large scale topical application.  Large scale application is merely an example of a factual circumstance that can convert topical treatment into systemic therapy.  To qualify as a systemic therapy, the treatment must affect the entire body in its treatment of the condition at issue.

The Court also held that under Warren v. McDonald, 28 Vet.App. 194 (2016), no certain order is required to determine if a treatment is a systemic therapy or like a corticosteroid or immunosuppressive drug.  Both elements must be present to justify a higher evaluation under DC 7806; however, the order in which they are addressed is of no importance.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Manual will be revised at III.iv.4.L.1.f to comply with the Court’s holding.  The rating criteria for skin disorders, to include the definition of systemic therapy and the preclusion of topical therapy as systemic therapy, was revised effective August 13, 2018.  The Court’s holding only applies to claims filed before or pending at the time of the August 13, 2018 revision.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran filed a claim for increase of the noncompensable evaluation for service-connected tinea pedis in February 2010.  A VA examination conducted the following month disclosed that the veteran treated the condition with a number of topical agents including Clotrimazole, which is an antifungal agent.  A VA examination undertaken in December 2011 indicated that the veteran used topical Clotrimazole constantly or on a near constant basis over the past 12 months and a topical corticosteroid, Clobetasol, was also used for treatment of his tinea pedis.  The examiner noted that the tinea pedis covered five to 20 percent of the veteran’s total body area.  The regional office, in February 2013, increased the evaluation for tinea pedis to 10 percent.  The veteran appealed.

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in February 2016, denied a higher evaluation because the tinea pedis was treated with topical creams and antihistamines, which are not systemic therapy, such as corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive drugs.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board failed to provide an adequate statement of reasons or bases concerning his Clobetasol treatment because it did not address the factual circumstances of that treatment to determine whether it may be considered systemic therapy, warranting a higher evaluation.  The veteran also argued that the Board failed to address whether the Benadryl is like or similar to a corticosteroid, as if it were, and its use was systemic therapy, he may have been entitled to a higher rating under DC 7806 in accordance with the Warren holding.

The Federal Circuit, in finding a distinction between topical systemic therapies in its 2017 decision in Johnson, clarified that topical therapy can be a systemic therapy based on the factual circumstances of each case.  The Federal Circuit had opined in Johnson that a topical therapy could be systemic if it was applied on a large enough scale.

The Court agreed with the parties that the Federal Circuit, in Johnson, did not mean to restrict the circumstances under which a topical therapy can be deemed a systemic therapy to the illustration it mentioned, as it was just an example.  The Court held that the “factual circumstances” discussed in Johnson by which a topical treatment may become a systemic therapy under DC 7806 are not limited to situations involving large scale topical application.  The Court explained that if the Federal Circuit meant that a topical treatment can be considered a systemic therapy based on the scale of application, such interpretation would make a redundant to a substantial degree, if not entirely, the diagnostic code’s reference to the percentage of body area affected by the skin condition.

The Court, in discussing Johnson’s definitions of systemic and therapy, concluded that to qualify as a systemic therapy it is not enough that the treatment standing alone affects the entire body, as it must affect the entire body in its treatment of the condition at issue.  The Court explained that the Board must decide how the topical treatment works, not by its contact with the affected location of the condition on the body, but instead in some other way that affects the body more broadly.

The Court rendered an example of topical treatment that may affect the body as a whole if it circulates through the bloodstream.  In affecting the body as a whole, it essentially would not matter where the topical treatment was applied, where the condition was located, or some other part of the body, as the body in its entirety would be involved in the treatment.  The Court noted that how a topical treatment works is a factual question that may, but not necessarily, require a medical opinion for its resolution, as the Board may make such a factual finding based on other evidence, such as medical dictionaries.

The Court concluded that the Board erred by failing to consider whether the veteran’s use of Clobetasol was systemic therapy, as its analysis stopped at the determination that the Clobetasol was applied topically and therefore was not systemic therapy.  Also, the Board did not discuss whether Clobetasol affected the body as a whole.

The Court rejected the relevance of side effects in determining whether a topical treatment is a systemic therapy, as this issue was raised by both parties at oral argument.  The Court stated that if a treatment for a skin condition does not affect the body as a whole, it cannot be considered systemic therapy, regardless of the side effects.

In addressing the veteran’s argument that the Board failed to determine if his use of Benadryl is like or similar to a corticosteroid, the Court indicated that, under Warren, the Board must determine if a given treatment is “like” a corticosteroid or other immunosuppressive drug in determining whether the treatment constituted a systemic therapy to warrant a higher rating.

The Court held that nothing in Warren requires a certain order in which the Board must determine if a treatment is a systemic therapy or like a corticosteroid or immunosuppressive drugs.  Both must be present, but the order in which they are addressed is of no import.  The Court found that the Board erred in failing to address whether Benadryl was “like” corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive drugs.

The Court rejected the Secretary’s argument that this omission was harmless error because the evidence showed that the Benadryl was used for less than six weeks, which would preclude a higher rating.  The Court indicated that the Board made a favorable finding that the veteran’s use of antihistamines was more than six weeks or “constant or near constant.”

The Court held that remand was required because the Board did not provide an adequate statement of reasons or bases on whether the use of Clobetasol is a systemic therapy.  Also, the Board did not address if the veteran’s Benadryl use is “like” corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive drugs and a systemic therapy used for more than 6 weeks.
