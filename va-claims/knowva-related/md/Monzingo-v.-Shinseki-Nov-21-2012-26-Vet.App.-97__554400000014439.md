Decision Assessment Document
 Monzingo v. Shinseki, Nov 21, 2012, 26 Vet.App. 97
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that there was too tenuous a connection between the veteran’s claim for hearing loss and the 2006 report *Noise and Military* *Service: Implications for Hearing Loss and Tinnitus (Noise and Military Service)* to reasonably expect that the report would be part of the record before the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) in every hearing loss or tinnitus claim, or that it was constructively part of the record.

The Court also held that there was too tenuous a connection between the hearing loss claim and the 1982 report *Tinnitus: Facts, Theories,* *and Treatments (Tinnitus)* to reasonably expect that it would be part of the record before the Board, absent the veteran’s submission of the document to VA or request that it be obtained.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual change.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served in the Army from October 1966 to October 1968, entered service with normal hearing in the left ear and a 45-decibel loss in the right ear.  Normal hearing in the left ear was shown at separation with a 35-decibel loss of the right ear.  The regional office (RO), in 1984, granted the veteran’s claim for service connection for tinnitus but denied service connection for hearing loss.

In June 2007, the veteran filed to reopen the hearing loss claim and underwent a VA audiology examination in February 2008.  The VA examiner diagnosed bilateral hearing loss, but opined that the left ear hearing loss was not caused by nor was the right ear hearing loss aggravated by in-service acoustic trauma.  The RO reopened but denied the claim.

The Board, in February 2010, affirmed the RO denial.  The Board noted normal hearing in the left ear during service and hearing loss having not been diagnosed until decades after service.  Regarding the right ear, the Board noted that the veteran left service with slightly improved hearing acuity in the right ear.  The Board found the February 2008 VA audiology examination highly probative in finding that the veteran’s bilateral hearing loss was not caused or aggravated by service.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board’s reasons or bases were inadequate by failing to address if his service-connected tinnitus constituted evidence of continuous symptomatology of hearing loss or explain relying upon an in-service examination report that showed improved hearing acuity during service in light of his assertion that hearing tests at that time were generally inaccurate.  In support of his argument, the veteran relied upon *Noise and Military Service* and *Tinnitus*, as constituting favorable evidence that was constructively in the Board’s possession that should have been addressed.  The veteran also argued that the February 2008 VA examination report was inadequate, as it lacked detail and rationale, and the examiner failed to comment on the findings of various hearing loss studies.

The Court discussed the constructive possession doctrine (also referred to as the constructive *notice* doctrine) beginning in *Bell v. Derwinski*, 2 Vet.App. 611, which held that, “where documents proffered by the appellant are within the Secretary’s control and could reasonably be expected to be part of the record before the Secretary and the Board, such documents are, in contemplation of the law, before the Secretary and the Board.”  *Bowey v. West*, 11 Vet.App. 106 (1998), the Court interpreted *Bell* as having two rules for determining whether a document was constructively before the Board:  (1) A “specific” rule that a document had to be clearly generated by VA or submitted to VA by the appellant, and (2) a “broader” rule that a document had to be within the Secretary's control and reasonably expected to be part of the record.  The Court further refined the constructive possession doctrine in *Goodwin v. West*, 11 Vet.App. 494 (1998), clarifying that even when a document is generated by VA, it will not be considered constructively before the Board in a particular claimant’s case unless the document has a direct relationship to the claimant’s appeal.

In applying the law to the case, the Court rejected the veteran’s argument that the constructive possession doctrine applied, holding that there was too tenuous a connection between the veteran’s service connection claim for hearing loss and the reports *Noise and Military Service* and *Tinnitus*.

The Court noted that *Noise and Military Service* was supported by a contract between the National Academy of Sciences (NAS) through Public Law 107-330, § 104(c) (Dec. 6, 2002).  However, the report’s findings were of the author and not necessarily reflective of the organizations or agencies that supported the project, the report was not specific to the veteran, and the only connection between the report and the veteran’s claim was the discussion of hearing loss as it related to military service.  The Court indicated that *Tinnitus* was not prepared or commissioned by VA, and bears no relationship to the veteran’s claim other than the general discussion on the relationship between tinnitus and hearing loss.  Note: Tinnitus was prepared by Working Group 89 of the Committee on Hearing Bioacoustics, and Biomechanics of the Commission on Behavioral and Social Sciences Education of the National Research Council.  A VA employee was a member of the working group.

The Court took judicial notice that (1) VA was ordered by Congress to contract for *Noise and Military Service*, (2) both *Noise and Military Service* and *Tinnitus* have been published, and (3) VA received a copy of *Noise and* *Military Service* from the NAS.  The Court did not take judicial notice of the reports’ findings and conclusions, as the Court concluded that they were not of universal notoriety or facts not subject to reasonable dispute.

The Court rejected the veteran’s reasons or bases arguments, as they were based on evidence that it had determined was outside the actual or constructive record before the Board.  The Court, noting that VA recognizes hearing loss and tinnitus as separate and distinct disabilities, found that the veteran failed to identify any competent evidence that tinnitus is evidence of his hearing loss.

The Court also rejected the veteran’s argument that the February 2008 VA audiology examination was inadequate, finding his argument to confuse the duties of a medical examiner with those of a VA adjudicator, as there is no requirement that a medical examiner comment on every favorable piece of evidence in a claims file.  The Court explained that an examination is adequate when it sufficiently informs the Board of a medical expert’s judgment on a medical question and the essential rationale for that opinion.  The Court agreed that the examiner did not comment on the findings of various medical studies regarding hearing loss; however, the general presumption of competence includes a presumption that physicians remain up-to-date on medical knowledge and current medical studies.

The Court affirmed the Board’s decision.
