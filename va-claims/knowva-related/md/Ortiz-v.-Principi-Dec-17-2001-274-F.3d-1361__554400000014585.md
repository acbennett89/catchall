Decision Assessment Document
 Ortiz v. Principi, Dec 17, 2001, 274 F.3d 1361
 U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

**What the case about:**

When the preponderance of the evidence is against a veteran’s claim, the benefit of the doubt rule (38 U.S.C. § 5107) should not be applied.  The Federal Circuit affirmed the decision of the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (CAVC).

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact.  Good training tool.

**Summary of the facts and the Court's reasons:**

VA and the CAVC found that the preponderance of the evidence was against the veteran’s claim for service connection.  For that reason, VA and the CAVC denied the claim without applying the benefit of the doubt rule.  On appeal to the Federal Circuit, the veteran argued that even if the preponderance of the evidence is against a claim, the evidence may nevertheless be in “approximate balance” (this key phrase is in the language of [section 5107](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5107)), thus triggering the application of the benefit of the doubt rule.

The Federal Circuit (Court) concluded that the benefit of the doubt rule is inapplicable when the preponderance of the evidence is found to be against the claimant.  The Court looked at the definition of “approximate” and found that it is generally understood to mean “almost exact . . . close to . . . nearly the same”.  The word “balance”, is defined as “a state of equilibrium or equipoise . . .  to be equal in value, weight”.  Thus, evidence is in “approximate balance” when the evidence in favor of and opposing the veteran’s claim is found to be almost exactly or nearly equal.  Applying these definitions, the benefit of the doubt rule applies only when the factfinder determines that the positive and negative evidence relating to the veteran’s claim are “nearly equal”, thus rendering any decision on the merits “too close to call”.  In this case, the claim is decided in the veteran’s favor.

On the other hand, a preponderance of the evidence is a state of proof that persuades the factfinders that the points in question are “more probably so than not”.  Thus, if VA is persuaded that the preponderant evidence weighs either for or against the veteran’s claim, it necessarily has determined that the evidence is not “nearly equal” or too “close to call”, and the benefit of the doubt rule has no application.
