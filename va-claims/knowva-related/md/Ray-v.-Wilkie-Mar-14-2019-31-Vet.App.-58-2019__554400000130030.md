Decision Assessment Document
Ray v. Wilkie, Mar 14, 2019, 31 Vet.App. 58 (2019)
U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the finding that an extra-schedular referral is warranted under 38 C.F.R. 4.16(b) is based on an evidentiary threshold that is lower than that for the decision to award an extra-schedular rating.  The Court held that the initial extra-schedular referral under 4.16(b) addresses whether there is sufficient evidence to substantiate a reasonable possibility that a veteran is unemployable by reasons of his or her service-connected disabilities.

The Court held that if the Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board) refers a claim to the Director, Compensation Service, for extra-schedular consideration and the Director denies an extra-schedular rating, the Board’s earlier decision to refer the claim does not automatically bind the Board to grant an extra-schedular rating.  However, if the Board denies an extra-schedular rating, it must provide adequate reasons or bases for its reasoning when the factual finding at the referral stage comes out differently at the review stage.

The Court held that the phrase “unable to secure and follow a substantially gainful occupation” under 4.16(b) has two components:   one economic and one noneconomic.  The economic component is defined as an occupation earning more than marginal income (outside of a protected environment) as determined by the U.S. Department of Commerce for one person.

The Court, in addressing the noneconomic component – ability to secure and follow that type of employment – held that attention must be given to:

* The veteran’s history education, skill and training,
* Whether the veteran has the physical ability (both exertional and nonexertional) to perform the type of activities (e.g., sedentary, light, medium, heavy, or very heavy) required to the occupation at issue.  Factors that may be relevant include, but are not limited to, the veteran’s limitations, if any, concerning lifting, bending, sitting, standing, walking, climbing, grasping, typing, and reaching, as well as auditory and visual limitations, and
* Whether the veteran has the mental ability to perform the activities required by the occupation at issue.  Factors that may be relevant include, but are not limited to, the veteran’s limitations, if any, concerning memory, concentration, ability to adapt to change, handle work place stress, get along with coworkers, and demonstrate ability and productivity.

The Court advised that it was not creating a checklist that must be run completely through in every case.  Discussion of any factor is only necessary if raised by the evidence.

**Impact on VBA:**

The Court's holding will be incorporated into guidance contained in the Manual under IV.ii.2.F.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who is service connected for several disabilities with a combined evaluation of 50 percent, submitted a clam for a total disability evaluation due to individual unemployability (TDIU), which was denied in March 2012.

The Board, in July 2014, remanded the TDIU issue for referral under 4.16(b) to Compensation Service for extra-scheduler TDIU consideration.  The Board found that the record showed that the veteran may be prevented from physical labor due to limitations from his service-connected ventral hernia.  The Board also noted that while a VA examiner from October 2011 indicated that the veteran was able to perform sedentary employment, the record suggested that the veteran may not be able to obtain such employment as it is inconsistent with his education and occupational background.

The Director denied an extra-schedular TDIU evaluation in March 2015.  The Board, in February 2017, denied an extra-schedular TDIU evaluation, finding that the veteran’s service-connected disabilities did not render him unable to secure and follow substantially gainful employment consistent with his education and special training.

The veteran argued that the Board’s 2014 referral decision was a binding factual finding that the Board impermissibly changed when it denied him an extra-schedular rating in 2017.

The Court concluded that the decision to refer a claim for extra-schedular consideration is a factual finding that does not bind the Board when it later decides whether to award an extra-schedular rating.  The Court explained that this is so because the decisions to refer and to award a rating are fundamentally different.  Although the referral decision could affect the later award decision in a case-specific way, there is no categorical relationship between these two distinct determinations.

The Court held that the initial extra-schedular referral decision under 4.16(b) addresses whether there is sufficient evidence to substantiate a reasonable possibility that a veteran is unemployable by reason of his or her service-connected disabilities.  The Director then performs his or her duties and, if the Director denies an extra-schedular rating, the Board then, reviewing that decision without deference and bearing in mind the benefit of the doubt standard, decides whether the preponderance of the evidence nevertheless shows that a veteran is unemployable by reason of his or her service-connected disabilities.

The Court further held that the Board, when reviewing the Director’s decision not to grant an extra-schedular TDIU rating, must ensure that it adequately explains its reasoning when a factual finding made at the referral stage comes out differently at the review stage.  The Court indicated that some factors that may affect the Board’s ability to reach a different result or the adequacy of its discussion of reasons or bases include, but are not limited to, the certainty or complexity of factfinding in the initial referral decision and to the extent to which the record has changed since the referral decision.  Therefore, the referral decision can still be relevant at the award stage in certain circumstances.

The Court concluded remand was warranted since the Board’s decision did not explain why the factual finding it made at the referral stage came out differently at the review stage.  The Court directed that the Board, on remand, must ensure that it explains its different factual determinations at the referral and review stages.

The Court, holding 4.16(b) to be ambiguous concerning the phrase “unable to secure and follow a substantially gainful occupation, found that the regulations failed to define either a “substantially gainful occupation” or what it means to be “unable to secure and follow” such an occupation.  Although 38 C.F.R. 4.16(a) provides that “substantially gainful employment” is employment that is marginal and provided a definition of marginal employment, the Court found this to be, at best, an incomplete definition.

The Court, after reviewing its TDIU case, relevant Social Security regulations, and the parties’ arguments, interpreted the phrase “unable to secure and follow a substantially gainful occupation” under 4.16(b) to have two components:  one economic and one noneconomic.  The Court defined the economic component as simply meaning that an occupation earning more than marginal income (outside of a protected environment) as determined by the U.S. Department of Commerce as the poverty threshold for one person.  Noneconomic factors identified by the Court consisted of the veteran’s history, education, skill, and training; and whether the veteran has the physical and mental ability to perform the activities required by the occupation at issue.

The Court stated that by discussing the aforementioned potentially relevant factors, it was not creating a checklist that must be run completely through in every case.  Instead, discussion of any factor is only necessary if raised by the evidence.

The Court set aside the Board’s February 2017 decision and remanded for further adjudication.
