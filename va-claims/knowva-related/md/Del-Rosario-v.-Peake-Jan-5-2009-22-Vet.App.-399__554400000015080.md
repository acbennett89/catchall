Decision Assessment Document
 Del Rosario v. Peake, Jan 5, 2009, 22 Vet.App. 399
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that in cases of forfeiture for benefits under [38 U.S.C. § 6103(a)](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/6103), written notice complying with [38 C.F.R. § 3.905(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=9eedc5e289e1354a231370545e00a2e7&node=se38.1.3_1905&rgn=div8) provides adequate notice to a claimant concerning the rights to submit evidence and to a hearing, and the consequences for failing to do so.  Also, VA does not have to advise a claimant that live testimony will necessarily be more favorable to him.

Although acknowledging that a forfeiture action is an adversarial process initiated by VA that must be declared beyond a reasonable doubt, the Court, in allowing the consideration of hearsay evidence, held that adversarial rules of evidence do not apply to a forfeiture proceeding.

**Impact on VBA:**

No new impact warranting regulatory revision or Manual revision.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who was in receipt of a 90 percent VA disability rating, rendered honorable service from 1954 to 1974.  In September 1997, the regional office (RO) requested that a field examination be undertaken to investigate if the veteran was involved in “claims fixing.”  The field examination obtained affidavits that indicated that the veteran had directed another person to provide fraudulent statements to the effect that this person had been detained by the Japanese at Camp O’ Donnell with the deceased husbands of surviving spouses during World War II in support of the spouses’ claims for DIC benefits.  The affidavits also exhibited that the veteran had received payments from the widow claimants.

In a June 2003 letter, the RO notified the veteran of its proposal to initiate forfeiture action.  The letter notified the veteran of the bases of the charge, pertinent statutory provisions governing forfeiture for fraud, as well as the right to a hearing within 60 days from the letter’s date.  The letter explained the purpose of the hearing, a written statement may be provided if a hearing is not desired, and that written evidence will be given equal weight to that presented in a hearing.

The veteran provided a written response in August 2003.  The RO, in August 2004, issued a decision concluding that the record was sufficient to warrant submission of the veteran for consideration of forfeiture for fraud.  Compensation and Pension Service later determined that the veteran had forfeited VA benefits because he had ‘knowingly and intentionally furnished false and fraudulent evidence in the prosecution of surviving spouses’ claims for VA benefits.”  The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in May 2006, determined that the forfeiture was proper.

The veteran argued before the Court that the his due process rights were violated because the RO’s June 2003 notice letter was terse and failed to inform him the importance of presenting live testimony and the Board relied upon untrustworthy and unreliable hearsay statements from individuals who were implicated as well in the charged fraud.

The Court, noting that it is well established that the essential requirements of due process are notice and opportunity to respond, found that the RO had satisfied these requirements with the June 2003 letter, as it had adequately notified the veteran of both his right to submit evidence and right to a hearing, and the consequences of failing to do so.  The Court indicated that there was no support for the proposition that live testimony is always in an appellant’s interests, as some people present themselves better in writing than in person.  The Court was not persuaded by the veteran’s argument that he could not waive his right to present live testimony without the assistance of counsel given the veteran’s history of assisting other veterans with claims since at least 1998.  The Court also rejected the veteran’s contention that he never affirmatively waived his right to a hearing, as his failure to request a hearing in light of the June 2003 letter constituted such a waiver.

The Court concluded that the language of the June 2003 notice and specific procedures outlined in [§ 3.905(b)](http://www.ecfr.gov/cgi-bin/text-idx?SID=9eedc5e289e1354a231370545e00a2e7&node=se38.1.3_1905&rgn=div8) adequately protected the veteran’s procedural due process rights.

The Court, observing that Federal Rules of Evidence generally do not apply before it, the Board, or the RO, concluded that the Board did not err in relying upon statements, which the veteran argued should be dismissed on the basis of hearsay, in upholding the forfeiture of benefits.  The Court also found that the VA provided the veteran adequate assistance and found no support for the veteran’s alternative argument that the VA’s duty to assist under [38 U.S.C. § 5103A](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/5103A) required the RO, “sua sponte,” to order a hearing.

The Court affirmed the Board’s May 2006 decision.
