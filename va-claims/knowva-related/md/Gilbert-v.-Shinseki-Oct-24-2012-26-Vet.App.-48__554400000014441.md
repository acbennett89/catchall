Decision Assessment Document
 Gilbert v. Shinseki, Oct 24, 2012, 26 Vet.App. 48
 U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Court)

**What the case is about:**

The Court held that the presumption of soundness applies only when a disease or injury not noted upon entering service manifests in service, and a question arises as to whether it preexisted service.  To rebut the presumption of soundness, VA is required to prove by clear and unmistakable evidence that a disease or injury manifesting in service both preexisted service and was not aggravated by service.  If VA rebuts the presumption, in-service incurrence of a disease or injury has not been established.

The Court held that the VA’s inability to rebut the presumption of soundness does not result in a presumption of aggravation, as the in-service disease or injury is deemed to have been incurred in service.  If the in-service injury or disease is deemed service-incurred under the presumption of soundness, disability compensation is only warranted for a current disability if the evidence is in equipoise that the current disability is related to the in-service disease or injury.

**Impact on VBA:**

Compensation Service is continuing to review this decision to determine if Manual revisions will be required.  Additional guidance may be forthcoming.  The case is presently on appeal to the Federal Circuit.

**Summary of the facts and Court’s reasons:**

The veteran, who served from November 1987 to November 1991, noted past substance abuse upon enlistment, but reported no history of psychiatric problems.  The enlistment examination found no psychiatric abnormalities.  The service medical records show the veteran being treated twice in service for substance abuse.  Upon separation, the veteran reported no psychiatric problems and was again found to be psychiatrically normal.

In 1993, the veteran sought mental health treatment, reporting that he had been intermittently suicidal since he was a young child and had episodic depression throughout his life.  He did not mention service and attributed his depression to relationship and financial problems.  Diagnoses of depression, alcohol dependence, history of substance abuse, and personality disorder were given.  Medical reports from 2001 through 2005 show continued treatment for psychiatric conditions.  Financial and relationship problems were noted in the records; however, the veteran also reported being present when an airliner was shot down “loaded with dead bodies” and when “an oil tanker … hit a landmine and burned.”  The veteran also reported having first noticed depression when discharged from the Navy and “need[ing] to get [his] thoughts sorted out from what happened to [him] in the military.”

The veteran, in June 2005, filed for disability compensation benefits for chronic depression and underwent a number of VA examinations.  An examiner from December 2007 diagnosed multiple psychiatric disorders, but found that they were unrelated to service and further commented that the veteran’s substance abuse, personality disorder, and depression preexisted service.  A VA examiner in 2009 rendered the same diagnoses and found that they were unrelated to and had preexisted service.  A June 2010 VA examiner found that none of the veteran’s psychiatric disabilities worsened beyond natural progression due to military service.  However, an examiner in February 2011 found the veteran’s depression and anxiety to be “tightly woven to his military experience.”

The Board of Veterans’ Appeals (Board), in June 2011, stated that the veteran was entitled to the statutory presumption that he was in sound condition upon entering service as no psychiatric disability was noted upon entry, unless the presumption is rebutted. Note: [38 U.S.C. § 1111](http://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/1111) provides that veterans shall be presumed to have entered service in sound condition, except as to disabilities noted at that time, unless “clear and unmistakable evidence demonstrates that the injury or disease (1) existed before acceptance and enrollment and (2) was not aggravated by such service.”

The Board found the first presumption prong rebutted, noting that the veteran had reported depression prior to service.  However, citing the VA examinations, the Board found the second prong of the presumption to be unrebutted, as it did not find clear and unmistakable evidence that the psychiatric disabilities were not aggravated during service.  Thus, the Board found the presumption to remain intact.  However, in denying the appeal, the Board found by a preponderance of evidence that the veteran’s psychiatric disabilities were unrelated to service.

The veteran argued before the Court that the Board erred by finding that the presumption of soundness remained intact, but subsequently finding his current disabilities were unrelated to service.  He alleged that the Board circumvented the clear and unmistakable evidence standard of the presumption of soundness by finding no nexus under the preponderance of the evidence standard.  He claimed that once the Board determined that clear and unmistakable evidence did not exist to rebut the presumption of soundness, he was entitled to have his claim considered and adjudicated based on the presumption that his preexisting psychiatric condition was aggravated by service.

The Court observed that the law surrounding the presumption of soundness and its application can be confusing, as evidenced by the veteran’s and Board’s general misunderstanding of the law and its application.

The Court held that before the presumption of soundness may be applied, there must be evidence that a disease or injury not noted upon entry in service had manifested or was incurred in service.  Once it is applied, if the VA is unable to rebut the presumption, the disease or injury manifested in service is deemed to have incurred in service, which establishes the second element of service connection.  If the presumption of soundness is not rebutted, resulting in an injury or disease being service-incurred, applying the presumption of aggravation following application of the presumption of soundness is unwarranted.

The Court held that even if an injury or disease is deemed incurred in service by applying the presumption of soundness, a veteran must still establish a current disability related to the in-service injury or disease.  The Court found that the Board did not circumvent the “clear and unmistakable” standard for its nexus determination, as different legal standards are employed for finding a nexus and for rebutting the presumption of soundness.

In applying the law to the case, the Court observed that notably absent from the Board’s reasons or bases were any discussion or finding as to whether the veteran suffered from depression in service, as absent a finding that the pre-service disease or injury manifested in service or was incurred in service, the presumption of soundness is inapplicable.

The Court rejected the veteran’s argument that the Board’s application of the presumption of soundness without a discussion of in-service manifestation might be construed as an implicit finding of in-service manifestation of a psychiatric disability, as the Board’s findings indicated no psychiatric symptoms in service and the veteran to not be credible as to his recollections of his medical history.  Rather, the Court found the Board to have misunderstood the law or, at least, failed to explain adequately its application of the presumption of soundness.

The Court found the veteran to have not been prejudiced by the error.  The Court explained that regardless of whether the Board erred in applying the presumption of soundness, a veteran must still demonstrate a nexus between the current disability and service.  The Board had determined that the veteran had not and the Court did not find such determination to be erroneous.

The Court affirmed the Board’s decision.
